    Medication · Simple carbohydrate

    Oral glucose (Glucose gel, dextrose gel)

    Puts fuel back into a brain that has run out and cannot make its own.

    EMR  Within EMR scope in Alberta, alongside glucometry. Requires an intact gag reflex and the ability to swallow safely.

    Breaks the chain at

    The brain runs out of fuel

    Mechanism

    There is no receptor and no clever pharmacology here. Glucose is absorbed across the mucous membranes of the mouth and then from the gut, raises blood glucose, and crosses into the brain by facilitated transport. Neurons resume normal metabolism. What makes it work as an emergency drug is that the brain is uniquely dependent: unlike muscle it cannot burn fat, and unlike liver it stores essentially no glycogen, so it fails within minutes of the supply stopping and recovers within minutes of it resuming.

    Why it works at that link and not another

    It is the rare intervention that treats the actual cause rather than a downstream consequence, and it reverses the whole cascade completely. A patient with focal weakness, slurred speech, and confusion who looks exactly like a stroke can be fully normal within fifteen minutes. That is precisely why blood glucose is checked on every patient with altered mental status, every time, without exception.

    Dosing

    RouteDoseNotes
    Oral gel15 to 25 gPlaced between the cheek and gum, or swallowed if the patient can. Do not squirt it into the back of the throat of a drowsy patient.
    Oral alternatives15 to 20 g of fast carbohydrateRoughly 150 mL of juice or regular soft drink, or three to four teaspoons of sugar in water. Less predictable absorption than gel.
    Follow-upRecheck in 15 minutesRepeat if still low. Once corrected, give a complex carbohydrate and protein to prevent a rebound fall.

    Target: Blood glucose above roughly 4.0 mmol/L with resolution of symptoms. Recheck rather than assuming.

    Timing

    This is the part that tells you when to reassess, and it is the part most people never learn properly.

    Onset
    10 to 20 minutes.
    Peak
    Around 30 minutes.
    Duration
    Short, roughly 1 to 2 hours, which is why follow-up food matters.

    What working looks like

    • Level of consciousness improving, usually first
    • Confusion, aggression, and slurred speech resolving
    • Sweating and tremor settling
    • Focal deficits, if hypoglycemia caused them, disappearing entirely
    • A patient who has no memory of the episode

    What it means if nothing happens

    If the glucose has been corrected and the patient has not improved within about fifteen minutes, hypoglycemia was not the whole story. Consider prolonged hypoglycemia that has already caused injury, a stroke, head injury, intoxication, post-ictal state, or sepsis. Recheck the glucose to confirm it actually rose.

    Cautions

    Reduced consciousness or absent gag reflex

    The absolute contraindication. Putting gel into the mouth of a patient who cannot protect their airway causes aspiration. If they cannot safely swallow, they need intravenous glucose or glucagon, which is beyond EMR scope, so escalate.

    Sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycemia

    Oral hypoglycemic drugs such as gliclazide and glyburide last far longer than the glucose you give. These patients correct, then fall again hours later, and they all need hospital assessment regardless of how well they look.

    Alcohol-associated hypoglycemia

    Alcohol suppresses the liver's ability to manufacture new glucose, so the patient cannot maintain their own level once yours runs out. They also recur.

    The well-looking patient who wants to stay home

    Reasonable for a straightforward insulin-related episode with a clear cause and someone at home. Not reasonable for a first episode, a sulfonylurea, alcohol, no obvious cause, or anyone living alone.

    Side effects are the drug doing its job elsewhere

    Glucose is about as close to a side-effect-free drug as exists, because you are replacing something the body was already short of. What problems there are come from the delivery rather than the substance: aspiration if given to someone who cannot swallow, nausea from a large sugar load, and a rebound fall an hour or two later when a fast sugar is given without any complex carbohydrate behind it. In a diabetic patient, overcorrection produces hyperglycemia, which is unpleasant but not remotely as dangerous as the hypoglycemia you just treated.
