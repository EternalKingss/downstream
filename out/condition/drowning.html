    Drowning

    Respiratory impairment from submersion, where the killer is hypoxia rather than the volume of water.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    The airway goes under
    Panic, breath holding

    The patient holds their breath, often while struggling, which consumes oxygen rapidly. Contributing factors are frequently the real story: alcohol, a seizure, a head or spinal injury from diving, hypothermia, or an underlying cardiac event.

    Handle spinal injury by mechanism, not reflex EMR

    Routine spinal immobilisation in all drownings delays airway management and is not supported. Restrict motion where there is a genuine mechanism such as diving, a fall, or a watercraft impact.

    The larynx spasms shut
    Protective, and then harmful

    Water contacting the larynx triggers reflex closure, which initially protects the lungs. It also means the patient cannot breathe even when their face clears the water. Most drowning victims aspirate remarkably little; the classic distinction between salt and fresh water drowning has essentially no bearing on prehospital management.

    Oxygen falls fast
    Before any water enters

    Breath holding plus exertion depletes oxygen within a minute or two. Consciousness is lost, and this is the point at which most of the neurological damage begins, well before any lung injury.

    shared node → Tissue oxygen falls
    Water enters the lungs
    As the spasm relaxes

    As consciousness is lost the laryngospasm relaxes and water enters. Water washes out surfactant, so alveoli collapse and stay collapsed, and it damages the alveolar membrane directly. The result is widespread collapse and a chemical inflammatory injury.

    anatomy → Alveoli

    Do not attempt to drain water EMR

    There is very little water in the lungs and any attempt to drain it wastes time and causes vomiting and aspiration. Abdominal thrusts have no role in drowning.

    Blood passes collapsed alveoli
    Severe shunt

    Collapsed and flooded alveoli are still perfused, so a large fraction of cardiac output passes through the lung without being oxygenated. This is why drowning patients can remain profoundly hypoxic despite high-flow oxygen and why positive pressure helps so much.

    shared node → Ventilation and perfusion stop matching

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration immediately. Expect an incomplete response because of the shunt, and be ready to assist ventilation rather than simply increasing the flow.

    Hypoxic bradycardia, then arrest
    Respiratory arrest first

    This is a hypoxic arrest. Breathing stops before the heart does, and the heart slows progressively into bradycardia and then asystole or PEA. That sequence is precisely why drowning resuscitation begins with rescue breaths rather than compressions alone.

    shared node → Breathing stops

    Rescue breaths first EMR

    This is the key deviation from standard adult arrest management. Drowning is a hypoxic arrest, so give five initial rescue breaths before compressions, and use a full thirty-to-two ratio with proper ventilation. Compression-only CPR is not appropriate here.

    Aggressive warming and careful handling EMR

    Wet patients lose heat extremely fast, and in cold water hypothermia may be profound. Remove wet clothing, dry, and insulate. Handle gently, because a very cold heart is irritable. Hypothermia is also protective, which is why prolonged resuscitation in cold-water drowning is justified.

    The lungs worsen over hours
    Delayed deterioration

    Surfactant loss and chemical injury produce progressive inflammation and pulmonary edema over the following hours. A patient who was pulled out and seemed fine can deteriorate significantly later, which is why anyone who aspirated needs observation.

    shared node → The diffusion barrier thickens

    Transport everyone who aspirated EMR

    Delayed deterioration over hours is real. Anyone who required rescue, coughed persistently, or has any respiratory symptom needs observation even if they now feel well.

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    The airway is submerged, the larynx spasms, and eventually water or the failure to breathe produces profound hypoxia. Very little water actually enters the lungs in most cases. What enters is enough to destroy surfactant and flood gas exchange, and the brain has already been starved by then.

    Stop and escalate

    • Any period of unconsciousness in the water
    • Persistent cough, breathlessness, or any abnormal saturation after rescue
    • Vomiting, which is very common and threatens the airway
    • Hypothermia
    • Suspected diving mechanism with neurological signs
    • A patient who feels fine but aspirated

    Telling it apart

    Not Cardiac arrest that caused the submersion?

    An adult who collapsed suddenly in shallow water or while swimming strongly may have had a primary cardiac event. The presence of a shockable rhythm points that way and shifts management toward standard arrest care.

    Not Hypothermic cardiac arrest?

    In cold water the two are intertwined and hypothermia is protective. Continue resuscitation and rewarming; the usual rules about duration do not apply, and people have survived remarkably long submersions in cold water.

    Not Seizure or intoxication as the cause?

    Always ask why they went under. The drowning may be the consequence rather than the event, and the underlying cause still needs treating.
