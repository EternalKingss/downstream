    Asthma

    Reversible narrowing of the small airways, driven by muscle spasm, swelling, and mucus.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Trigger exposure
    Allergen, cold air, exertion, infection

    An asthmatic airway is chronically inflamed and hyperresponsive even between attacks. A trigger that would do nothing to a normal airway provokes a disproportionate response here. Common triggers are allergens, respiratory infections, cold dry air, exercise, smoke, and emotional stress.

    Inflammatory response fires
    Mast cells degranulate

    Mast cells in the airway wall release histamine, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins. These mediators act on smooth muscle, blood vessels, and mucus glands simultaneously, which is why the next step happens in three ways at once rather than one.

    Airway narrows three ways
    Spasm, swelling, mucus

    Smooth muscle constricts around the bronchiole. The mucosa lining it swells. Goblet cells pour out thick mucus that plugs the lumen. All three reduce the radius, and airway resistance rises with the fourth power of the radius, so modest narrowing produces enormous resistance. This step is the single most important thing to understand about asthma, because bronchodilators reverse only the first of the three.

    Salbutamol EMR

    Relaxes bronchial smooth muscle. Reverses the spasm component only, which is why a severe attack can look barely improved after a dose.

    Epinephrine ACP

    Hits further upstream than salbutamol: beta-2 relaxes muscle, alpha-1 vasoconstriction reduces mucosal swelling, and mast cell stabilisation slows further mediator release. Reserved for severe or near-arrest presentations, and outside EMR scope.

    Air gets trapped on exhalation
    Wheeze, prolonged expiratory phase

    Bronchioles have no cartilage and are held open partly by the outward pull of surrounding inflated lung. On inspiration the lung expands and airways widen slightly; on expiration they narrow. So an already narrowed airway closes off first during exhalation. Air enters more easily than it leaves, the chest progressively hyperinflates, and the diaphragm is pushed into a flattened, mechanically inefficient position.

    Work of breathing rises
    Accessory muscles, tripod position

    The patient now has to generate far more pressure for every breath, against high resistance, using a diaphragm that hyperinflation has already put at a disadvantage. Accessory muscles are recruited. Speech shortens from sentences to phrases to single words, which is one of the most reliable field measures of severity you have.

    Position of comfort, upright EMR

    Sitting upright and leaning forward fixes the shoulder girdle so accessory muscles can pull against a stable base. Never force an asthmatic to lie flat.

    Calm the patient EMR

    Anxiety raises respiratory rate and oxygen demand, worsening air trapping. This is not hand-holding, it is a physiological intervention with a measurable effect.

    Tissue oxygen falls
    Anxiety, restlessness, falling SpO2

    Poorly ventilated alveoli behind obstructed airways still receive blood flow, so blood passes through without picking up oxygen. Meanwhile the respiratory muscles are consuming a rapidly increasing share of the oxygen that does get in.

    shared node → Tissue oxygen falls

    Oxygen EMR

    Raises the alveolar oxygen gradient. Buys time downstream but does nothing to the obstruction itself.

    Carbon dioxide starts to rise
    Late sign, ominous

    Early in an attack CO2 is usually low, because the patient is breathing fast and CO2 diffuses readily. A normal CO2 in a severe asthmatic is therefore not reassuring, it is a warning that ventilation is beginning to fail. A rising CO2 means the patient can no longer move enough air, and is close to exhaustion.

    shared node → Carbon dioxide accumulates
    Respiratory muscles fail
    Silent chest, falling effort

    The muscles have been working many times harder than normal, for a long time, while hypoxic. They fail. The wheeze disappears, not because the airways opened but because there is no longer enough airflow to make a sound. This is the most dangerous misreading in respiratory medicine.

    shared node → Respiratory muscles fail

    Assisted ventilation EMR

    Slow rate, long expiratory time, low volume. The instinct to bag fast is wrong here; you will stack breaths on top of already trapped air and can drop the blood pressure or cause a pneumothorax.

    Breathing stops
    Then hypoxic cardiac arrest

    Effort ceases. A pulse is usually present for a few minutes. Ventilation at this point is often entirely effective, but it is difficult, because the chest is hyperinflated and air still cannot escape easily.

    shared node → Breathing stops

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    An overreactive airway constricts, swells, and fills with mucus. Air gets in more easily than it gets out, so the chest progressively overinflates while the patient works harder and harder to empty it.

    Stop and escalate

    • Silent chest, or a wheeze that fades without treatment
    • Speaking in single words, or unable to speak at all
    • Falling respiratory rate or reduced effort in a patient who was working hard
    • Drowsiness, confusion, or agitation
    • Exhaustion, or a patient who says they cannot keep going

    Telling it apart

    Not COPD?

    Asthma is reversible and episodic, often with a clear trigger and a history of similar attacks that resolved. COPD is chronic with a smoking history, chronic productive cough, and a baseline the patient never fully returns from.

    Not Anaphylaxis?

    Anaphylaxis affects more than the airway. Look for urticaria, angioedema, gastrointestinal symptoms, and hypotension. If the patient is wheezing and hypotensive after an exposure, treat it as anaphylaxis.

    Not Pulmonary edema?

    Cardiac history, orthopnea, crackles rather than wheeze, frothy pink sputum, and peripheral edema. Cardiac wheeze exists and can genuinely fool you, so weigh the whole picture.
