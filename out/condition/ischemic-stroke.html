    Ischemic stroke

    A vessel supplying the brain blocks, and the tissue it fed begins dying immediately.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    A cerebral artery blocks
    Thrombus or embolus

    Either a clot forms on an atherosclerotic plaque in the vessel itself, or a clot travels from elsewhere and lodges there. The commonest embolic source is the left atrium in atrial fibrillation, where stagnant blood clots and then throws fragments into the circulation. Roughly eighty-five percent of strokes are ischemic rather than hemorrhagic.

    Flow to that territory stops
    No collateral rescue

    Cerebral arteries are functionally end arteries. The circle of Willis provides some collateral supply but it is complete in fewer people than diagrams suggest, and rarely adequate to rescue a territory acutely.

    The core infarcts within minutes
    ~2 million neurons per minute

    Neurons have essentially no energy reserve. Without oxygen and glucose the sodium-potassium pumps fail, cells swell, calcium floods in, and the tissue dies. This part is not recoverable by the time you arrive.

    A rim survives, for now
    The tissue treatment saves

    Surrounding the dead core is a zone receiving just enough collateral flow to stay alive but not enough to function. It produces the deficit you can see, and it is the entire target of thrombolysis and thrombectomy. It shrinks continuously.

    shared node → Brain tissue starves but isn't dead yet

    Oxygen EMR

    Only if saturation is below 94 percent. Routine oxygen in a normoxic stroke patient has not been shown to help. The penumbra is exquisitely sensitive to hypoxia, so treat any genuine desaturation promptly.

    Establish last known well time EMR

    This is arguably the most valuable single piece of information you will gather all shift. It determines eligibility for thrombolysis and thrombectomy. It is when the patient was last seen normal, not when the deficit was noticed. A patient who woke up with symptoms was last well when they went to bed.

    Stroke screen and pre-alert EMR

    A structured screen, face-arm-speech-time or similar, plus a phone call ahead. A pre-alerted stroke centre can have the scanner and team ready, which saves more minutes than anything you do on scene.

    Do not treat the blood pressure EMR

    Blood pressure is usually high in acute stroke and that is largely a compensation maintaining flow to the penumbra through collateral vessels. Lowering it prehospital can extend the infarct.

    Function is lost where the tissue is
    Face, arm, speech, vision, balance

    Because function is mapped to location, the deficit tells you the territory. Middle cerebral artery strokes give face and arm weakness with speech involvement. Posterior circulation strokes give dizziness, double vision, ataxia, and vomiting, and are missed far more often because they do not look like the textbook picture.

    Check blood glucose EMR

    Hypoglycemia mimics stroke exactly, including one-sided weakness, and it is completely reversible. Never transport a stroke without a glucose reading.

    The infarcted tissue swells
    Peaks at 24 to 72 hours

    Dead and dying tissue swells over the following days. In a large stroke this can raise intracranial pressure enough to cause herniation, which is why a patient who was stable on arrival can deteriorate badly on day two.

    shared node → Intracranial pressure rises
    Airway protection is lost
    Swallow and cough fail

    Stroke frequently damages the ability to swallow and to cough, even in a patient who is fully awake. Aspiration is one of the commonest complications and a major cause of death after stroke. Nothing by mouth, including sips of water, until swallowing has been formally assessed.

    shared node → Airway stops protecting itself

    Nothing by mouth EMR

    No food, no drink, no oral medication. Aspiration pneumonia is a leading cause of death after stroke and it is entirely preventable at this stage.

    Position and protect EMR

    Head elevated around thirty degrees if tolerated, or recovery position on the affected side if consciousness is reduced. Suction available.

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    A clot occludes a cerebral artery. The core of the territory dies within minutes; a surrounding rim survives on collateral flow and is salvageable if the vessel is reopened fast enough. Everything you do is about protecting that rim and shortening the clock.

    Stop and escalate

    • Any deficit within the treatment window, which makes this a time-critical emergency
    • Deteriorating level of consciousness, suggesting a large stroke, swelling, or a bleed instead
    • Inability to protect the airway
    • Sudden severe headache, which points toward hemorrhage
    • Seizure at onset, more common in hemorrhagic stroke
    • A patient dismissed as intoxicated who has ataxia, vertigo, and vomiting, which may be a cerebellar stroke

    Telling it apart

    Not Hemorrhagic stroke?

    You cannot reliably tell them apart in the field and you should not try. Hemorrhage is more likely with sudden severe headache, vomiting, rapid deterioration, seizure at onset, and very high blood pressure. The distinction requires a scan, which is another reason the destination matters.

    Not Hypoglycemia?

    Can produce identical focal deficits. Check the glucose. Every time.

    Not Todd's paralysis?

    Temporary weakness following a seizure, which resolves over minutes to hours. A witnessed seizure at onset makes it more likely, but it is a diagnosis made in retrospect and these patients still go to a stroke centre.

    Not Bell's palsy?

    Facial weakness involving the forehead, so the patient cannot wrinkle their brow or close the eye on that side. A stroke spares the forehead because it receives input from both hemispheres.
