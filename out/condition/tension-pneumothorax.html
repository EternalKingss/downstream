    Condition · Pleural space

    Tension pneumothorax

    Air enters the pleural space through a one-way valve and pressure keeps climbing until it strangles the circulation.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    The pleural seal breaks
    Penetrating or blunt injury

    Air enters the pleural space, either through the chest wall from outside or from a torn lung surface. The negative pressure that held the lung expanded is lost.

    anatomy → Pleural space
    A one-way valve forms
    Air in, none out

    Tissue flaps over the breach so that inspiration draws air in but expiration cannot push it back out. This is the single feature that separates a tension pneumothorax from a simple one, and it is why this condition gets progressively worse rather than staying stable.

    Release the occlusive dressing EMR

    If you sealed an open chest wound and the patient then deteriorated, you may have created the valve yourself. Lifting the dressing to let trapped air escape is within EMR scope, costs nothing, and can reverse the entire cascade in seconds. This is the highest-value thing an EMR can do for this patient.

    Vented chest seal EMR

    A commercial vented seal lets air out while preventing it from being drawn back in, which is exactly the opposite of the pathological valve.

    The lung collapses
    Absent breath sounds on that side

    Without negative pressure to hold it out, the elastic lung recoils toward the hilum. Ventilation on that side stops. Blood continues to flow past the collapsed lung without picking up oxygen.

    Intrathoracic pressure climbs
    Every breath adds more air

    The trapped air has nowhere to go and the volume increases with each breath. Pressure in the affected hemithorax now exceeds atmospheric, reversing the normal state of the chest entirely.

    Needle decompression ACP

    Definitive prehospital treatment. Outside EMR and generally outside PCP scope in Alberta, so for an EMR the intervention is early recognition and getting the right resource moving.

    Recognise and escalate early EMR

    This condition is fully reversible right up until it is not. Recognition is the intervention at this level.

    The mediastinum is pushed across
    Great vessels compressed

    Rising pressure shifts the heart, trachea, and great vessels toward the opposite side. The superior and inferior vena cava are thin-walled, low-pressure vessels and they kink and compress easily.

    Blood can't get back to the heart
    Obstructive shock

    With venous return obstructed, the right ventricle has nothing to pump. Cardiac output collapses. Neck veins distend because blood is backing up behind the obstruction, though this sign disappears if the patient is also hypovolemic from bleeding.

    Tissue oxygen falls
    From both collapse and shock

    Two mechanisms stack here: one lung is not ventilating, and circulation is failing so what oxygen there is cannot be delivered.

    shared node → Tissue oxygen falls

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration. Supports the one lung still working while the underlying obstruction is addressed.

    Circulation stops
    Usually PEA

    The heart typically continues to produce organised electrical activity with no output, because the problem is entirely mechanical. Compressions and defibrillation will not fix it. Only decompression will.

    shared node → Circulation stops

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    A simple pneumothorax becomes lethal when the leak only lets air in. Every breath adds more, the lung collapses, and eventually the pressure crushes the great vessels so blood cannot return to the heart. This kills through shock, not through hypoxia.

    Stop and escalate

    • Absent or markedly reduced breath sounds on one side after chest trauma
    • Rapidly worsening shortness of breath with rising pulse and falling blood pressure
    • Distended neck veins, though absent if the patient is also bleeding
    • Deterioration shortly after an occlusive dressing was applied
    • A ventilated patient who becomes suddenly harder to bag and drops their pressure

    Telling it apart

    Not Simple pneumothorax?

    Simple pneumothorax is stable. Tension is progressive and comes with shock. If the patient is deteriorating in front of you rather than holding steady, assume tension.

    Not Hemothorax?

    Blood is dull to percussion and sits at the base; air is hyperresonant and collects at the apex. Hemothorax also causes hypovolemia directly, so neck veins are flat rather than distended.

    Not Cardiac tamponade?

    Both cause obstructive shock with distended neck veins. Tamponade has muffled heart sounds and equal breath sounds bilaterally; tension has unilaterally absent breath sounds.
