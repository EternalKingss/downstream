    Hemorrhagic and hypovolemic shock

    There is not enough volume in the circuit to fill the pump.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Volume leaves the circuit
    Bleeding, burns, vomiting, diarrhea

    Either whole blood is lost through hemorrhage, or plasma is lost through burns, or water and electrolytes are lost through the gut. The mechanism differs but the circulatory consequence is identical.

    Stop the bleeding first EMR

    Nothing else matters while volume is still leaving. Direct pressure, tourniquet, wound packing, pelvic binding, splinting long bones. This is the only intervention that addresses the actual cause.

    Preload falls
    Less returning to the heart

    Roughly two thirds of blood volume normally sits in the venous system. As volume falls, venous return falls, and by the Frank-Starling relationship a less-filled ventricle contracts less forcefully and ejects less.

    anatomy → Veins

    Keep them horizontal EMR

    Supine improves venous return to the heart and cerebral perfusion. Routine leg elevation has fallen out of favour and is not required, but sitting a shocked patient upright is actively harmful.

    The body compensates hard
    Class I and II, pressure still normal

    Venoconstriction returns stored blood to circulation, arteriolar constriction raises resistance, and heart rate rises. Skin, gut, muscle, and kidney are sacrificed to preserve brain and heart. A fit young adult can lose a litre and a half and still show a normal blood pressure.

    shared node → The body compensates
    Tissue perfusion falls
    Cap refill, cool skin, anxiety

    The sacrificed tissues are already ischemic. Capillary refill lengthens, skin cools, urine output falls, and the patient becomes anxious as cerebral flow starts to drop.

    shared node → Tissue perfusion falls

    Oxygen and rapid transport EMR

    Maximise the oxygen content of the blood that remains, and get the patient somewhere that can give blood and stop internal bleeding. Definitive care for major hemorrhage is a surgeon.

    Cells switch to anaerobic metabolism
    Lactate accumulates

    Energy production collapses to a fraction of normal and lactic acid builds up. The falling pH begins to impair the very compensation keeping the patient alive.

    shared node → Cells switch to anaerobic metabolism

    Aggressive warming EMR

    Hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy reinforce each other in what trauma care calls the lethal triad. Warmth is one of the few parts of that triangle you can influence directly in the field.

    Compensation collapses
    Class III to IV, pressure falls

    Acidosis blunts vascular and myocardial responsiveness to catecholamines. Vasoconstriction fails, pressure drops, consciousness falls, and the loop becomes self-reinforcing: worse perfusion produces worse acidosis produces worse perfusion.

    shared node → Compensation fails
    Circulation stops
    Hypovolemic PEA

    An empty heart cannot generate output no matter how well it is contracting or how organised its rhythm looks. Compressions on an empty ventricle achieve very little; the answer is volume and hemorrhage control.

    shared node → Circulation stops

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    The tank is empty. Everything the body does from that point is an attempt to make less blood cover the same distance, and every one of those attempts costs something. Blood pressure is the last thing to fail and the worst thing to wait for.

    Stop and escalate

    • Tachycardia with cool, clammy skin and a normal blood pressure
    • Narrowing pulse pressure
    • Capillary refill beyond two seconds
    • Agitation, anxiety, or confusion
    • Any hypotension, which means at least thirty percent volume loss in an adult
    • A child who looks well but has a mechanism that should have hurt them, because children compensate then crash

    Telling it apart

    Not Cardiogenic shock?

    Both are cold and clammy, but cardiogenic shock has signs of congestion: crackles, distended neck veins, and a cardiac history. Hypovolemic shock has flat neck veins and clear lungs.

    Not Septic shock?

    Early sepsis is typically warm, flushed, and febrile with a bounding pulse and wide pulse pressure, which is the opposite picture. Late sepsis becomes cold and looks identical.

    Not Obstructive shock?

    Tension pneumothorax and tamponade both cause distended neck veins with a mechanical obstruction to filling. Hypovolemia gives flat neck veins.
