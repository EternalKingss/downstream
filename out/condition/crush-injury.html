    Crush injury and crush syndrome

    Prolonged compression kills muscle, and releasing the compression sends its contents into the circulation all at once.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Muscle is compressed
    Rubble, machinery, or lying immobile

    Not only dramatic entrapments. An older patient who has been lying on the floor for a day, or an unconscious overdose patient who has been on their arm all night, develops exactly the same syndrome. Duration matters more than the weight.

    anatomy → Muscles and fascial compartments

    Establish the entrapment time precisely EMR

    The single most valuable piece of information you gather. It determines whether this is a simple injury or a crush syndrome, and only you can obtain it. Ask bystanders, check timestamps, be specific.

    Compressed muscle becomes ischemic
    Direct pressure plus no perfusion

    Direct mechanical damage plus loss of blood supply. Muscle starts dying within a few hours. Because circulation to the area is cut off, the products of that death stay locally sequestered rather than entering the bloodstream.

    Dying cells spill their contents locally
    Potassium, myoglobin, acid, phosphate

    Muscle cells hold very high concentrations of potassium. As they die, potassium, myoglobin, phosphate, and organic acids accumulate in the trapped tissue, building up a toxic load that has nowhere to go while the compression remains.

    Fluid pours into damaged muscle
    Hypovolemia on release

    Damaged muscle is intensely oedematous and can sequester litres of fluid once perfusion returns. The patient becomes profoundly hypovolemic even without external bleeding.

    Aggressive warming EMR

    Entrapped patients are usually exposed for prolonged periods and are frequently hypothermic, which worsens arrhythmia risk in an already irritable heart.

    Compression is released
    The dangerous moment

    Circulation is restored, and everything that accumulated washes into the central circulation at once. This is why crush syndrome kills at the moment of rescue rather than during entrapment, and why extrication is coordinated with the medical team rather than done as fast as possible.

    shared node → Damaged muscle releases its contents

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration during and after extrication.

    Do not release without warning the team EMR

    The defining principle of crush injury management. If entrapment has exceeded roughly fifteen minutes to an hour, release is a medical event. Coordinate it: fluid running first where available, monitoring on, and everyone ready. Never free a long-entrapped limb simply because you can.

    Potassium hits the heart
    Arrhythmia and arrest

    Rising potassium disturbs cardiac conduction directly, producing peaked T waves, widening QRS, and then ventricular fibrillation or asystole. This is the immediate killer and it can happen within minutes of release.

    Monitor continuously through and after release EMR

    Arrhythmia and arrest occur within minutes of reperfusion. Keep hands on, pads on, and reassess constantly.

    Myoglobin blocks the kidneys
    Over hours to days

    Myoglobin precipitates in the renal tubules, particularly in a dehydrated acidotic patient, causing acute kidney injury. This is the delayed killer, and it is why aggressive early fluid is the mainstay of treatment.

    Fluid before release where possible PCP

    Aggressive crystalloid started before the release dilutes the potassium load and protects the kidneys from myoglobin. Beyond EMR scope, which makes early escalation and a clear description of entrapment time essential.

    Compartment syndrome develops
    Swelling after reperfusion

    Reperfusion oedema raises compartment pressure, causing a second wave of ischemia in muscle that survived the original crush.

    shared node → Pressure rises inside a closed compartment

    Do not elevate or wrap the limb tightly EMR

    Elevation worsens the perfusion gradient in a swelling compartment, and tight wrapping adds external pressure to a limb that is already swelling.

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    While the weight is on, the damage is local and contained. The moment it comes off, potassium and myoglobin from destroyed muscle flood the bloodstream. Patients who were talking to you under the rubble have arrested within minutes of being freed.

    Stop and escalate

    • Entrapment longer than about fifteen minutes
    • An older patient found on the floor after a long lie
    • Dark, tea-coloured urine
    • Any arrhythmia during or after extrication
    • Tense, swollen, insensate limb
    • Sudden deterioration at the moment of release
    • A patient who seems well while trapped, which is expected and not reassuring

    Telling it apart

    Not Simple crush without syndrome?

    Brief compression causes local injury without significant systemic release. Duration is the discriminator, which is why the entrapment time matters so much.

    Not Compartment syndrome?

    A complication of crush injury rather than an alternative, and it commonly develops after reperfusion.

    Not Hemorrhagic shock?

    Crush patients are hypovolemic from fluid sequestration rather than bleeding, though they often have both. The distinguishing features are the hyperkalemia risk and the myoglobin.
