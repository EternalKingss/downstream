    Alcohol intoxication and withdrawal

    Intoxication suppresses the brain and the airway; withdrawal does the opposite and is the more dangerous of the two.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Acute alcohol depresses the brain
    Enhances inhibition, blocks excitation

    Alcohol enhances GABA, the main inhibitory neurotransmitter, and blocks glutamate, the main excitatory one. Both push in the same direction: less brain activity. The cerebellum is affected early, which is why coordination and speech go first.

    anatomy → Cerebrum
    Airway protection is lost
    The actual danger of intoxication

    Reduced consciousness with a full stomach and a high likelihood of vomiting. Aspiration is the commonest way acute intoxication kills. Recovery position and suction are the treatment, and this is not a trivial call.

    shared node → Airway stops protecting itself

    Oxygen EMR

    If hypoxic or reduced consciousness.

    Recovery position and suction EMR

    Aspiration is the killer in acute intoxication. Never leave an intoxicated patient supine or unobserved.

    It hides everything else
    The dangerous assumption

    Intoxicated patients fall, are assaulted, become hypothermic, and become hypoglycemic. Alcohol blunts pain and clouds the history, so injuries are missed. Alcohol never explains a deteriorating level of consciousness, unequal pupils, or a focal deficit.

    Never assume alcohol explains it EMR

    The single most important principle. Head injury, hypoglycemia, stroke, hypothermia, infection, and other drugs all present identically and all coexist with alcohol constantly. Check glucose, check temperature, look for head injury, and reassess.

    Keep them warm EMR

    Alcohol causes vasodilation and defeats the main defence against cold. Intoxicated patients outdoors in Alberta become hypothermic remarkably fast.

    It causes hypoglycemia
    Blocks new glucose production

    Alcohol suppresses the liver's ability to manufacture new glucose. In a patient who has not eaten, and particularly in children and malnourished chronic drinkers, this produces genuine hypoglycemia that looks exactly like intoxication.

    shared node → The brain runs out of fuel

    Oral glucose EMR

    If hypoglycemic and able to swallow safely. Alcohol-related hypoglycemia recurs, because the liver still cannot make new glucose, so these patients need hospital rather than a snack.

    The brain adapts to chronic alcohol
    Fewer brakes, more accelerator

    With sustained exposure the brain downregulates inhibitory GABA receptors and upregulates excitatory glutamate receptors to maintain balance. The system is now calibrated to work in the presence of alcohol.

    Removing it leaves an overexcited brain
    6 to 24 hours after the last drink

    The adaptations remain but the alcohol is gone, leaving excitation unopposed. Tremor, sweating, anxiety, tachycardia, hypertension, nausea, and insomnia begin within six to twenty-four hours. This is the mirror image of intoxication and it is far more dangerous.

    shared node → Neurons fire uncontrollably

    Benzodiazepines and thiamine PCP

    Benzodiazepines replace the missing inhibition and are the definitive treatment for withdrawal. Thiamine prevents Wernicke's encephalopathy. Both beyond EMR scope, so recognising withdrawal and escalating is your contribution.

    Seizures, then delirium tremens
    24 to 72 hours

    Generalised seizures typically at twelve to forty-eight hours. Delirium tremens at around forty-eight to seventy-two hours: severe confusion, hallucinations, agitation, fever, and profound autonomic instability. Untreated, delirium tremens has a mortality of several percent, which is why withdrawal is the emergency and intoxication usually is not.

    Take withdrawal seriously EMR

    A shaking, sweating, tachycardic patient who has stopped drinking is in a genuinely dangerous state. Ask when the last drink was, because the timeline predicts what is coming.

    Long-term damage compounds everything
    Clotting, thiamine, falls

    Liver disease impairs clotting, so head injuries bleed more and gastrointestinal bleeding is harder to stop. Thiamine deficiency causes Wernicke's encephalopathy, with confusion, eye movement abnormalities, and ataxia, which is reversible if treated and permanent if not. Brain shrinkage stretches bridging veins, making subdural haematoma common after apparently minor falls.

    Watch for head injury in every fall EMR

    Chronic drinkers have impaired clotting and stretched bridging veins. A minor-seeming fall can produce a subdural that presents days later. Have a very low threshold for transport.

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    Alcohol is a depressant, so acutely it threatens the airway and masks injury. But a brain that has adapted to it becomes dangerously overexcitable when it is removed, and severe withdrawal has a real mortality that intoxication mostly does not.

    Stop and escalate

    • Any deterioration in consciousness rather than gradual improvement
    • Unequal pupils or any focal neurological sign
    • Head injury, however minor it appears
    • Seizure
    • Low glucose
    • Hypothermia
    • Tremor, sweating, and tachycardia in someone who has stopped drinking
    • Confusion with abnormal eye movements and ataxia, suggesting Wernicke's
    • Vomiting blood

    Telling it apart

    Not Head injury?

    The critical one. Intoxication improves slowly over hours; a head injury deteriorates. Any decline, asymmetry, or focal sign is a head injury until proven otherwise, whatever the alcohol level.

    Not Hypoglycemia?

    Indistinguishable clinically and frequently caused by the alcohol itself. Check the glucose on every intoxicated patient.

    Not Stroke?

    Cerebellar stroke causes ataxia, slurred speech, and vomiting and is regularly dismissed as drunkenness. Focal signs are not alcohol.

    Not Opioid or sedative overdose?

    Mixed overdoses are the rule. Pinpoint pupils and a very low respiratory rate suggest opioids and warrant naloxone.
