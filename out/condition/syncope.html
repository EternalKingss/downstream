    Syncope

    A brief global drop in brain perfusion that causes collapse and then resolves once the patient is horizontal.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Something drops cerebral flow
    Vagal, postural, or cardiac

    Three broad groups. Vasovagal, from pain, fear, prolonged standing, straining, or the sight of blood, where a vagal surge slows the heart and dilates vessels. Orthostatic, from volume depletion, medication, or autonomic failure. Cardiac, from an arrhythmia or an outflow obstruction, and this is the group that kills people.

    Cerebral perfusion falls
    The brain is at the top of the circuit

    Standing means the brain sits well above the heart, so it needs adequate pressure to be perfused at all. A sudden fall in cardiac output or vascular tone drops flow below what the brain needs within seconds.

    Oxygen EMR

    Only if hypoxic. Most simple syncope does not need it.

    Lie them flat EMR

    Restores cerebral perfusion immediately. Keep them supine for a while and sit them up slowly, because premature standing frequently causes a second episode.

    Prodrome, if there is one
    Lightheaded, greying vision, nausea

    Vasovagal and orthostatic syncope usually give warning: lightheadedness, tunnel or greying vision, nausea, sweating, ringing in the ears. Cardiac syncope characteristically gives no warning at all, and the patient simply drops. That absence of warning is one of the most useful discriminators you can ask about.

    Ask about the prodrome EMR

    No warning, syncope during exertion, or syncope while lying down all point toward a cardiac cause. That single question meaningfully changes the risk.

    Consciousness is lost and they fall
    Sometimes with brief jerking

    Loss of consciousness follows within seconds. Brief myoclonic jerking from cerebral hypoxia is common and is regularly reported by bystanders as a seizure. The fall itself causes injury, and in older adults a syncopal head injury is often the more serious problem.

    Assess for injury from the fall EMR

    An unguarded fall from standing regularly produces head injuries, facial fractures, and hip fractures. In an older adult on anticoagulants, that head injury is the emergency, not the faint.

    Lying flat restores flow
    Recovery within seconds

    Horizontal removes the pressure penalty of getting blood to the head. Perfusion is restored and consciousness returns quickly, usually within seconds to a minute, with no prolonged confusion. Sitting the patient up too early can cause an immediate second episode.

    The cause is still present
    This is the real question

    The faint has resolved; whatever caused it may not have. Cardiac syncope means an arrhythmia or obstruction that can recur and cause arrest. Syncope from bleeding means the patient is still bleeding. The event is a symptom, and treating it as the diagnosis is how these patients get missed.

    Check glucose and take a full history EMR

    Glucose, medications especially antihypertensives and diuretics, recent bleeding, chest pain, palpitations, and family history of sudden death in a young person.

    Do not dismiss it EMR

    The patient looks completely well by the time you assess them, which is exactly why cardiac syncope gets left at home. A well-looking patient with unexplained syncope still needs assessment.

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    Standing up costs the circulation something. When it can no longer pay, the brain drops off the top of the circuit, the patient falls over, gravity fixes the problem, and they wake up. The question is never the faint itself, it is what caused it.

    Stop and escalate

    • No warning at all before collapse
    • Syncope during exertion
    • Syncope while sitting or lying down
    • Chest pain or palpitations before or after
    • Family history of sudden cardiac death under 40
    • Abnormal or irregular pulse
    • Any injury from the fall, especially head injury on anticoagulants
    • Failure to recover fully within a minute or two

    Telling it apart

    Not Seizure?

    Seizure has a prolonged postictal confusion; syncope recovers within seconds. Tongue biting on the side and incontinence favour seizure, though incontinence occurs in both. Brief jerking is common in syncope and does not make it a seizure.

    Not Cardiac arrest?

    If they do not wake up, it is not syncope. Check the pulse in every collapse.

    Not Stroke or TIA?

    Focal deficits, and generally without complete loss of consciousness. Syncope is a global perfusion problem and should not leave focal signs.

    Not Hypoglycemia?

    Slower onset, sweating and confusion that persists after lying down, and it does not resolve simply by being horizontal.
