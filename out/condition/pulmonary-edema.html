    Pulmonary edema

    Fluid is forced out of the pulmonary capillaries into the alveoli, drowning the patient from the inside.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Pressure rises behind the left heart
    Failure, arrhythmia, or hypertension

    The left ventricle cannot move forward what the lungs are delivering. Pressure rises in the left atrium, back through the pulmonary veins, and into the pulmonary capillaries. Causes include acute infarction, new atrial fibrillation, a hypertensive crisis, valve failure, or decompensated chronic heart failure.

    anatomy → Myocardium

    Nitroglycerin scope varies

    Dilates veins, drops preload, and lowers the pressure driving fluid across the capillary wall. Genuinely effective and works within minutes, provided the blood pressure supports it. Ineffective and dangerous if the patient is hypotensive, because then the problem is cardiogenic shock.

    Sit fully upright, legs dependent EMR

    The single most effective non-drug intervention. Pools blood in the legs, reduces venous return, drops pulmonary capillary pressure, and lets fluid settle away from the upper lung. Works within minutes and requires nothing but a stretcher. Never lay these patients flat, for any reason.

    Look for the trigger EMR

    Chest pain, a new irregular pulse, or a very high blood pressure changes what the receiving team does first.

    Fluid is forced out of the capillaries
    Hydrostatic beats oncotic

    Fluid movement across the capillary wall is a balance between hydrostatic pressure pushing out and oncotic pressure from plasma proteins pulling in. When capillary pressure rises high enough, the balance tips and plasma is forced into the interstitium.

    anatomy → Capillaries
    The interstitium fills first
    Breathlessness before crackles

    Fluid accumulates around the alveoli before entering them. The lung becomes stiffer, the diffusion distance lengthens, and the patient becomes breathless and anxious. At this stage the chest may still sound relatively clear, which fools people.

    shared node → The diffusion barrier thickens
    Fluid enters the alveoli
    Crackles, then frothy sputum

    Once the interstitium is saturated, fluid crosses into the air spaces. Crackles appear at the bases and rise. Air mixing with plasma produces the characteristic frothy sputum, which is pink when red cells are forced across too.

    anatomy → Alveoli

    CPAP PCP

    Positive pressure pushes fluid back out of the alveoli, recruits collapsed lung, and reduces preload. One of the most dramatically effective interventions in prehospital care, and outside EMR scope, so recognising who needs it and escalating early is your contribution.

    Flooded alveoli are perfused but not ventilated
    Shunt

    Blood flows past alveoli full of fluid and returns unoxygenated. As with pneumonia, this is a true shunt, and it explains why some patients respond poorly to oxygen alone.

    shared node → Ventilation and perfusion stop matching
    Hypoxia weakens the heart further
    The loop closes

    A hypoxic myocardium contracts less effectively, which raises filling pressures, which forces more fluid across, which worsens the hypoxia. This loop is why acute pulmonary edema deteriorates so fast and why interrupting it early matters so much.

    shared node → Tissue oxygen falls

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration. Interrupting the hypoxia limb of the spiral is the highest-value thing an EMR can do here, because it protects the myocardium that is driving the whole process.

    Keep them calm EMR

    These patients are terrified and the anxiety is physiologically real: catecholamines raise heart rate and afterload in a heart that cannot cope with either.

    The patient tires
    Rising CO2, falling effort

    Breathing against stiff, wet lungs at a very high rate for a sustained period is exhausting. When effort falls and carbon dioxide starts to rise, the patient is close to needing their breathing taken over entirely.

    shared node → Respiratory muscles fail

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    Pressure behind the left heart rises until it overcomes the forces holding plasma inside the capillaries. Fluid crosses into the lung tissue and then into the air spaces themselves. The patient is literally drowning in their own plasma, and it can develop over minutes.

    Stop and escalate

    • Frothy or pink sputum
    • Unable to tolerate lying flat at all
    • Crackles rising above the bases
    • Hypotension, which reframes this as cardiogenic shock
    • Chest pain alongside the breathlessness
    • Falling respiratory effort or drowsiness
    • Saturation that stays low on high-flow oxygen

    Telling it apart

    Not Asthma or COPD?

    Cardiac wheeze is real and fools people. Favour edema with orthopnea, ankle swelling, frothy sputum, a cardiac history, and basal crackles. Salbutamol in undiagnosed pulmonary edema adds tachycardia to a failing heart.

    Not Pneumonia?

    Fever, focal crackles rather than bilateral basal, purulent rather than frothy sputum, and pleuritic pain.

    Not Pulmonary embolism?

    Sudden onset with a clear chest and no orthopnea.

    Not Non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema?

    Drowning, smoke inhalation, high altitude, and severe sepsis can flood alveoli through capillary damage rather than pressure. The lungs look the same but nitroglycerin will not help, because the pressure was never the problem.
