    Cardiogenic shock

    The tank is full and the pipes are fine, but the pump has failed.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Contractile muscle is lost
    Usually a large infarct

    Most commonly a large anterior infarct destroying a substantial portion of the left ventricle. It can also follow sustained arrhythmia, myocarditis, end-stage cardiomyopathy, valve rupture, or severe cardiac contusion after blunt trauma.

    anatomy → Myocardium
    Stroke volume collapses
    Roughly 40 percent of LV involved

    The ventricle fills normally but cannot eject effectively. Cardiac output falls, and the heart compensates the only way it can, by increasing rate, which shortens diastole, which reduces both filling time and coronary perfusion time. The compensation makes the problem worse.

    shared node → Cardiac output falls

    Early recognition and escalation EMR

    Mortality is very high and definitive treatment is well beyond prehospital scope. Getting the right resources moving early is what you can actually change.

    Fluid backs up into the lungs
    Crackles, frothy sputum, orthopnea

    Blood the left ventricle cannot move forward accumulates behind it, raising pulmonary capillary pressure until fluid is forced into the alveoli. The patient is now hypoxic on top of being hypoperfused.

    shared node → Fluid backs up into the lungs

    Oxygen EMR

    Titrate to 94 percent or above. These patients are hypoxic from pulmonary edema on top of being hypoperfused, so the oxygen content of what little output they have matters enormously.

    Position upright if pressure allows EMR

    Sitting up lets fluid settle to the bases and improves the mechanics of breathing. Balance this against the fact that they are hypotensive, and let the patient's own tolerance guide you.

    Do not load fluid reflexively EMR

    This is the one shock where the tank is already full. Fluid worsens the pulmonary edema without improving output. Recognising cardiogenic shock and flagging it early is the intervention at EMR level.

    Tissue perfusion falls
    Cold, mottled, confused

    Forward flow is inadequate. Skin becomes cold and mottled, capillary refill lengthens, and the patient becomes confused. The classic picture is a patient who is wet in the lungs and dry at the fingertips.

    shared node → Tissue perfusion falls
    The heart starves itself
    The self-reinforcing loop

    Coronary perfusion depends on diastolic blood pressure, which is now falling. So the failing heart receives less blood, which weakens it further, which lowers the pressure further. This is the loop that makes cardiogenic shock so lethal, and it is why simply loading fluid does not fix it.

    shared node → Heart muscle starves

    Minimise exertion completely EMR

    Every bit of work this patient does raises myocardial oxygen demand in a heart that is already starving itself.

    Circulation stops
    Progressive, or a terminal rhythm

    Either through progressive failure to asystole and PEA, or through a lethal arrhythmia arising from the ischemic, dilated, and electrolyte-deranged myocardium.

    shared node → Circulation stops

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    Enough heart muscle has stopped working that cardiac output can no longer meet demand. Fluid backs up behind the failing ventricle while the tissues in front of it starve, so the patient is simultaneously overloaded and underperfused.

    Stop and escalate

    • Hypotension with crackles and distended neck veins together
    • Cold, mottled peripheries with a wet-sounding chest
    • Chest pain with hypotension
    • Deteriorating level of consciousness in a cardiac patient
    • Blood pressure falling after nitroglycerin, which suggests right ventricular involvement

    Telling it apart

    Not Hypovolemic shock?

    Flat neck veins and clear lungs point to volume loss. Distended neck veins and crackles point to the pump.

    Not Obstructive shock?

    Tension pneumothorax has unilaterally absent breath sounds; tamponade has muffled heart sounds with clear lungs. Cardiogenic shock usually has crackles and a cardiac history.

    Not Septic shock?

    Fever, a source of infection, warm skin early, and a wide pulse pressure. Cardiogenic shock is cold from the start.
