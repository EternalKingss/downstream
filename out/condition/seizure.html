    Seizure and status epilepticus

    Uncontrolled synchronous neuronal firing, which threatens the patient mainly through the airway and through its own metabolic cost.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    The balance tips
    Epilepsy, glucose, sodium, fever, toxins

    Excitation overwhelms inhibition. Causes include established epilepsy with a missed dose, low glucose, low sodium, hypoxia, fever in young children, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, stimulant toxicity, head injury, stroke, infection, and eclampsia in pregnancy. Finding the cause is as important as managing the event.

    Oral glucose EMR

    Only once the seizure has stopped and the patient can protect their airway and swallow. Never put anything in the mouth of a convulsing patient.

    Check glucose EMR

    Hypoglycemia is a common and instantly reversible cause. Check it in every seizure patient, before assuming epilepsy.

    Firing spreads through the cortex
    Tonic then clonic phases

    A focal discharge recruits neighbouring neurons and generalises. The tonic phase is sustained contraction of every muscle group at once, which is why the patient goes rigid and often cries out as air is forced through the larynx. The clonic phase is rhythmic contraction and relaxation.

    shared node → Neurons fire uncontrollably

    Protect them, don't restrain them EMR

    Move furniture and hazards away, pad the head, loosen anything tight around the neck. Do not hold the limbs down, which causes injury and achieves nothing, and never put anything in the mouth. People do not swallow their tongue.

    Effective ventilation stops
    Cyanosis during the event

    During the tonic phase the respiratory muscles are contracted rather than cycling, so no meaningful gas exchange occurs. Patients commonly become cyanotic during a generalised seizure. This resolves once the seizure stops, which is why a self-limiting seizure is usually not harmful.

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration during and after. Do not attempt to force anything between the teeth to deliver it; a mask held near the face is fine until the convulsion stops.

    The airway is unprotected
    Secretions, blood, vomit

    The patient cannot maintain their own airway or clear secretions, the tongue may be bitten and bleeding, and vomiting is common. This, not the convulsion, is what actually harms most seizure patients.

    shared node → Airway stops protecting itself

    Recovery position once convulsing stops EMR

    The single highest-value intervention here. Lets secretions, blood, and vomit drain rather than be aspirated, and lets the tongue fall forward.

    Suction and airway adjuncts EMR

    A nasopharyngeal airway is usually better tolerated than an oropharyngeal one in a patient with a clenched jaw or a returning gag reflex.

    Metabolic demand goes through the roof
    Heat, lactate, glucose consumption

    Continuously firing neurons and contracting muscles consume oxygen and glucose at an enormous rate while producing heat and lactate. Temperature rises, lactic acidosis develops, and glucose is consumed. In a short seizure none of this matters; in a prolonged one it becomes the problem.

    shared node → Cells switch to anaerobic metabolism

    Cool actively if febrile EMR

    Prolonged seizing generates significant heat, and hyperthermia worsens neuronal injury.

    It doesn't stop
    Beyond 5 minutes, or recurring

    Status epilepticus is a seizure lasting beyond about five minutes, or repeated seizures without recovery of consciousness in between. At this point the brain's own inhibitory mechanisms have failed, it stops being self-limiting, and neuronal death begins from metabolic exhaustion regardless of oxygenation.

    Time it, and time the gaps EMR

    Actual clock times, not estimates. Whether this is a two-minute seizure or status epilepticus determines everything the receiving team does, and people consistently overestimate seizure duration.

    Benzodiazepines PCP

    The definitive treatment for status, enhancing inhibitory signalling to break the discharge. Outside EMR scope, so recognising status and escalating quickly is the intervention available to you.

    Postictal recovery
    Confusion, drowsiness, sometimes weakness

    After a generalised seizure the brain is metabolically depleted and function returns gradually. Confusion, drowsiness, headache, and sometimes temporary focal weakness are expected. A patient who is not steadily improving is not simply postictal, and you should look for another cause.

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    The brain's electrical activity loses its brakes and spreads. Most seizures stop on their own and the patient recovers. The dangerous ones are the ones that do not stop, and the dangerous moment in any of them is the airway.

    Stop and escalate

    • Seizure lasting more than five minutes, or a second seizure before recovery
    • First seizure in an adult with no history
    • Seizure in pregnancy, which is eclampsia until proven otherwise
    • Seizure after head injury
    • Failure to steadily improve in the postictal period
    • Fever with headache and neck stiffness
    • Focal weakness that persists

    Telling it apart

    Not Syncope with myoclonic jerks?

    Fainting commonly causes brief jerking from cerebral hypoxia and gets reported as a seizure. Syncope has rapid full recovery once horizontal, with no prolonged postictal confusion.

    Not Cardiac arrest?

    Arrest frequently produces a brief convulsion. Always check for a pulse in anyone who collapsed and convulsed, especially if they do not begin recovering.

    Not Hypoglycemia?

    Can cause a seizure directly and is fully reversible. Checking glucose distinguishes them and takes seconds.

    Not Psychogenic non-epileptic seizure?

    May have preserved awareness, closed and resisting eyes, side-to-side head movement, and no postictal confusion. Do not attempt this call in the field; treat as a seizure.
