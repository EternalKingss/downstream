    Condition · Spinal cord

    Spinal cord injury

    The cable is damaged, and everything below the damage is disconnected.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Bone or disc damages the cord
    Flexion, extension, rotation, compression

    Vertebrae fracture or dislocate and impinge on the cord, or ligaments tear and allow abnormal movement. In older adults with pre-existing spinal narrowing, cord injury can occur with no fracture at all, from a relatively minor fall involving neck extension.

    anatomy → Spinal cord

    Spinal motion restriction EMR

    Manual in-line stabilisation from the moment of first contact, then appropriate immobilisation. The goal is preventing further movement of an unstable column, not immobilising a patient rigidly for the sake of it. Modern practice favours minimal handling and a scoop or vacuum mattress over a rigid long board.

    Signals stop crossing the injury
    Motor and sensory loss below the level

    Descending motor tracts and ascending sensory tracts are interrupted. The level of injury determines what is lost, because everything below is cut off. Complete injuries lose everything below; incomplete injuries retain some function and have far better prognosis, which is why documenting exactly what is preserved matters.

    Document a baseline neurological exam EMR

    Movement and sensation in all four limbs, with the level at which sensation changes, recorded before and after every move. Whether an injury is complete or incomplete changes prognosis and management enormously, and your baseline may be the only pre-hospital record of it.

    Breathing may be lost
    C3-4-5 keeps the diaphragm alive

    The phrenic nerve arises from C3, C4, and C5. An injury above that level stops spontaneous breathing entirely and immediately. An injury lower in the cervical or upper thoracic cord spares the diaphragm but paralyses the intercostals, leaving a patient breathing with the diaphragm alone, who will tire over hours.

    anatomy → Chest wall and diaphragm

    Watch the breathing continuously EMR

    Diaphragmatic breathing with no chest wall movement means the intercostals are out. That patient is working on one muscle group and will fatigue. Be ready to assist ventilation before they fail rather than after.

    Sympathetic control is lost
    If the injury is above T6

    Sympathetic outflow leaves the cord between T1 and L2. An injury above that disconnects it, causing vasodilation, loss of the ability to increase heart rate, and loss of the ability to shiver or sweat below the injury.

    anatomy → Autonomic nervous system
    Blood pressure falls without tachycardia
    Warm, dry, pink, bradycardic

    Vessels dilate, blood pools, preload falls, and the heart cannot speed up to compensate because the accelerator fibres are also cut. Vagal input is unaffected because it travels in a cranial nerve, so the patient becomes bradycardic instead of tachycardic.

    shared node → Tissue perfusion falls

    Treat for hemorrhage first EMR

    A hypotensive trauma patient is far more likely to be bleeding than to have neurogenic shock. Neurogenic shock is a field diagnosis of exclusion, and getting this the wrong way round is dangerous.

    Swelling and poor perfusion extend the damage
    Hours after the impact

    Bleeding into the cord, edema, and inflammation over the following hours damage tissue that survived the initial impact. Hypotension and hypoxia accelerate this markedly. This is the part of the injury that prehospital care actually affects.

    Oxygen EMR

    The injured cord is highly sensitive to hypoxia. Maintain saturation above 94 percent, and watch for tiring diaphragmatic breathing.

    Maintain blood pressure EMR

    The injured cord needs perfusion to survive the swelling. Hypotension converts salvageable tissue into permanent deficit.

    Temperature control fails
    Can't shiver or vasoconstrict

    Below the injury the patient cannot vasoconstrict, cannot shiver, and cannot sweat. They become poikilothermic, drifting toward ambient temperature. In Alberta this is not a footnote; these patients become hypothermic remarkably fast, even indoors.

    Aggressive warming EMR

    These patients cannot thermoregulate below the injury. Blankets on early, minimise exposure, warm the compartment.

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    Bone or disc material injures the cord, and then swelling, bleeding, and poor perfusion over the following hours convert some of the salvageable tissue into permanent damage. Handling and oxygenation are how you influence that second part.

    Stop and escalate

    • Any weakness, numbness, or tingling after trauma
    • Neck or back pain with midline tenderness
    • Diaphragmatic breathing with no chest movement
    • Hypotension without tachycardia
    • Priapism, uncommon but highly specific
    • Loss of bladder or bowel control
    • A patient who cannot be reliably assessed because of intoxication, distracting injury, or altered consciousness

    Telling it apart

    Not Neurogenic shock alone?

    Neurogenic shock is the circulatory consequence of cord injury, not a separate event. The distinction that matters in the field is neurogenic shock versus hemorrhagic shock, not versus cord injury.

    Not Spinal shock?

    A confusingly named neurological phenomenon: temporary total loss of cord function below the injury, with flaccid paralysis and absent reflexes, which may resolve over days to weeks. It means the initial exam can overstate the eventual deficit.

    Not Peripheral nerve injury?

    Follows the distribution of a single nerve or root rather than a level below which everything is lost. A patient with an isolated wrist drop has a radial nerve problem, not a cord problem.
