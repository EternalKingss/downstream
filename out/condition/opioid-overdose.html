    Opioid overdose

    The brainstem's drive to breathe is suppressed while the lungs themselves are completely healthy.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Opioids bind brainstem receptors
    Mu receptors in the respiratory centre

    Opioids act on mu receptors throughout the central nervous system. In the medulla they suppress the chemoreceptor response to rising carbon dioxide, which is the primary stimulus to breathe. The lungs, the chest wall, and the airway are all mechanically normal.

    anatomy → Brainstem

    Naloxone EMR

    A competitive antagonist that displaces opioids from the receptor, reversing the whole cascade at its source. Its duration is often shorter than the opioid it reversed, which is the single most important thing to know about it: patients can re-sedate.

    Titrate naloxone to breathing, not to consciousness EMR

    The goal is adequate ventilation, not a fully awake patient. Reversing too aggressively precipitates acute withdrawal: vomiting, agitation, and a patient who leaves before the naloxone wears off.

    The drive to breathe is suppressed
    Slow, shallow, then irregular

    Respiratory rate falls, often to single figures, and tidal volume drops. The patient does not feel breathless because the sensation of air hunger is generated by the same suppressed system. They are entirely comfortable while suffocating.

    Alveolar ventilation falls
    CO2 rises, oxygen falls

    Not enough air reaches the alveoli each minute. Carbon dioxide accumulates and oxygen falls. Unlike most respiratory emergencies, both go wrong together, because the problem is total ventilation rather than gas exchange.

    shared node → Carbon dioxide accumulates

    Ventilate them EMR

    This is the actual treatment, and it is more important than naloxone. Bag-valve-mask ventilation reverses the hypoxia immediately while naloxone takes minutes. A patient who is ventilated well does not die of an opioid overdose.

    Consciousness falls
    Pinpoint pupils, unrousable

    Sedation from the drug is compounded by hypoxia and carbon dioxide narcosis. Pinpoint pupils are characteristic but not universal, and are absent in some mixed overdoses and in severe hypoxia where the pupils may dilate.

    shared node → Level of consciousness drops

    Consider what else is on board EMR

    Naloxone does nothing for benzodiazepines, alcohol, or stimulants. A patient who does not wake up after adequate naloxone has something else going on, including possibly a head injury from the collapse.

    The airway is lost
    Tongue, secretions, vomit

    Muscle tone falls, the tongue occludes the pharynx, and the gag reflex disappears. Vomiting is common with opioids, so aspiration is a genuine and frequent second injury. This is often what actually kills, rather than the respiratory depression alone.

    shared node → Airway stops protecting itself

    Open and protect the airway EMR

    Head-tilt chin-lift, suction ready, adjunct as needed, and recovery position once breathing adequately. Vomiting is very common, both from the opioid and after naloxone.

    Tissue oxygen falls
    Cyanosis, bradycardia

    Progressive hypoxia leads to cyanosis and then to hypoxic bradycardia as the heart itself begins to fail.

    shared node → Tissue oxygen falls

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration. Useful, but it does not fix the problem, because the patient is not moving enough air to use it.

    Respiratory then cardiac arrest
    Fully reversible if caught

    Breathing stops while the heart continues for some minutes. This window is enormous, because a patient in opioid-induced respiratory arrest who receives ventilation will very often recover completely. If it progresses to cardiac arrest, survival falls sharply.

    shared node → Breathing stops

    Do not let them refuse transport EMR

    Naloxone commonly wears off before long-acting opioids do, and fentanyl analogues can outlast several doses. A patient who wakes up, feels fine, and walks away can re-sedate and die alone. This is the commonest fatal error in overdose management.

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    Opioids do not damage the lungs at all. They switch off the brainstem's response to carbon dioxide, so the patient simply stops breathing enough. It is a ventilation problem with a perfect set of lungs, and it is one of the most completely reversible emergencies in medicine.

    Stop and escalate

    • Respiratory rate under 10
    • Any period of apnea
    • Cyanosis
    • Failure to respond to adequate naloxone, which means another cause
    • A patient who wants to leave after naloxone
    • Known fentanyl or carfentanil exposure, which may need repeated dosing
    • Signs of aspiration, or a head injury from the collapse

    Telling it apart

    Not Other sedative overdose?

    Benzodiazepines and alcohol cause sedation with less profound respiratory depression and normal-sized pupils. Naloxone will not reverse them, and mixed overdoses are the rule rather than the exception.

    Not Head injury?

    Reduced consciousness with pinpoint pupils can also occur with a pontine bleed. A patient who does not respond to naloxone, or who has any external head trauma, needs to be treated as a head injury.

    Not Hypoglycemia?

    Check the glucose. It is quick and it is a completely different treatment.

    Not Hypoxia from another cause?

    Any cause of severe hypoxia produces reduced consciousness. The distinguishing feature of opioid overdose is that the respiratory rate is low rather than high.
