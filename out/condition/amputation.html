    Traumatic amputation

    A part is severed, and the immediate problem is bleeding while the longer-term one is whether it can be reattached.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    The part is severed
    Guillotine, crush, or avulsion

    A clean guillotine cut has the best reattachment prospects. Crush amputations destroy tissue over a wide zone. Avulsion, where the part is torn off, damages nerves and vessels far up the limb and reattaches poorly. The mechanism determines the surgical outcome.

    anatomy → Soft tissue and wounds

    Look for other injuries EMR

    The energy that removed a limb went elsewhere too. The amputation is visually dominant and it is easy to miss a chest or abdominal injury next to it.

    Severed arteries constrict and retract
    Sometimes less bleeding than expected

    A cleanly transected artery constricts and retracts into surrounding tissue, which can substantially limit blood loss. A partially transected vessel cannot do this and bleeds far worse, which is why an incomplete amputation is often more dangerous than a complete one.

    anatomy → Arteries and arterioles

    Handle partial amputations gently EMR

    Do not complete a partial amputation. Splint it in alignment, dress it, and support it. Partial amputations often bleed worse because the vessels cannot retract.

    Blood loss can be catastrophic
    Especially proximal limbs

    A proximal limb amputation can exsanguinate a patient in minutes. This is exactly the injury that tourniquets exist for, and the evidence for their early use is strong.

    shared node → The body compensates

    Tourniquet early for major limb amputation EMR

    High and tight, tightened until bleeding stops, time written on it. Do not hesitate and do not loosen it. Modern evidence is unambiguous that early tourniquet use in traumatic amputation saves lives and that the limb tolerates it well for hours.

    The severed part starts dying
    The reattachment clock

    Muscle-containing parts survive roughly four to six hours warm, extended to perhaps twelve if properly cooled. Digits, which contain little muscle, tolerate longer, up to around twelve hours warm and twenty-four cooled. Preservation directly changes whether reattachment is possible.

    Preserve the part correctly EMR

    Wrap it in slightly moistened sterile gauze, seal it in a plastic bag, and place that bag on ice or in ice water. Never put the part directly on ice, which causes freezing injury, and never submerge it in water, which macerates the tissue. Both mistakes destroy any chance of reattachment.

    Bring the part, but never delay for it EMR

    The patient always takes priority. If the part cannot be found quickly, transport and let others search and follow with it.

    The wound is contaminated
    Infection risk

    Almost all traumatic amputations are contaminated. Infection risk is high and influences whether reattachment is attempted at all.

    Hemorrhagic shock
    If bleeding is not controlled

    Blood loss combined with the pain-driven catecholamine response and often other injuries from the same mechanism.

    shared node → Compensation fails

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration in significant blood loss.

    Warmth and horizontal EMR

    Hypothermia impairs clotting, and these patients are usually significantly volume-depleted.

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    Clean guillotine amputations often bleed less than you would expect, because vessels constrict and retract. Crush and avulsion amputations bleed worse and reattach poorly. Control the bleeding first, then preserve the part properly.

    Stop and escalate

    • Proximal limb amputation, above the elbow or knee
    • Uncontrolled bleeding despite direct pressure
    • Signs of shock
    • Partial amputation with heavy bleeding
    • Crush or avulsion mechanism
    • Prolonged time since injury
    • Other injuries from the same mechanism

    Telling it apart

    Not Degloving injury?

    Skin is stripped from underlying tissue but the part remains attached. It looks less severe than an amputation and often is not, because the skin has lost its blood supply and will die.

    Not Crush injury with an intact limb?

    An intact but crushed limb carries the crush syndrome risks on release. An amputated limb does not, because the damaged tissue is no longer connected.
