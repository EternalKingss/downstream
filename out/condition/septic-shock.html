    Septic shock

    The response to infection dilates the vessels and makes them leak, so the same volume no longer fills a much bigger container.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Infection is established
    Lung, urine, skin, abdomen

    Pneumonia and urinary infection are the most common sources, followed by skin and soft tissue, and abdominal sources. In older adults the classic signs of infection are often absent and confusion may be the only presentation.

    Temperature management EMR

    Fever raises metabolic demand considerably. Hypothermia in sepsis is a bad sign rather than a comfort issue and needs active warming.

    The response goes systemic
    Mediators enter the bloodstream

    Bacterial products and the body's own inflammatory mediators enter the circulation and act on vessels everywhere, not just at the site of infection. The response that should have been local becomes body-wide, and it is this response rather than the bacteria that causes the shock.

    Vessels dilate everywhere
    Warm, flushed, bounding pulse

    Systemic vascular resistance collapses. The same cardiac output now has to fill a far larger vascular space, so pressure falls even though the heart may be pumping more than usual. This is why early sepsis looks warm and flushed rather than cold and pale, and why it is so often missed.

    Recognise it early EMR

    Sepsis kills more people than most of the dramatic conditions on this site, and the single biggest determinant of survival is how early it is identified. Fever or hypothermia, a rate over 90, a respiratory rate over 20, and confusion in the context of possible infection is enough to escalate.

    Capillaries start leaking
    Fluid drains into the tissues

    Inflammatory mediators increase capillary permeability, so plasma escapes into the interstitium. The patient can be visibly swollen and fluid overloaded while their intravascular space is profoundly empty. This is a relative hypovolemia stacked on top of the vasodilation.

    anatomy → Capillaries
    Blood goes to the wrong places
    Flow without delivery

    Microvascular regulation breaks down. Some capillary beds are overperfused and others are shut, and even where flow exists the cells may be unable to use the oxygen properly. Lactate rises despite what looks like adequate delivery, which is a feature that distinguishes this from simple hypovolemia.

    Tissue perfusion fails
    Then the skin turns cold

    As the process advances, compensatory vasoconstriction and falling cardiac output turn the warm flushed patient into a cold mottled one. By this point the mortality is very high.

    shared node → Tissue perfusion falls

    Oxygen EMR

    Titrate to target. Oxygen demand is elevated by fever and by the inflammatory response itself.

    Keep them horizontal EMR

    Maximises what venous return there is in a patient whose vascular space is far too large for their volume.

    Organs begin to fail
    Kidneys, liver, clotting, brain

    Sustained maldistributed perfusion and widespread inflammation damage multiple organ systems at once. Clotting becomes deranged, kidneys shut down, and consciousness falls.

    shared node → Cells switch to anaerobic metabolism

    Rapid transport and pre-alert EMR

    Antibiotics and fluid resuscitation are time-critical and are not available to you. Getting the receiving hospital ready is the intervention.

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    Infection triggers a body-wide inflammatory response. Blood vessels dilate and become porous, so circulating volume both spreads too thin and drains into the tissues. There was never a shortage of blood; the container simply got too large and started leaking.

    Stop and escalate

    • Confusion in an older adult with any sign of infection, which may be the only presentation
    • Fever or an abnormally low temperature
    • Respiratory rate over 20 without an obvious respiratory cause
    • Warm, flushed skin with hypotension, which is the opposite of what shock is supposed to look like
    • Mottled skin or a non-blanching rash
    • A patient who says they feel like they are dying

    Telling it apart

    Not Hypovolemic shock?

    Early sepsis is warm with a wide pulse pressure and bounding pulses; hypovolemia is cold with a narrow pulse pressure. Late sepsis looks identical to hypovolemia.

    Not Anaphylaxis?

    Both are distributive. Anaphylaxis is sudden, follows an exposure, and comes with urticaria, angioedema, and airway involvement. Sepsis develops over hours to days with a fever and an infective source.

    Not Neurogenic shock?

    Neurogenic shock is warm and dry below the level of injury, usually bradycardic, and follows spinal trauma. Septic patients are usually tachycardic and often diaphoretic.
