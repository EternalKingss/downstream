    Meningitis and meningococcal sepsis

    Infection of the membranes around the brain and cord, which in its bacterial form can kill a healthy person within hours.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Organisms reach the meninges
    Bloodstream, sinuses, or skull fracture

    Most commonly bacteria colonising the nasopharynx cross into the bloodstream and then into the cerebrospinal fluid. It can also spread directly from sinus or ear infection, or through a skull fracture. Viral meningitis is far commoner and usually far less dangerous; bacterial meningitis is the emergency.

    Protect yourself EMR

    Meningococcal disease is transmitted by respiratory droplets. Mask the patient and yourself, document the exposure, and follow your service's post-exposure prophylaxis process. This is one of the few genuinely important self-protection scenarios in EMS.

    The membranes inflame
    Headache, neck stiffness, photophobia

    Cerebrospinal fluid is normally a near-sterile, immunologically quiet space, so the inflammatory response when something enters it is intense. Inflamed meninges are exquisitely sensitive to stretch, which produces neck stiffness and pain on any movement that tugs on them, and to light.

    Minimise light and stimulation EMR

    Photophobia is genuine and distressing. Dim the compartment, handle gently, and avoid unnecessary movement of the neck.

    The brain swells
    Pressure rises inside the box

    Inflammation increases capillary permeability and impairs cerebrospinal fluid reabsorption. Both add volume inside a rigid skull, so intracranial pressure climbs and consciousness falls.

    The organism spills into the blood
    Meningococcal sepsis

    In meningococcal disease particularly, the organism multiplies in the bloodstream and releases endotoxin. This can occur with or without meningitis, and the septic form is the one that kills fastest. Deterioration from feeling unwell to profound shock can take a matter of hours.

    Recognise and pre-alert EMR

    Antibiotics are time-critical and are not yours to give. Getting the patient to them fast, with the hospital warned, is the intervention that changes survival.

    Small vessels are damaged and clot
    The non-blanching rash

    Endotoxin damages the endothelium and activates clotting throughout the microcirculation. Small vessels leak and thrombose, producing bleeding into the skin. That is the petechial or purpuric rash, and it does not blanch under pressure because the blood is outside the vessels.

    anatomy → Capillaries

    Look for the rash, everywhere EMR

    Check the whole body including the trunk, soles, and conjunctivae, and use the glass test: a rash that does not blanch under pressure is bleeding into the skin. It is a late sign, so its absence is not reassuring, but its presence is a genuine emergency.

    Circulation fails
    Distributive shock

    Widespread vasodilation and capillary leak produce distributive shock, while microvascular thrombosis simultaneously blocks delivery. Peripheral tissue can become ischemic enough to require amputation in survivors.

    shared node → Tissue perfusion falls

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration if hypoxic or shocked. Metabolic demand is high from fever and inflammation.

    Keep them horizontal if shocked EMR

    Maximises venous return in a patient whose vascular space is far too large for their volume.

    Consciousness and circulation fail
    Hours from first symptom

    Raised intracranial pressure and profound shock together produce rapid deterioration to unresponsiveness, seizure, and death. Speed of recognition is essentially the whole prognosis.

    Test yourself

    In one breath

    The membranes wrapping the central nervous system become infected and inflamed. Because that space is continuous around the brain and cord, the infection is never localised, and when the organism spills into the bloodstream it causes a form of septic shock that moves faster than almost anything else you will see.

    Stop and escalate

    • Non-blanching rash of any size, anywhere
    • Fever with headache and neck stiffness
    • Photophobia
    • Altered consciousness or confusion with fever
    • Cold hands and feet with a fever, an early sign in children
    • Leg pain, unusual skin colour, and cold extremities, which precede the rash in children
    • A rapid deterioration over hours
    • A bulging fontanelle or high-pitched cry in an infant

    Telling it apart

    Not Subarachnoid hemorrhage?

    Both give headache and neck stiffness. Subarachnoid is instantly maximal and afebrile; meningitis builds over hours with fever.

    Not Influenza or a viral illness?

    The single hardest and most consequential call, because early meningococcal disease looks exactly like flu. Cold hands and feet with fever, leg pain, abnormal skin colour, and rapid progression are the discriminators. If the parents say the child is deteriorating faster than any illness they have seen, believe them.

    Not Viral meningitis?

    Much commoner and usually far less severe, but not distinguishable in the field. Treat every suspected meningitis as bacterial.
