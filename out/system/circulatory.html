    Circulatory system

    Move oxygenated blood to tissue and bring it back.

    Where things go wrong

    Every condition below is filed under the part it physically damages. Read down the list and notice that the groups are really a list of the ways this system can fail — and that knowing which group you are in tells you most of what you need before you have a diagnosis.

    Myocardium

    Failure mode: The pump can't generate output

    Acute coronary syndromeSudden cardiac arrestCardiogenic shockCardiac tamponadeCongestive heart failureStimulant toxicity

    The muscular wall of the heart, and the only muscle in the body that contracts continuously from before birth until death without ever resting. The left ventricle is by far the thickest, because it has to push blood through the entire systemic circulation while the right ventricle only has to reach the lungs next door.

    What it does when it works

    • Cardiac output is heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Roughly five litres a minute at rest, and it can climb four or five times that in exercise.
    • Stroke volume depends on three things: preload, which is how full the ventricle is before it contracts; contractility, which is how hard the muscle squeezes; and afterload, which is the pressure it has to push against.
    • The Frank-Starling relationship means that within limits, the more the ventricle is stretched by incoming blood, the harder it contracts. A heart that is filled well pumps well.
    • Myocardium is almost entirely aerobic. It has very little capacity for anaerobic metabolism, and it extracts around seventy percent of the oxygen from the blood passing through it even at rest.

    Why this structure matters

    Because the heart extracts most of the available oxygen even at baseline, it cannot compensate for increased demand by extracting more. Its only option is to increase flow. That single fact explains angina, explains why tachycardia is dangerous in ischemia, and explains why a hypoxic heart deteriorates so quickly.

    In the field

    • The left ventricle fills during diastole, and coronary blood flow to it also happens during diastole. Tachycardia shortens diastole disproportionately, so a fast heart both needs more oxygen and receives less of it.
    • The right and left ventricles fail differently. Left-sided failure backs fluid up into the lungs and causes crackles and breathlessness. Right-sided failure backs fluid up into the body and causes distended neck veins, liver congestion, and swollen legs.
    • Damaged myocardium does not regenerate. What is lost in an infarct is replaced by scar, which conducts poorly and does not contract at all.

    Coronary arteries

    Failure mode: The pump's own blood supply is cut

    Acute coronary syndrome

    Two arteries branching off the aorta immediately above the aortic valve, supplying the heart muscle itself. The left main divides into the left anterior descending and the circumflex; the right coronary artery supplies the right side, the inferior wall in most people, and the SA and AV nodes.

    What it does when it works

    • They fill during diastole, when the heart is relaxed. During systole the contracting muscle squeezes them nearly shut, so the heart can only feed itself between beats.
    • They are functional end arteries. Collateral circulation exists but is usually inadequate to rescue tissue acutely.
    • Flow is regulated by local metabolic demand, so a working region of myocardium can recruit more supply, up to the limit the vessel allows.

    Why this structure matters

    Every acute coronary syndrome is a supply and demand problem in these vessels. Stable angina is demand outrunning a fixed narrowed supply, and it resolves with rest. An infarct is supply abruptly cut by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque, and it does not resolve with rest. The distinction is entirely about whether the obstruction is fixed and partial or sudden and complete.

    In the field

    • Which artery is blocked determines the presentation. Right coronary occlusions cause inferior infarcts, which often involve the right ventricle and are preload dependent, which is exactly why nitroglycerin can drop the pressure through the floor in those patients.
    • The right coronary artery also supplies the conduction nodes in most people, so inferior infarcts commonly come with bradycardia and heart block.
    • Time is muscle. Roughly speaking, myocardium starts dying within twenty to thirty minutes of complete occlusion and infarction is largely complete by six hours. Everything you do prehospital is about shortening that clock.

    Conduction system

    Failure mode: Electrical timing breaks down

    Sudden cardiac arrestCardiogenic shockElectrical injury

    Specialised cardiac cells that generate and conduct the electrical impulse: the sinoatrial node in the right atrium, the atrioventricular node at the junction, the bundle of His, the left and right bundle branches, and the Purkinje fibres spreading through the ventricular walls.

    What it does when it works

    • The SA node fires at 60 to 100 per minute and sets the rate because it is the fastest pacemaker present.
    • The AV node deliberately delays the impulse by about a tenth of a second, which lets the atria finish emptying into the ventricles before the ventricles contract. That delay is where the atrial kick comes from.
    • Backup pacemakers exist further down. The AV junction fires at 40 to 60, the ventricles at 20 to 40. Slower, and progressively less effective.
    • Electrical activity precedes mechanical activity. A rhythm on the monitor is a statement about electricity, not about output.

    Why this structure matters

    Rate and rhythm both matter, and for different reasons. Too slow and cardiac output falls because there are not enough beats. Too fast and output also falls, because diastole shortens so much the ventricles never fill properly. And if the atria stop contributing their kick, as in atrial fibrillation, output drops by roughly a fifth in a patient who may not have a fifth to spare.

    In the field

    • Pulseless electrical activity is this system working perfectly while the pump produces nothing. The electricity is not the problem, so shocking it will not help. Look for the mechanical cause.
    • Ventricular fibrillation is this system in complete disorder, with no coordinated contraction at all. It is electrical, and defibrillation is the answer.
    • Potassium, calcium, magnesium, hypoxia, acidosis, and hypothermia all disturb conduction. A rhythm problem is often a chemistry problem wearing a costume.

    Heart valves

    Failure mode: Blood goes the wrong way or can't get out

    Congestive heart failure

    Four one-way valves. The tricuspid and mitral valves sit between atria and ventricles and are tethered by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles. The pulmonic and aortic valves sit at the ventricular outflows.

    What it does when it works

    • They open and close passively, driven purely by the pressure difference across them. There is no active mechanism.
    • The chordae stop the AV valves from being blown backwards into the atria when ventricular pressure spikes.
    • Valve closure produces the heart sounds. The first sound is the AV valves shutting at the start of systole; the second is the outflow valves shutting at the end of it.

    Why this structure matters

    A leaking valve means part of every stroke volume goes backwards, so the heart does full work for partial output. A narrowed valve means the ventricle has to generate far higher pressure to push the same volume through, which raises oxygen demand enormously. Both eventually cause the ventricle behind them to fail.

    In the field

    • Aortic stenosis is the one to remember. These patients have a fixed obstruction to outflow and depend on preload and a high pressure gradient. Nitroglycerin drops their preload and can cause abrupt collapse.
    • A papillary muscle can rupture during an inferior infarct, converting a competent mitral valve into a catastrophically leaking one in a single beat. Sudden severe pulmonary edema in a patient with chest pain should raise it.

    Pericardium

    Failure mode: The heart can't fill

    Cardiac tamponade

    A tough fibrous sac around the heart, lined by a serous membrane, containing about fifteen to fifty millilitres of lubricating fluid. Like the pleural space, it is a sealed potential space rather than an actual cavity.

    What it does when it works

    • Anchors the heart within the mediastinum and reduces friction as it beats.
    • Limits acute overdistension of the chambers.
    • The fibrous layer is stiff and does not stretch quickly, though it will slowly accommodate large volumes over weeks or months.

    Why this structure matters

    Because the sac does not stretch acutely, rate of accumulation matters far more than volume. As little as 100 to 150 millilitres of blood arriving quickly can cause tamponade, while a slowly growing chronic effusion can reach a litre or more with the patient still walking around. This is the same lesson as the pleural space, in a smaller container.

    In the field

    • Tamponade compresses the thin-walled right side of the heart first, because the right atrium and ventricle have the lowest pressures and collapse most easily.
    • Beck's triad is hypotension, distended neck veins, and muffled heart sounds. It is classic, taught everywhere, and frequently incomplete in real patients. Do not require all three.

    Arteries and arterioles

    Failure mode: Pressure can't be maintained, or the route is blocked

    External hemorrhageSeptic shockNeurogenic shockTraumatic amputationAnaphylaxis

    Thick-walled, muscular, elastic vessels carrying blood away from the heart. Large arteries act as elastic reservoirs that smooth pulsatile flow; arterioles are the small muscular vessels that actually control where blood goes.

    What it does when it works

    • Arterioles are the resistance vessels. Their smooth muscle tone determines systemic vascular resistance, and therefore blood pressure, and therefore which tissues get flow.
    • Blood pressure is cardiac output multiplied by systemic vascular resistance. Only two variables, which is why every form of shock is a failure of one, the other, or both.
    • Sympathetic stimulation constricts arterioles in skin, gut, and muscle to preserve flow to brain and heart. That redistribution is what makes a shocked patient pale, cool, and clammy.
    • Elastic recoil in the aorta during diastole maintains diastolic pressure, which is what actually perfuses the coronary arteries.

    Why this structure matters

    Blood pressure is not the same as perfusion, and this is the single most misunderstood point in shock. A patient can maintain a completely normal blood pressure by clamping down their arterioles while their tissues are already starving. Compensated shock is defined by a normal pressure hiding an abnormal state, and the blood pressure only falls once compensation has been exhausted.

    In the field

    • Narrowing pulse pressure is an early sign of hypovolemia: the diastolic rises from vasoconstriction while systolic starts to fall from lost volume.
    • In a healthy adult, blood pressure often does not fall until roughly thirty percent of blood volume is gone. By the time it drops, the patient is in serious trouble.
    • Children compensate even harder and hold their pressure even longer, then crash without much warning. Never be reassured by a normal blood pressure in an injured child.

    Capillaries

    Failure mode: Exchange fails, or fluid leaks out

    Hemorrhagic and hypovolemic shockSeptic shockThe deteriorating childAnaphylaxis

    Vessels one cell thick, roughly the diameter of a single red blood cell, forming networks through every tissue. This is the only place in the entire circulation where anything is actually exchanged. Everything else is plumbing.

    What it does when it works

    • Oxygen, carbon dioxide, glucose, and waste diffuse across the wall down their concentration gradients.
    • Hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out at the arterial end; oncotic pressure from plasma proteins pulls it back in at the venous end. The balance normally leaves a small net outflow that the lymphatics collect.
    • Precapillary sphincters open and close beds according to local demand, so only a fraction of capillaries are perfused at any given moment.

    Why this structure matters

    This is where the entire point of the circulatory system actually happens, and it is where shock actually kills. Blood pressure, heart rate, and cardiac output are all just proxies for whether these vessels are delivering. Capillary refill is the one bedside test that looks directly at this level rather than at a proxy.

    In the field

    • In septic shock the capillaries become leaky and dilated, so fluid pours into the tissues while the vessels themselves stay wide open. The patient can be simultaneously fluid overloaded and intravascularly empty.
    • Prolonged capillary refill beyond two seconds is a genuinely useful early sign in children, and much less reliable in cold adults or cold environments.
    • Anaphylaxis causes massive capillary leak. That is where the circulating volume goes, and it is why these patients are profoundly hypotensive without losing a drop of blood.

    Veins

    Failure mode: Blood can't get back to the heart

    External hemorrhageHemorrhagic and hypovolemic shockNeurogenic shock

    Thin-walled, low-pressure, highly compliant vessels returning blood to the heart. They hold roughly sixty to seventy percent of total blood volume at any moment, which makes them the body's blood reservoir rather than just a return path.

    What it does when it works

    • Venous tone is under sympathetic control. Constricting the veins squeezes stored blood back into circulation, effectively giving an internal transfusion of up to about a litre.
    • One-way valves in the limbs prevent backflow, and skeletal muscle contraction pumps blood upward against gravity.
    • Negative intrathoracic pressure during inspiration actively draws blood back toward the chest.

    Why this structure matters

    Preload is venous return, and stroke volume depends on preload. Anything that reduces venous return reduces cardiac output immediately, no matter how strong the heart is. This is why tension pneumothorax, tamponade, and positive pressure ventilation can all drop a blood pressure without the heart being damaged at all.

    In the field

    • Distended neck veins mean blood is backing up behind an obstruction: tension pneumothorax, tamponade, or right heart failure. Flat neck veins in a shocked patient point to volume loss instead.
    • Nitroglycerin works mainly here. It dilates veins, blood pools in the periphery, preload falls, and the heart has less work to do. That is also exactly why it is dangerous in a preload-dependent patient.
    • Aggressive bagging raises intrathoracic pressure and impedes venous return. Overventilating a hypotensive patient makes them more hypotensive.

    Blood

    Failure mode: Nothing there to carry the oxygen

    Gastrointestinal bleedingExternal hemorrhageHemorrhagic and hypovolemic shockHemothoraxSmoke inhalationCarbon monoxide poisoning

    About five litres in an adult, roughly seven percent of body weight. Slightly over half is plasma, mostly water with proteins and electrolytes; the rest is cells, overwhelmingly red cells.

    What it does when it works

    • Hemoglobin carries almost all the oxygen. Only about one and a half percent travels dissolved in plasma, which is why oxygen content depends far more on hemoglobin than on the partial pressure.
    • Each gram of hemoglobin carries roughly 1.34 millilitres of oxygen when fully saturated.
    • Carbon dioxide travels mostly as bicarbonate, which also makes blood the body's main acid-base buffer.
    • Platelets and clotting factors seal breaches; white cells handle infection.

    Why this structure matters

    Oxygen delivery is hemoglobin multiplied by saturation multiplied by cardiac output. Notice that saturation is only one of three terms. A patient can have a perfect SpO2 of one hundred percent and still be dying of oxygen starvation because they have lost half their hemoglobin or half their cardiac output. The pulse oximeter measures the least important variable of the three.

    In the field

    • Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin roughly two hundred and fifty times more avidly than oxygen. The oximeter cannot tell the difference and will read reassuringly high while the patient suffocates.
    • Bleeding loses whole blood, so it loses hemoglobin and volume together. Replacing volume alone dilutes what carrying capacity is left.
    • Anemic patients desaturate their tissues long before they turn blue, because cyanosis needs an absolute quantity of desaturated hemoglobin they simply do not have.
