    Skin and temperature

    Barrier, thermoregulation, fluid containment.

    Where things go wrong

    Every condition below is filed under the part it physically damages. Read down the list and notice that the groups are really a list of the ways this system can fail — and that knowing which group you are in tells you most of what you need before you have a diagnosis.

    Skin as a barrier

    Failure mode: The seal is breached

    Thermal burns

    The largest organ in the body, in three layers. The epidermis is a thin outer sheet of dead keratinised cells, the dermis beneath it holds vessels, nerves, and glands, and below that lies subcutaneous fat.

    What it does when it works

    • Keeps water in and organisms out. The dead outer layer is the actual waterproof barrier.
    • The dermis carries the sensory nerve endings, which is why partial thickness burns are agonising and full thickness burns are numb.
    • Sweat glands and hair follicles reach down into the dermis, and skin regenerates from them after a partial thickness injury.

    Why this structure matters

    Breaching the barrier causes two problems at once: fluid pours out, and organisms get in. In a large burn, plasma loss through the damaged surface and into the surrounding tissue is enough to cause hypovolemic shock over hours. Depth matters because it determines whether the structures that regenerate skin still exist.

    In the field

    • Superficial burns are red, painful, and blanch. Partial thickness are blistered, wet, and extremely painful. Full thickness are dry, leathery, white or charred, and painless because the nerves are destroyed.
    • A painless burn is worse than a painful one, which is the opposite of the intuition.
    • Circumferential burns act like a tourniquet as the tissue swells, and around the chest they restrict ventilation.

    Temperature regulation

    Failure mode: Core temperature drifts outside the survivable range

    HypothermiaHeat exhaustion and heat stroke

    A control system with a set point in the hypothalamus, sensors in the skin and core, and effectors that are mostly skin blood vessels, sweat glands, and skeletal muscle.

    What it does when it works

    • Core temperature is held near 37 degrees within a fraction of a degree.
    • To lose heat: vasodilation brings blood to the surface, and sweating removes heat as the water evaporates.
    • To conserve heat: vasoconstriction shunts blood away from the surface, and shivering generates heat by muscular work.
    • Heat is lost by radiation, conduction, convection, and evaporation. Wet skin and wind massively accelerate all of them.

    Why this structure matters

    Enzymes work within a narrow temperature range. Below about 35 degrees, function progressively fails: clotting slows, the heart becomes irritable, and consciousness falls. Above about 40, proteins begin to denature and cells die. Both extremes eventually disable the very mechanisms that were meant to correct them, which is why severe cases do not self-rescue.

    In the field

    • Shivering stops below roughly 32 degrees. A cold patient who has stopped shivering is worse, not better.
    • Sweating stops in classic heat stroke, so hot dry skin in a collapsed patient is an emergency.
    • Trauma patients become hypothermic quickly even in mild weather, and hypothermia impairs clotting directly. Cutting clothes off and leaving them exposed contributes to their death.
    • A severely hypothermic heart is extremely irritable and rough handling can precipitate ventricular fibrillation.

    Soft tissue and wounds

    Failure mode: Tissue is torn, crushed, or devitalised

    Thermal burnsElectrical injuryTraumatic amputation

    Skin, fat, fascia, and muscle considered together as the tissue that gets injured in most trauma. Different mechanisms damage it in characteristically different ways.

    What it does when it works

    • Bleeding is stopped by vessel constriction, then a platelet plug, then a fibrin clot.
    • Inflammation clears debris and organisms, then new tissue is laid down and remodelled over weeks.
    • Healing requires blood supply, so devitalised or heavily contaminated tissue does not heal.

    Why this structure matters

    The mechanism predicts the problem. Clean incisions bleed but heal well. Crush injuries devitalise tissue and release its contents into the circulation. Degloving separates skin from its blood supply so it dies even though it looks intact. Puncture wounds seal over and trap contamination underneath.

    In the field

    • An impaled object should be stabilised in place, not removed. It may be the only thing preventing catastrophic bleeding.
    • Amputated parts go in a dry sealed bag, then on ice. Never directly on ice and never in water, both of which destroy the tissue.
    • Wounds over joints, on the hands, or on the face have functional consequences out of proportion to their size.
    • Anything that bit or punctured deeply carries an infection and tetanus question that changes the destination.
