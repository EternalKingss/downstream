    Endocrine and metabolic

    Keep fuel, fluid, and chemistry inside survivable limits.

    Where things go wrong

    Every condition below is filed under the part it physically damages. Read down the list and notice that the groups are really a list of the ways this system can fail — and that knowing which group you are in tells you most of what you need before you have a diagnosis.

    Pancreas

    Failure mode: Blood sugar control is lost

    HypoglycemiaDiabetic ketoacidosisHyperosmolar hyperglycemic state

    A gland lying behind the stomach with two entirely separate jobs. The bulk of it makes digestive enzymes that drain into the duodenum. Scattered through it are the islets of Langerhans, clusters of hormone-producing cells that release insulin and glucagon straight into the bloodstream.

    What it does when it works

    • Beta cells release insulin when blood glucose rises. Insulin unlocks glucose entry into muscle and fat, and tells the liver to store rather than release.
    • Alpha cells release glucagon when glucose falls, telling the liver to break down glycogen and manufacture new glucose.
    • The two hormones oppose each other continuously, holding blood glucose in a remarkably narrow band around 4 to 7 mmol/L.
    • The digestive portion produces enzymes powerful enough to digest the pancreas itself, which is why they are secreted in an inactive form.

    Why this structure matters

    Insulin is the only hormone that lowers blood glucose, while several raise it. That asymmetry means the body is far better defended against low sugar than high, and it is why type 1 diabetes was uniformly fatal before insulin existed. Understanding that insulin also switches off fat breakdown explains ketoacidosis entirely.

    In the field

    • Without insulin, cells cannot take up glucose even when the blood is full of it. The patient is starving in the middle of plenty, which is why they break down fat and produce ketones.
    • Those digestive enzymes activating inside the gland is acute pancreatitis: the organ digests itself, causing severe pain and massive fluid shifts.
    • Islet cells are a tiny fraction of the gland's mass but their failure is what kills.

    Adrenal glands

    Failure mode: The stress response fails

    Adrenal crisis

    Two small glands capping the kidneys, each with an outer cortex and inner medulla that function as separate organs. The cortex makes steroid hormones; the medulla is effectively a modified sympathetic ganglion that dumps adrenaline directly into the blood.

    What it does when it works

    • The cortex produces cortisol, which maintains blood pressure, mobilises glucose, and modulates inflammation, and aldosterone, which retains sodium and water and excretes potassium.
    • The medulla releases adrenaline and noradrenaline into the circulation during sympathetic activation, producing the body-wide fight-or-flight response.
    • Cortisol output rises several-fold under physical stress such as infection, trauma, or surgery.

    Why this structure matters

    Cortisol is permissive: without it, blood vessels stop responding properly to catecholamines. A patient in adrenal crisis is hypotensive and does not respond to the usual compensations, because the hormone that lets those compensations work is missing.

    In the field

    • Anyone on long-term steroids has a suppressed adrenal response and cannot mount a stress response. Illness, injury, or abruptly stopping the steroid can precipitate a crisis.
    • Adrenal crisis presents as shock with hypotension, vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, and often low sodium and high potassium.
    • Ask about steroid use in any unexplained shock. Many patients carry a steroid card or emergency injection.

    Thyroid gland

    Failure mode: Metabolic rate is set wrong

    Nothing mapped yet

    A butterfly-shaped gland across the front of the trachea, just below the larynx. It produces thyroid hormone, which sets the baseline metabolic rate of essentially every cell in the body.

    What it does when it works

    • Thyroid hormone determines how fast cells consume oxygen and produce heat.
    • It increases the number and sensitivity of beta-adrenergic receptors, which is why thyroid excess looks so much like sympathetic overdrive.
    • Output is regulated by TSH from the pituitary in a feedback loop, and turnover is slow, so changes develop over weeks rather than minutes.

    Why this structure matters

    Because it sets the gain on the whole metabolic system, extremes at either end are life-threatening in opposite directions. Thyroid storm produces fever, tachyarrhythmia, agitation, and cardiovascular collapse. Myxoedema coma produces hypothermia, bradycardia, hypoventilation, and unresponsiveness.

    In the field

    • Thyroid storm is often triggered by infection, trauma, or surgery in someone with untreated overactive thyroid, and it looks like severe sepsis with a very high temperature.
    • Myxoedema coma appears mostly in older women in winter, and the hypothermia does not respond to warming alone.
    • A large goitre can compress the trachea and cause airway compromise when the neck is extended.

    Body fluid and electrolytes

    Failure mode: Cells swell or shrivel, and nerves misfire

    Diabetic ketoacidosisHyperosmolar hyperglycemic stateCrush injury and crush syndromeAlcohol intoxication and withdrawal

    Water makes up roughly sixty percent of an adult, split between fluid inside cells and fluid outside them. The extracellular portion divides again into plasma inside vessels and interstitial fluid bathing the tissues. Dissolved electrolytes set how water distributes between these spaces.

    What it does when it works

    • Sodium is the dominant extracellular ion and is what holds water in the extracellular space. Potassium is the dominant intracellular ion.
    • The difference across the membrane, maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, is what makes nerves and muscle electrically excitable at all.
    • Water moves passively by osmosis toward whichever side has more dissolved solute.
    • The kidneys control the whole system by adjusting what is excreted and what is retained.

    Why this structure matters

    Every nerve impulse and every heartbeat depends on ion gradients across membranes. Derange the electrolytes and the electrical behaviour of the heart and brain changes directly. Potassium is the one that kills fastest: both high and low levels cause lethal arrhythmias, and the therapeutic window is narrow.

    In the field

    • Crush injury, burns, and renal failure all release or retain potassium, which is why those patients arrest suddenly.
    • Sodium changes act on the brain. Falling sodium pulls water into brain cells, causing confusion and seizures, and it is a common cause of seizure in older patients on diuretics.
    • Correcting sodium too quickly is itself dangerous, which is why these patients are managed slowly in hospital rather than fixed in the field.
