    Nervous system

    Sense, decide, and drive everything else.

    Where things go wrong

    Every condition below is filed under the part it physically damages. Read down the list and notice that the groups are really a list of the ways this system can fail — and that knowing which group you are in tells you most of what you need before you have a diagnosis.

    Cerebrum

    Failure mode: Thinking, speech, and movement break

    HypoglycemiaIschemic strokeSeizure and status epilepticusTraumatic brain injuryBehavioural emergency and severe agitationAlcohol intoxication and withdrawal

    The two large hemispheres that make up most of the brain. The outer cortex handles conscious thought, voluntary movement, sensation, language, and personality. Deeper structures handle memory, emotion, and the relay of nearly all sensory traffic.

    What it does when it works

    • The frontal lobes handle judgement, planning, personality, and voluntary movement, with the motor strip running across the back of the frontal lobe.
    • The parietal lobes handle sensation and spatial awareness; the temporal lobes handle hearing, memory, and comprehension of language; the occipital lobes handle vision.
    • Motor and sensory pathways cross over, so the left hemisphere controls the right side of the body and vice versa.
    • In the great majority of people, including most left-handers, language lives in the left hemisphere.

    Why this structure matters

    Because function is mapped to location, the deficit tells you where the lesion is. Right-sided weakness with speech difficulty means the left hemisphere. Left-sided weakness with neglect, where the patient seems unaware of their own left side, means the right. That is not academic: it is how you describe a stroke usefully to the receiving team.

    In the field

    • The cerebrum can be substantially damaged without the patient dying, because breathing and circulation are controlled lower down. A patient can be devastated neurologically and completely stable physiologically.
    • It is also the most metabolically demanding tissue in the body, consuming around twenty percent of total oxygen while making up two percent of body mass, with essentially no stored reserve.
    • Right hemisphere strokes are missed more often, because the patient can still speak fluently and the deficit is easier to overlook.

    Brainstem

    Failure mode: Automatic functions stop

    Traumatic brain injuryOpioid overdose

    The midbrain, pons, and medulla, forming the stalk connecting the cerebrum to the spinal cord. Physically small, roughly the size of a thumb, and it runs everything that keeps you alive without your involvement.

    What it does when it works

    • Contains the respiratory centres that set the rate and depth of breathing, driven primarily by carbon dioxide levels rather than oxygen.
    • Contains the cardiovascular centres controlling heart rate and vascular tone.
    • Houses the reticular activating system, which maintains consciousness and wakefulness.
    • Every signal travelling between the brain and the body passes through it, and ten of the twelve cranial nerves originate here.

    Why this structure matters

    This is the smallest amount of tissue whose loss is immediately fatal. Damage here stops breathing, and it is also where rising intracranial pressure eventually squeezes the brain against the skull opening at the base. Almost every neurological death is ultimately a brainstem death, whatever started the process higher up.

    In the field

    • Opioids act primarily on the brainstem respiratory centre. They do not touch the lungs at all, which is precisely why naloxone reverses the problem completely.
    • The pattern of breathing tells you about the brainstem. Irregular, deep and sighing, or alternating deep and shallow breathing in a head-injured patient are ominous.
    • Pupil responses are a brainstem function. A unilaterally blown pupil in a head injury suggests the temporal lobe is being pushed down against the third cranial nerve, which is a herniation emergency.

    Cerebellum

    Failure mode: Coordination and balance are lost

    Nothing mapped yet

    The dense, tightly folded structure sitting behind the brainstem beneath the cerebrum. It contains more neurons than the rest of the brain combined, all devoted to making movement smooth and accurate.

    What it does when it works

    • Compares the movement you intended with the movement actually occurring and corrects the difference continuously.
    • Maintains balance and posture using input from the inner ear, from vision, and from position sensors in muscles and joints.
    • Coordinates the timing of muscle groups so that movement is smooth rather than jerky.
    • It does not initiate movement or generate strength. It refines both.

    Why this structure matters

    Cerebellar problems produce a patient who is not weak and not confused, but cannot control their movement. The most important field consequence is that they can look exactly like intoxication: staggering gait, slurred speech, poor coordination, and abnormal eye movements. Cerebellar strokes get dismissed as drunkenness with fatal regularity.

    In the field

    • Sudden vertigo, vomiting, and inability to walk, in someone who is otherwise alert, should raise cerebellar stroke rather than an inner ear problem.
    • The posterior fossa where it sits is a small, rigid compartment. Swelling here has nowhere to go and compresses the brainstem quickly, so a cerebellar bleed deteriorates faster than its size suggests.
    • Alcohol affects the cerebellum early and strongly, which is why field sobriety tests are essentially cerebellar function tests.

    Meninges and cerebrospinal fluid

    Failure mode: Pressure builds with nowhere to go

    Hemorrhagic strokeTraumatic brain injuryMeningitis and meningococcal sepsis

    Three membranes wrapping the brain and cord, plus the fluid between them. From the outside in: the tough dura mater, the web-like arachnoid, and the delicate pia mater stuck to the brain surface. Cerebrospinal fluid circulates in the subarachnoid space and through the ventricles.

    What it does when it works

    • About 150 millilitres of cerebrospinal fluid is present at any time, and roughly 500 millilitres is produced and reabsorbed daily.
    • The fluid cushions the brain, effectively reducing its working weight from around 1,400 grams to under 50, and removes metabolic waste.
    • Bridging veins cross the subdural space; arteries including the middle meningeal run in the epidural space outside the dura.
    • The skull is a closed rigid box containing three things: brain, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid. That is the Monro-Kellie doctrine, and it governs everything that follows.

    Why this structure matters

    Because the box is rigid and full, adding anything means displacing something else. A growing hematoma is initially compensated by squeezing out cerebrospinal fluid and venous blood, which is why a patient can have a lucid interval and appear fine. Once that compensation is exhausted, pressure rises very steeply for very small additional volume, and deterioration becomes sudden.

    In the field

    • Epidural bleeds are arterial, fast, and classically show a lucid interval followed by rapid collapse. Subdural bleeds are venous, slower, and common in older adults and heavy drinkers whose brains have shrunk and stretched the bridging veins.
    • Clear or blood-tinged fluid from the nose or ears after head trauma suggests a basilar skull fracture with a cerebrospinal fluid leak. Do not pack it; let it drain.
    • Infection of these membranes is meningitis, and because the space is continuous around the brain and cord, it spreads throughout the entire central nervous system.

    Cerebral blood vessels

    Failure mode: Brain tissue loses its supply, or bleeds

    Ischemic strokeHemorrhagic strokeSyncopePre-eclampsia and eclampsia

    Four arteries supply the brain: two carotids in front and two vertebral arteries behind. They join at the circle of Willis at the base of the brain, which then distributes flow through the anterior, middle, and posterior cerebral arteries.

    What it does when it works

    • The brain receives about fifteen percent of cardiac output, roughly 750 millilitres a minute, continuously and without interruption.
    • Autoregulation keeps cerebral flow constant across a wide range of blood pressures, roughly a mean arterial pressure of 60 to 150.
    • Cerebral perfusion pressure is mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. Both terms matter.
    • The circle of Willis provides some collateral supply, though it is complete and functional in fewer people than textbooks imply.

    Why this structure matters

    Neurons begin dying within minutes of losing flow, and roughly two million die every minute during a large vessel stroke. There is usually a surrounding zone of tissue that is starved but not yet dead, kept marginally alive by collateral flow. That zone is what treatment saves, and it shrinks with every minute.

    In the field

    • Middle cerebral artery strokes are the most common and produce the classic picture of face, arm, and speech involvement.
    • Posterior circulation strokes present with dizziness, double vision, ataxia, and vomiting rather than one-sided weakness, and they are missed far more often.
    • Because autoregulation fails below a mean arterial pressure of about 60, hypotension is devastating to an already injured brain. A single hypotensive episode measurably worsens outcomes in traumatic brain injury.

    Spinal cord

    Failure mode: Signals can't get past the injury

    Spinal cord injury

    A bundle of nerve tissue about as thick as a finger running from the brainstem down inside the vertebral column, ending around the first or second lumbar vertebra. Below that it continues as a bundle of nerve roots called the cauda equina.

    What it does when it works

    • Motor tracts descend from the brain to the muscles; sensory tracts ascend from the body to the brain.
    • Reflex arcs operate entirely within the cord, without involving the brain at all, which is why a spinal patient can have reflexes below an injury that has cut them off from the brain.
    • The phrenic nerve arises from C3, C4, and C5 and drives the diaphragm.
    • Sympathetic outflow leaves the cord between roughly T1 and L2, while parasympathetic outflow comes from the cranial nerves and the sacral segments.

    Why this structure matters

    The cord is an information cable running through a bony tube, and the level of injury determines exactly what is lost, because everything below is disconnected. Central nervous tissue does not regenerate meaningfully, so what is lost tends to stay lost, which is why preventing further injury during extrication and handling matters so much.

    In the field

    • C3, 4, and 5 keep the diaphragm alive. An injury above that stops spontaneous breathing entirely. Injury in the lower cervical or thoracic cord spares the diaphragm but takes out the intercostals, producing shallow diaphragmatic breathing that fatigues.
    • Injury above roughly T6 cuts sympathetic outflow and causes neurogenic shock: warm, dry, pink skin with hypotension and bradycardia.
    • Much of the final damage is secondary rather than primary, caused by swelling, bleeding, hypoxia, and hypotension in the hours after the injury. Keeping the pressure and the saturation up is genuinely neuroprotective.

    Peripheral nerves

    Failure mode: The signal doesn't reach the muscle

    Neuromuscular respiratory failureCholinergic toxicity

    All the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord: the roots leaving the cord, the plexuses where they reorganise, and the named nerves running to skin and muscle. Motor fibres carry instructions out, sensory fibres carry information in.

    What it does when it works

    • Each spinal nerve root supplies a predictable strip of skin, called a dermatome, and a predictable group of muscles, called a myotome. That mapping is consistent enough to localise an injury.
    • Signals travel as electrical impulses along the axon, then cross to muscle chemically at the neuromuscular junction using acetylcholine.
    • Unlike central nervous tissue, peripheral nerves can regenerate, though slowly, at roughly a millimetre a day.

    Why this structure matters

    Peripheral nerve findings localise injuries with real precision. Numbness in a specific dermatome or weakness in a specific movement tells you which root or nerve is involved, and it distinguishes a peripheral problem from a central one. It also tells you when a fracture or dislocation has damaged something beyond bone.

    In the field

    • Always check sensation and movement distal to any fracture or dislocation, before and after any manipulation or splinting, and document both.
    • Anything that blocks the neuromuscular junction causes paralysis with a completely intact brain. Organophosphate poisoning and nerve agents work here, flooding the junction with acetylcholine and causing weakness alongside massive secretions.
    • Ascending weakness starting in the feet and moving upward, as in Guillain-Barré, threatens the diaphragm and is a respiratory emergency even though the patient may look calm.

    Autonomic nervous system

    Failure mode: Automatic control is lost or overwhelmed

    SyncopeBehavioural emergency and severe agitationStimulant toxicityCholinergic toxicity

    The part of the nervous system running organs without conscious involvement, split into two opposing branches: sympathetic, which prepares for exertion, and parasympathetic, which handles rest and digestion.

    What it does when it works

    • Sympathetic activation raises heart rate and contractility, constricts vessels in skin and gut, dilates bronchioles and pupils, mobilises glucose, and triggers sweating. Its main messengers are noradrenaline and adrenaline.
    • Parasympathetic activation, travelling largely in the vagus nerve, slows the heart, constricts pupils and bronchioles, and stimulates digestion and secretions. Its messenger is acetylcholine.
    • The two are in constant balance, and the resting state is not neutral: there is continuous background tone from both.
    • Adrenergic receptors come in subtypes that matter enormously for pharmacology. Alpha-1 constricts vessels, beta-1 speeds and strengthens the heart, beta-2 relaxes bronchial and vascular smooth muscle.

    Why this structure matters

    Almost every drug an EMR or paramedic gives works on this system, and knowing the receptor subtypes turns drug effects from a memorised list into something you can derive. Salbutamol is beta-2, so it opens airways and, when it spills onto beta-1, causes tachycardia. Epinephrine hits all of them at once, which is why it works in anaphylaxis. Nothing about that has to be memorised if you know the receptors.

    In the field

    • The classic signs of shock are simply the sympathetic response made visible: tachycardia, pale cool clammy skin, dilated pupils, and anxiety.
    • Cholinergic poisoning from organophosphates or nerve agents produces massive parasympathetic overactivity: salivation, tearing, urination, defecation, vomiting, tiny pupils, and bronchial secretions that drown the patient.
    • Vagal stimulation from straining, vomiting, or pain can drop the heart rate and blood pressure sharply enough to cause syncope.
