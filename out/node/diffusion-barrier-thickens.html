    The diffusion barrier thickens

    Gas has further to travel between air and blood, so less of it makes the trip.

    What is actually happening

    The alveolar-capillary membrane is normally around half a micron thick, thinner than a red blood cell is wide. Fluid, pus, inflammatory cells, or fibrous tissue between the alveolus and the capillary lengthen that distance, and the rate of diffusion falls accordingly.

    Why it follows from almost anything

    It follows pulmonary edema, where plasma is forced across from the capillary side; pneumonia, where infection fills the alveolus from the air side; inflammation from inhaled toxins or drowning; and chronic fibrosis.

    What you see

    • Hypoxia, usually well before any rise in carbon dioxide
    • Crackles as fluid-filled or collapsed alveoli pop open with inspiration
    • Increasing work of breathing as the lung becomes stiffer and harder to inflate
    • Saturation that improves with oxygen, unlike a true shunt, though often not to normal

    The trap

    Carbon dioxide diffuses roughly twenty times more readily than oxygen, so in a thickening barrier the patient becomes hypoxic long before carbon dioxide starts to rise, and often has a low carbon dioxide from breathing fast. A rising carbon dioxide in this setting means the patient is tiring rather than that the barrier suddenly got worse, and it is a much later and more serious sign.
