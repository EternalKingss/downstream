    Skill

    Defibrillation

    The only intervention that reverses ventricular fibrillation, and every minute of delay costs survival.

    EMR

    Why it works

    In ventricular fibrillation, myocardial cells are depolarising chaotically and independently, so the chamber quivers instead of contracting. A shock depolarises all of them simultaneously, producing a brief moment of complete electrical silence from which the fastest natural pacemaker, normally the sinoatrial node, can resume coordinated control. You are not restarting the heart; you are stopping the chaos so it can restart itself.

    How to do it, and why each step exists

    Every step below has a reason. If you know the reason, you can adapt when the situation does not match the textbook.

    Get pads on as early as possible

    In a patient with chest pain, put pads on before they arrest, not after. The likeliest thing to kill an infarct patient in the first hour is a shockable rhythm.

    Correct pad placement, right upper chest and left lower ribs

    The current must pass through the ventricular mass. Placing both pads too close together sends current across the surface rather than through the heart.

    Dry the chest and shave heavy hair if needed

    Sweat conducts current across the skin between pads rather than through the chest, and hair prevents contact. Both waste the shock.

    Continue compressions while the machine charges

    Charging takes several seconds and there is no reason to stop for it. Every second of compression maintains the perfusion pressure that makes the shock more likely to work.

    Clear briefly, shock, and resume immediately

    Resume compressions the instant the shock is delivered. Do not pause to check for a pulse: the heart rarely produces output immediately after defibrillation, and the pause costs perfusion pressure you have to rebuild.

    Remove medication patches and avoid pacemakers

    Patches can arc and burn. Place pads at least 8 cm from an implanted device, which is usually visible as a lump under the skin below the collarbone.

    Common errors

    • Delaying pad placement until after arrest in a high-risk patient
    • Long pauses before and after the shock
    • Checking a pulse straight after a shock instead of resuming compressions
    • Pads too close together, or over a wet or hairy chest
    • Shocking asystole or PEA, which is not an electrical problem and will not respond
    • Not drying a patient pulled from water before applying pads
