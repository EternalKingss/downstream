    Skill

    Opening the airway

    The highest-value five seconds in prehospital medicine, and it costs nothing.

    EMR

    Why it works

    Consciousness maintains the muscle tone that holds the tongue forward off the posterior pharyngeal wall. Lose consciousness and the tongue falls back, and the airway closes. No drug reverses that. Lifting the jaw forward physically pulls the tongue away from the wall, because the tongue is attached to the mandible.

    How to do it, and why each step exists

    Every step below has a reason. If you know the reason, you can adapt when the situation does not match the textbook.

    Look and listen from the head first

    Snoring means partial obstruction by the tongue. Gurgling means fluid and needs suction before anything else. Silence in a patient who should be breathing means complete obstruction.

    Head-tilt chin-lift for a non-trauma patient

    Tilting the head extends the neck and lifting the chin moves the mandible forward, taking the tongue with it. Two movements, one result.

    Jaw thrust if spinal injury is suspected

    Achieves the same mandibular displacement without extending the cervical spine. Note that a patent airway always outranks spinal precaution: if a jaw thrust is not working, use head-tilt chin-lift.

    Neutral position with shoulder padding in infants

    The infant occiput is proportionally large, so lying supine on a flat surface flexes the neck and closes the airway. Padding under the shoulders restores neutral. Extending an infant's neck like an adult's also closes it.

    Suction anything you can see, under direct vision

    Blind suctioning pushes material deeper and can trigger vagal bradycardia or laryngospasm. Suction on the way out, and limit each attempt.

    Reassess after every move

    Airways that were open close again when the patient is moved, rolled, or loaded. Position is not a one-time task.

    Common errors

    • Placing an oropharyngeal airway in a patient who still has a gag reflex, causing vomiting into an unprotected airway
    • Over-extending an infant's neck, which closes the airway rather than opening it
    • Blind finger sweeps, which push obstructions deeper
    • Assuming the airway stays open after you move the patient
    • Prioritising a collar over a patent airway

    Adjuncts

    Oropharyngeal airway

    Sized from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the jaw. Only for a patient with no gag reflex, because it will cause vomiting in anyone with one. It holds the tongue off the pharyngeal wall but does not replace correct positioning.

    Nasopharyngeal airway

    Sized from the nostril to the earlobe. Tolerated with an intact gag reflex, which makes it useful in seizure, intoxication, and clenched jaws. Avoid in suspected basilar skull fracture.

    Where you use it

    Opioid overdoseSeizure and status epilepticusTraumatic brain injuryAlcohol intoxication and withdrawal
