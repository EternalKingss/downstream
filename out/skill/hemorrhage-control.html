    Skill

    External hemorrhage control

    Mechanical, sequential, and one of the few things you do that unambiguously saves lives.

    EMR

    Why it works

    Bleeding stops when a clot forms and holds. Everything you do is either helping the clot form by holding the vessel closed long enough, or stopping the flow entirely so it can. Pressure needs to be on the bleeding point, not spread vaguely across a wide dressing.

    How to do it, and why each step exists

    Every step below has a reason. If you know the reason, you can adapt when the situation does not match the textbook.

    Direct pressure, precisely on the point, hard

    Most external bleeding stops with correct pressure. If it is not working, you are usually not pressing hard enough or not pressing exactly on the source. Use one or two fingertips rather than a flat palm over a large pad.

    Do not lift the dressing to check

    A forming clot is fragile and lifting the dressing tears it away, restarting the bleed. Add dressings on top; never replace what is already there.

    Tourniquet for uncontrolled limb bleeding

    High and tight above the wound, tightened until the bleeding stops and the distal pulse is gone. A partially tightened tourniquet occludes venous return but not arterial inflow and makes bleeding worse.

    Write the time on it

    Duration determines the surgical decision on arrival. Modern evidence supports leaving a correctly applied tourniquet for a considerable period, and you should not loosen it once applied.

    Pack junctional wounds with hemostatic gauze

    For groin, axilla, and neck, where a tourniquet cannot go. Pack firmly directly into the cavity onto the bleeding point, then hold sustained pressure for the full recommended time. Stuffing gauze loosely on top does nothing.

    Keep them warm throughout

    Clotting factors are enzymes and they work poorly when cold. Hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy reinforce each other, and temperature is the part you control.

    Common errors

    • Pressure spread over too wide an area rather than on the bleeding point
    • Repeatedly lifting dressings to inspect
    • Hesitating to apply a tourniquet
    • A tourniquet applied too loosely, which increases bleeding
    • Loosening or removing a tourniquet in the field
    • Cutting all the clothing off and leaving the patient exposed on a cold scene
