    Body system

    Respiratory system

    Move air in, get oxygen into blood, get carbon dioxide out.

    Full page →

    Filed by the part that is physically damaged, not by diagnosis. The groups below are really a list of the ways this system fails.

    Upper airway

    Failure mode: Air can't get past the entrance

    AnaphylaxisForeign body airway obstructionDrowningSmoke inhalation

    Everything from the nose and mouth down to the vocal cords: nasal passages, oropharynx, the epiglottis, and the larynx. It is a shared tube. Food and air both travel through the pharynx, and only the epiglottis keeps them going to the right places.

    What it does when it works

    • Warms, humidifies, and filters incoming air before it reaches the lungs.
    • The epiglottis folds back over the larynx during swallowing so food goes to the esophagus, not the trachea.
    • The vocal cords sit at the narrowest point of the adult airway. In children under about eight, the narrowest point is lower, at the cricoid ring.
    • Muscle tone holds the tongue forward and off the posterior pharyngeal wall.

    Why this structure matters

    The upper airway is the single most common place a prehospital patient dies, and almost always for a boring reason. An unconscious patient loses the muscle tone that holds the tongue forward, the tongue falls back against the pharyngeal wall, and the airway closes. No drug fixes that. A head-tilt chin-lift does.

    In the field

    • It is soft tissue. It swells, and swelling in a tube reduces flow to the fourth power of the radius. Halve the radius and you have cut flow to a sixteenth.
    • In small children the airway is proportionally narrower, so the same millimetre of swelling is catastrophic where an adult would barely notice.

    Trachea and main bronchi

    Failure mode: The main pipe is blocked or displaced

    Foreign body airway obstruction

    The trachea runs from the cricoid cartilage down to the carina, where it splits into the right and left main bronchi. It is held permanently open by C-shaped cartilage rings, with the open side of the C facing backwards against the esophagus.

    What it does when it works

    • Conducts air. No gas exchange happens here at all.
    • Ciliated cells sweep a mucus layer upward toward the pharynx, carrying trapped debris out.
    • Sits midline in the neck, which makes it a useful physical landmark.

    Why this structure matters

    The right main bronchus branches at a shallower angle than the left, so aspirated material and over-inserted tubes both tend to go right. Because the trachea is normally midline and mobile, it is also a pressure gauge for the chest: anything that builds enough pressure in one hemithorax will push it toward the other side.

    In the field

    • Tracheal deviation is a late and often absent sign of tension pneumothorax. Do not wait for it.
    • The cartilage rings mean this section cannot collapse from bronchospasm the way smaller airways can.

    Bronchioles

    Failure mode: Air can't flow through

    AsthmaCOPDAnaphylaxisBronchiectasis

    The small airways beyond the segmental bronchi, roughly one millimetre across and smaller. Critically, they have no cartilage. Their walls are smooth muscle wrapped around a mucosa, and their diameter is actively controlled.

    What it does when it works

    • Smooth muscle tone sets airway calibre. Sympathetic stimulation of beta-2 receptors relaxes the muscle and widens the airway; parasympathetic stimulation narrows it.
    • Because there is no cartilage, bronchioles are held open partly by the outward pull of surrounding lung tissue rather than by their own structure.
    • That outward pull is strongest when the lung is inflated, which is why airways are wider on inspiration and narrower on expiration.

    Why this structure matters

    This is where obstructive lung disease lives. Three things can narrow a bronchiole and they usually happen together: the muscle constricts, the lining swells, and mucus fills the lumen. A bronchodilator only reverses the first one, which is exactly why a severe asthmatic can look barely improved after salbutamol.

    In the field

    • Because airways are narrower on expiration, obstruction always hits exhalation first. That is why asthma produces a prolonged expiratory phase and expiratory wheeze.
    • Poiseuille's law applies here with brutal force. Airway resistance rises with the fourth power of the radius, so a small amount of narrowing produces a very large amount of work.

    Alveoli

    Failure mode: Gas can't cross the membrane

    COPDPneumoniaPulmonary edemaDrowningEmphysemaSmoke inhalation

    Roughly 300 million thin-walled sacs at the end of the airway tree, each wrapped in a capillary net. The barrier between air and blood is about half a micron thick, thinner than a red blood cell is wide.

    What it does when it works

    • Oxygen and carbon dioxide cross the alveolar-capillary membrane by simple diffusion, driven only by the pressure difference across it.
    • Type II cells produce surfactant, which lowers surface tension and stops the sacs collapsing on themselves at the end of expiration.
    • The total surface area is around 70 square metres, roughly the floor of a small apartment, folded into your chest.

    Why this structure matters

    Diffusion has no pump behind it. It depends on three things and nothing else: the pressure gradient, the thickness of the barrier, and the surface area available. Every alveolar disease attacks one of those three. Pulmonary edema thickens the barrier. Pneumonia floods and thickens it. Emphysema destroys the surface area. Altitude drops the gradient. Understanding which one you are dealing with tells you whether oxygen will help much.

    In the field

    • Carbon dioxide diffuses roughly twenty times more readily than oxygen. In early alveolar disease you see hypoxia first, and CO2 stays normal or even low because the patient is breathing fast. Rising CO2 is a late and ominous sign.
    • Supplemental oxygen works by raising the gradient. It does nothing for a thickened membrane or destroyed surface area except push harder against the same obstacle.

    Pleural space

    Failure mode: Lung can't expand

    Tension pneumothoraxSimple pneumothoraxHemothoraxPleural effusion

    Not really a space. It is a sealed potential space between two membranes: the visceral pleura stuck to the lung surface and the parietal pleura lining the inside of the chest wall. Between them sits a few millilitres of lubricating fluid and nothing else.

    What it does when it works

    • The pressure inside is negative, roughly minus five centimetres of water at rest, dropping further on inspiration.
    • That negative pressure is the only thing holding the lung against the chest wall. The lung has no attachment of its own and is elastic, so left to itself it would recoil into a small lump near the hilum.
    • The thin fluid layer lets the two surfaces slide over each other with almost no friction through every breath.

    Why this structure matters

    The entire respiratory system runs on a vacuum seal. Break the seal and the mechanics fail immediately, no matter how healthy the lung tissue is. This is why every condition in this group looks similar: the lung is fine, it simply cannot inflate. What differs between them is only what got into the space, and how fast.

    In the field

    • Air, blood, and fluid all do the same mechanical thing here. What separates pneumothorax, hemothorax, and effusion is the rate of onset and whether the patient is also losing circulating volume.
    • A one-way valve leak turns a survivable pneumothorax into a tension pneumothorax, because now pressure keeps climbing with every breath and starts squeezing the heart and great vessels too.

    Pulmonary vessels

    Failure mode: Blood isn't there to pick it up

    Pulmonary embolismPulmonary hypertension

    The pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the capillary beds around the alveoli, and the pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood to the left atrium. This is the only place in the body where arteries carry deoxygenated blood.

    What it does when it works

    • A low-pressure, high-compliance circuit. Pulmonary artery pressure runs around 25/10, a fraction of systemic pressure.
    • The whole cardiac output passes through it every minute. There is no parallel path.
    • Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction diverts blood away from poorly ventilated regions, matching perfusion to ventilation automatically.

    Why this structure matters

    Ventilation without perfusion is useless. You can move perfect air into a perfect alveolus and achieve nothing if no blood arrives to collect the oxygen. This is why a pulmonary embolism produces profound hypoxia with a clear chest, normal breath sounds, and a patient who looks far sicker than their exam suggests.

    In the field

    • A PE patient often has an unremarkable respiratory exam. Sudden onset dyspnea with clear lungs and no obvious cause should raise this immediately.
    • Because the entire cardiac output goes through here, a large enough embolus obstructs right ventricular outflow and kills through cardiovascular collapse rather than through hypoxia.

    Chest wall and diaphragm

    Failure mode: Nothing is driving the pump

    The deteriorating childFlail chestOpioid overdoseRib fracturesNeuromuscular respiratory failure

    The ribs, intercostal muscles, and the diaphragm, plus the nerves that drive them. The diaphragm does most of the work of quiet breathing and is supplied by the phrenic nerve, which arises from cervical roots three, four, and five.

    What it does when it works

    • Inspiration is active. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, the ribs swing up and out, thoracic volume increases, and pressure inside drops below atmospheric so air flows in.
    • Expiration at rest is passive. The lung and chest wall simply recoil.
    • Accessory muscles in the neck and abdomen are recruited only when demand is high.

    Why this structure matters

    Air does not get sucked in by the lungs. It gets pushed in by atmospheric pressure because the chest made a vacuum. Anything that stops the chest making that vacuum stops ventilation, no matter how healthy the lungs are. That includes a broken segment of ribs, a paralysed diaphragm, a depressed brainstem, or a patient too exhausted to keep going.

    In the field

    • C3, 4, and 5 keep the diaphragm alive. A cervical spine injury above that level takes out spontaneous breathing entirely.
    • Opioids do not damage the lungs at all. They suppress the brainstem drive to breathe, which is a chest wall and bellows problem, and it is completely reversible with naloxone.
    • Accessory muscle use is not a diagnosis, it is a fuel gauge. Those muscles fatigue, and when they fail the patient stops compensating very suddenly.

    Body system

    Circulatory system

    Move oxygenated blood to tissue and bring it back.

    Full page →

    Filed by the part that is physically damaged, not by diagnosis. The groups below are really a list of the ways this system fails.

    Myocardium

    Failure mode: The pump can't generate output

    Acute coronary syndromeSudden cardiac arrestCardiogenic shockCardiac tamponadeCongestive heart failureStimulant toxicity

    The muscular wall of the heart, and the only muscle in the body that contracts continuously from before birth until death without ever resting. The left ventricle is by far the thickest, because it has to push blood through the entire systemic circulation while the right ventricle only has to reach the lungs next door.

    What it does when it works

    • Cardiac output is heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Roughly five litres a minute at rest, and it can climb four or five times that in exercise.
    • Stroke volume depends on three things: preload, which is how full the ventricle is before it contracts; contractility, which is how hard the muscle squeezes; and afterload, which is the pressure it has to push against.
    • The Frank-Starling relationship means that within limits, the more the ventricle is stretched by incoming blood, the harder it contracts. A heart that is filled well pumps well.
    • Myocardium is almost entirely aerobic. It has very little capacity for anaerobic metabolism, and it extracts around seventy percent of the oxygen from the blood passing through it even at rest.

    Why this structure matters

    Because the heart extracts most of the available oxygen even at baseline, it cannot compensate for increased demand by extracting more. Its only option is to increase flow. That single fact explains angina, explains why tachycardia is dangerous in ischemia, and explains why a hypoxic heart deteriorates so quickly.

    In the field

    • The left ventricle fills during diastole, and coronary blood flow to it also happens during diastole. Tachycardia shortens diastole disproportionately, so a fast heart both needs more oxygen and receives less of it.
    • The right and left ventricles fail differently. Left-sided failure backs fluid up into the lungs and causes crackles and breathlessness. Right-sided failure backs fluid up into the body and causes distended neck veins, liver congestion, and swollen legs.
    • Damaged myocardium does not regenerate. What is lost in an infarct is replaced by scar, which conducts poorly and does not contract at all.

    Coronary arteries

    Failure mode: The pump's own blood supply is cut

    Acute coronary syndrome

    Two arteries branching off the aorta immediately above the aortic valve, supplying the heart muscle itself. The left main divides into the left anterior descending and the circumflex; the right coronary artery supplies the right side, the inferior wall in most people, and the SA and AV nodes.

    What it does when it works

    • They fill during diastole, when the heart is relaxed. During systole the contracting muscle squeezes them nearly shut, so the heart can only feed itself between beats.
    • They are functional end arteries. Collateral circulation exists but is usually inadequate to rescue tissue acutely.
    • Flow is regulated by local metabolic demand, so a working region of myocardium can recruit more supply, up to the limit the vessel allows.

    Why this structure matters

    Every acute coronary syndrome is a supply and demand problem in these vessels. Stable angina is demand outrunning a fixed narrowed supply, and it resolves with rest. An infarct is supply abruptly cut by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque, and it does not resolve with rest. The distinction is entirely about whether the obstruction is fixed and partial or sudden and complete.

    In the field

    • Which artery is blocked determines the presentation. Right coronary occlusions cause inferior infarcts, which often involve the right ventricle and are preload dependent, which is exactly why nitroglycerin can drop the pressure through the floor in those patients.
    • The right coronary artery also supplies the conduction nodes in most people, so inferior infarcts commonly come with bradycardia and heart block.
    • Time is muscle. Roughly speaking, myocardium starts dying within twenty to thirty minutes of complete occlusion and infarction is largely complete by six hours. Everything you do prehospital is about shortening that clock.

    Conduction system

    Failure mode: Electrical timing breaks down

    Sudden cardiac arrestCardiogenic shockElectrical injury

    Specialised cardiac cells that generate and conduct the electrical impulse: the sinoatrial node in the right atrium, the atrioventricular node at the junction, the bundle of His, the left and right bundle branches, and the Purkinje fibres spreading through the ventricular walls.

    What it does when it works

    • The SA node fires at 60 to 100 per minute and sets the rate because it is the fastest pacemaker present.
    • The AV node deliberately delays the impulse by about a tenth of a second, which lets the atria finish emptying into the ventricles before the ventricles contract. That delay is where the atrial kick comes from.
    • Backup pacemakers exist further down. The AV junction fires at 40 to 60, the ventricles at 20 to 40. Slower, and progressively less effective.
    • Electrical activity precedes mechanical activity. A rhythm on the monitor is a statement about electricity, not about output.

    Why this structure matters

    Rate and rhythm both matter, and for different reasons. Too slow and cardiac output falls because there are not enough beats. Too fast and output also falls, because diastole shortens so much the ventricles never fill properly. And if the atria stop contributing their kick, as in atrial fibrillation, output drops by roughly a fifth in a patient who may not have a fifth to spare.

    In the field

    • Pulseless electrical activity is this system working perfectly while the pump produces nothing. The electricity is not the problem, so shocking it will not help. Look for the mechanical cause.
    • Ventricular fibrillation is this system in complete disorder, with no coordinated contraction at all. It is electrical, and defibrillation is the answer.
    • Potassium, calcium, magnesium, hypoxia, acidosis, and hypothermia all disturb conduction. A rhythm problem is often a chemistry problem wearing a costume.

    Heart valves

    Failure mode: Blood goes the wrong way or can't get out

    Congestive heart failure

    Four one-way valves. The tricuspid and mitral valves sit between atria and ventricles and are tethered by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles. The pulmonic and aortic valves sit at the ventricular outflows.

    What it does when it works

    • They open and close passively, driven purely by the pressure difference across them. There is no active mechanism.
    • The chordae stop the AV valves from being blown backwards into the atria when ventricular pressure spikes.
    • Valve closure produces the heart sounds. The first sound is the AV valves shutting at the start of systole; the second is the outflow valves shutting at the end of it.

    Why this structure matters

    A leaking valve means part of every stroke volume goes backwards, so the heart does full work for partial output. A narrowed valve means the ventricle has to generate far higher pressure to push the same volume through, which raises oxygen demand enormously. Both eventually cause the ventricle behind them to fail.

    In the field

    • Aortic stenosis is the one to remember. These patients have a fixed obstruction to outflow and depend on preload and a high pressure gradient. Nitroglycerin drops their preload and can cause abrupt collapse.
    • A papillary muscle can rupture during an inferior infarct, converting a competent mitral valve into a catastrophically leaking one in a single beat. Sudden severe pulmonary edema in a patient with chest pain should raise it.

    Pericardium

    Failure mode: The heart can't fill

    Cardiac tamponade

    A tough fibrous sac around the heart, lined by a serous membrane, containing about fifteen to fifty millilitres of lubricating fluid. Like the pleural space, it is a sealed potential space rather than an actual cavity.

    What it does when it works

    • Anchors the heart within the mediastinum and reduces friction as it beats.
    • Limits acute overdistension of the chambers.
    • The fibrous layer is stiff and does not stretch quickly, though it will slowly accommodate large volumes over weeks or months.

    Why this structure matters

    Because the sac does not stretch acutely, rate of accumulation matters far more than volume. As little as 100 to 150 millilitres of blood arriving quickly can cause tamponade, while a slowly growing chronic effusion can reach a litre or more with the patient still walking around. This is the same lesson as the pleural space, in a smaller container.

    In the field

    • Tamponade compresses the thin-walled right side of the heart first, because the right atrium and ventricle have the lowest pressures and collapse most easily.
    • Beck's triad is hypotension, distended neck veins, and muffled heart sounds. It is classic, taught everywhere, and frequently incomplete in real patients. Do not require all three.

    Arteries and arterioles

    Failure mode: Pressure can't be maintained, or the route is blocked

    External hemorrhageSeptic shockNeurogenic shockTraumatic amputationAnaphylaxis

    Thick-walled, muscular, elastic vessels carrying blood away from the heart. Large arteries act as elastic reservoirs that smooth pulsatile flow; arterioles are the small muscular vessels that actually control where blood goes.

    What it does when it works

    • Arterioles are the resistance vessels. Their smooth muscle tone determines systemic vascular resistance, and therefore blood pressure, and therefore which tissues get flow.
    • Blood pressure is cardiac output multiplied by systemic vascular resistance. Only two variables, which is why every form of shock is a failure of one, the other, or both.
    • Sympathetic stimulation constricts arterioles in skin, gut, and muscle to preserve flow to brain and heart. That redistribution is what makes a shocked patient pale, cool, and clammy.
    • Elastic recoil in the aorta during diastole maintains diastolic pressure, which is what actually perfuses the coronary arteries.

    Why this structure matters

    Blood pressure is not the same as perfusion, and this is the single most misunderstood point in shock. A patient can maintain a completely normal blood pressure by clamping down their arterioles while their tissues are already starving. Compensated shock is defined by a normal pressure hiding an abnormal state, and the blood pressure only falls once compensation has been exhausted.

    In the field

    • Narrowing pulse pressure is an early sign of hypovolemia: the diastolic rises from vasoconstriction while systolic starts to fall from lost volume.
    • In a healthy adult, blood pressure often does not fall until roughly thirty percent of blood volume is gone. By the time it drops, the patient is in serious trouble.
    • Children compensate even harder and hold their pressure even longer, then crash without much warning. Never be reassured by a normal blood pressure in an injured child.

    Capillaries

    Failure mode: Exchange fails, or fluid leaks out

    Hemorrhagic and hypovolemic shockSeptic shockThe deteriorating childAnaphylaxis

    Vessels one cell thick, roughly the diameter of a single red blood cell, forming networks through every tissue. This is the only place in the entire circulation where anything is actually exchanged. Everything else is plumbing.

    What it does when it works

    • Oxygen, carbon dioxide, glucose, and waste diffuse across the wall down their concentration gradients.
    • Hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out at the arterial end; oncotic pressure from plasma proteins pulls it back in at the venous end. The balance normally leaves a small net outflow that the lymphatics collect.
    • Precapillary sphincters open and close beds according to local demand, so only a fraction of capillaries are perfused at any given moment.

    Why this structure matters

    This is where the entire point of the circulatory system actually happens, and it is where shock actually kills. Blood pressure, heart rate, and cardiac output are all just proxies for whether these vessels are delivering. Capillary refill is the one bedside test that looks directly at this level rather than at a proxy.

    In the field

    • In septic shock the capillaries become leaky and dilated, so fluid pours into the tissues while the vessels themselves stay wide open. The patient can be simultaneously fluid overloaded and intravascularly empty.
    • Prolonged capillary refill beyond two seconds is a genuinely useful early sign in children, and much less reliable in cold adults or cold environments.
    • Anaphylaxis causes massive capillary leak. That is where the circulating volume goes, and it is why these patients are profoundly hypotensive without losing a drop of blood.

    Veins

    Failure mode: Blood can't get back to the heart

    External hemorrhageHemorrhagic and hypovolemic shockNeurogenic shock

    Thin-walled, low-pressure, highly compliant vessels returning blood to the heart. They hold roughly sixty to seventy percent of total blood volume at any moment, which makes them the body's blood reservoir rather than just a return path.

    What it does when it works

    • Venous tone is under sympathetic control. Constricting the veins squeezes stored blood back into circulation, effectively giving an internal transfusion of up to about a litre.
    • One-way valves in the limbs prevent backflow, and skeletal muscle contraction pumps blood upward against gravity.
    • Negative intrathoracic pressure during inspiration actively draws blood back toward the chest.

    Why this structure matters

    Preload is venous return, and stroke volume depends on preload. Anything that reduces venous return reduces cardiac output immediately, no matter how strong the heart is. This is why tension pneumothorax, tamponade, and positive pressure ventilation can all drop a blood pressure without the heart being damaged at all.

    In the field

    • Distended neck veins mean blood is backing up behind an obstruction: tension pneumothorax, tamponade, or right heart failure. Flat neck veins in a shocked patient point to volume loss instead.
    • Nitroglycerin works mainly here. It dilates veins, blood pools in the periphery, preload falls, and the heart has less work to do. That is also exactly why it is dangerous in a preload-dependent patient.
    • Aggressive bagging raises intrathoracic pressure and impedes venous return. Overventilating a hypotensive patient makes them more hypotensive.

    Blood

    Failure mode: Nothing there to carry the oxygen

    Gastrointestinal bleedingExternal hemorrhageHemorrhagic and hypovolemic shockHemothoraxSmoke inhalationCarbon monoxide poisoning

    About five litres in an adult, roughly seven percent of body weight. Slightly over half is plasma, mostly water with proteins and electrolytes; the rest is cells, overwhelmingly red cells.

    What it does when it works

    • Hemoglobin carries almost all the oxygen. Only about one and a half percent travels dissolved in plasma, which is why oxygen content depends far more on hemoglobin than on the partial pressure.
    • Each gram of hemoglobin carries roughly 1.34 millilitres of oxygen when fully saturated.
    • Carbon dioxide travels mostly as bicarbonate, which also makes blood the body's main acid-base buffer.
    • Platelets and clotting factors seal breaches; white cells handle infection.

    Why this structure matters

    Oxygen delivery is hemoglobin multiplied by saturation multiplied by cardiac output. Notice that saturation is only one of three terms. A patient can have a perfect SpO2 of one hundred percent and still be dying of oxygen starvation because they have lost half their hemoglobin or half their cardiac output. The pulse oximeter measures the least important variable of the three.

    In the field

    • Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin roughly two hundred and fifty times more avidly than oxygen. The oximeter cannot tell the difference and will read reassuringly high while the patient suffocates.
    • Bleeding loses whole blood, so it loses hemoglobin and volume together. Replacing volume alone dilutes what carrying capacity is left.
    • Anemic patients desaturate their tissues long before they turn blue, because cyanosis needs an absolute quantity of desaturated hemoglobin they simply do not have.

    Body system

    Nervous system

    Sense, decide, and drive everything else.

    Full page →

    Filed by the part that is physically damaged, not by diagnosis. The groups below are really a list of the ways this system fails.

    Cerebrum

    Failure mode: Thinking, speech, and movement break

    HypoglycemiaIschemic strokeSeizure and status epilepticusTraumatic brain injuryBehavioural emergency and severe agitationAlcohol intoxication and withdrawal

    The two large hemispheres that make up most of the brain. The outer cortex handles conscious thought, voluntary movement, sensation, language, and personality. Deeper structures handle memory, emotion, and the relay of nearly all sensory traffic.

    What it does when it works

    • The frontal lobes handle judgement, planning, personality, and voluntary movement, with the motor strip running across the back of the frontal lobe.
    • The parietal lobes handle sensation and spatial awareness; the temporal lobes handle hearing, memory, and comprehension of language; the occipital lobes handle vision.
    • Motor and sensory pathways cross over, so the left hemisphere controls the right side of the body and vice versa.
    • In the great majority of people, including most left-handers, language lives in the left hemisphere.

    Why this structure matters

    Because function is mapped to location, the deficit tells you where the lesion is. Right-sided weakness with speech difficulty means the left hemisphere. Left-sided weakness with neglect, where the patient seems unaware of their own left side, means the right. That is not academic: it is how you describe a stroke usefully to the receiving team.

    In the field

    • The cerebrum can be substantially damaged without the patient dying, because breathing and circulation are controlled lower down. A patient can be devastated neurologically and completely stable physiologically.
    • It is also the most metabolically demanding tissue in the body, consuming around twenty percent of total oxygen while making up two percent of body mass, with essentially no stored reserve.
    • Right hemisphere strokes are missed more often, because the patient can still speak fluently and the deficit is easier to overlook.

    Brainstem

    Failure mode: Automatic functions stop

    Traumatic brain injuryOpioid overdose

    The midbrain, pons, and medulla, forming the stalk connecting the cerebrum to the spinal cord. Physically small, roughly the size of a thumb, and it runs everything that keeps you alive without your involvement.

    What it does when it works

    • Contains the respiratory centres that set the rate and depth of breathing, driven primarily by carbon dioxide levels rather than oxygen.
    • Contains the cardiovascular centres controlling heart rate and vascular tone.
    • Houses the reticular activating system, which maintains consciousness and wakefulness.
    • Every signal travelling between the brain and the body passes through it, and ten of the twelve cranial nerves originate here.

    Why this structure matters

    This is the smallest amount of tissue whose loss is immediately fatal. Damage here stops breathing, and it is also where rising intracranial pressure eventually squeezes the brain against the skull opening at the base. Almost every neurological death is ultimately a brainstem death, whatever started the process higher up.

    In the field

    • Opioids act primarily on the brainstem respiratory centre. They do not touch the lungs at all, which is precisely why naloxone reverses the problem completely.
    • The pattern of breathing tells you about the brainstem. Irregular, deep and sighing, or alternating deep and shallow breathing in a head-injured patient are ominous.
    • Pupil responses are a brainstem function. A unilaterally blown pupil in a head injury suggests the temporal lobe is being pushed down against the third cranial nerve, which is a herniation emergency.

    Cerebellum

    Failure mode: Coordination and balance are lost

    Nothing mapped yet

    The dense, tightly folded structure sitting behind the brainstem beneath the cerebrum. It contains more neurons than the rest of the brain combined, all devoted to making movement smooth and accurate.

    What it does when it works

    • Compares the movement you intended with the movement actually occurring and corrects the difference continuously.
    • Maintains balance and posture using input from the inner ear, from vision, and from position sensors in muscles and joints.
    • Coordinates the timing of muscle groups so that movement is smooth rather than jerky.
    • It does not initiate movement or generate strength. It refines both.

    Why this structure matters

    Cerebellar problems produce a patient who is not weak and not confused, but cannot control their movement. The most important field consequence is that they can look exactly like intoxication: staggering gait, slurred speech, poor coordination, and abnormal eye movements. Cerebellar strokes get dismissed as drunkenness with fatal regularity.

    In the field

    • Sudden vertigo, vomiting, and inability to walk, in someone who is otherwise alert, should raise cerebellar stroke rather than an inner ear problem.
    • The posterior fossa where it sits is a small, rigid compartment. Swelling here has nowhere to go and compresses the brainstem quickly, so a cerebellar bleed deteriorates faster than its size suggests.
    • Alcohol affects the cerebellum early and strongly, which is why field sobriety tests are essentially cerebellar function tests.

    Meninges and cerebrospinal fluid

    Failure mode: Pressure builds with nowhere to go

    Hemorrhagic strokeTraumatic brain injuryMeningitis and meningococcal sepsis

    Three membranes wrapping the brain and cord, plus the fluid between them. From the outside in: the tough dura mater, the web-like arachnoid, and the delicate pia mater stuck to the brain surface. Cerebrospinal fluid circulates in the subarachnoid space and through the ventricles.

    What it does when it works

    • About 150 millilitres of cerebrospinal fluid is present at any time, and roughly 500 millilitres is produced and reabsorbed daily.
    • The fluid cushions the brain, effectively reducing its working weight from around 1,400 grams to under 50, and removes metabolic waste.
    • Bridging veins cross the subdural space; arteries including the middle meningeal run in the epidural space outside the dura.
    • The skull is a closed rigid box containing three things: brain, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid. That is the Monro-Kellie doctrine, and it governs everything that follows.

    Why this structure matters

    Because the box is rigid and full, adding anything means displacing something else. A growing hematoma is initially compensated by squeezing out cerebrospinal fluid and venous blood, which is why a patient can have a lucid interval and appear fine. Once that compensation is exhausted, pressure rises very steeply for very small additional volume, and deterioration becomes sudden.

    In the field

    • Epidural bleeds are arterial, fast, and classically show a lucid interval followed by rapid collapse. Subdural bleeds are venous, slower, and common in older adults and heavy drinkers whose brains have shrunk and stretched the bridging veins.
    • Clear or blood-tinged fluid from the nose or ears after head trauma suggests a basilar skull fracture with a cerebrospinal fluid leak. Do not pack it; let it drain.
    • Infection of these membranes is meningitis, and because the space is continuous around the brain and cord, it spreads throughout the entire central nervous system.

    Cerebral blood vessels

    Failure mode: Brain tissue loses its supply, or bleeds

    Ischemic strokeHemorrhagic strokeSyncopePre-eclampsia and eclampsia

    Four arteries supply the brain: two carotids in front and two vertebral arteries behind. They join at the circle of Willis at the base of the brain, which then distributes flow through the anterior, middle, and posterior cerebral arteries.

    What it does when it works

    • The brain receives about fifteen percent of cardiac output, roughly 750 millilitres a minute, continuously and without interruption.
    • Autoregulation keeps cerebral flow constant across a wide range of blood pressures, roughly a mean arterial pressure of 60 to 150.
    • Cerebral perfusion pressure is mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. Both terms matter.
    • The circle of Willis provides some collateral supply, though it is complete and functional in fewer people than textbooks imply.

    Why this structure matters

    Neurons begin dying within minutes of losing flow, and roughly two million die every minute during a large vessel stroke. There is usually a surrounding zone of tissue that is starved but not yet dead, kept marginally alive by collateral flow. That zone is what treatment saves, and it shrinks with every minute.

    In the field

    • Middle cerebral artery strokes are the most common and produce the classic picture of face, arm, and speech involvement.
    • Posterior circulation strokes present with dizziness, double vision, ataxia, and vomiting rather than one-sided weakness, and they are missed far more often.
    • Because autoregulation fails below a mean arterial pressure of about 60, hypotension is devastating to an already injured brain. A single hypotensive episode measurably worsens outcomes in traumatic brain injury.

    Spinal cord

    Failure mode: Signals can't get past the injury

    Spinal cord injury

    A bundle of nerve tissue about as thick as a finger running from the brainstem down inside the vertebral column, ending around the first or second lumbar vertebra. Below that it continues as a bundle of nerve roots called the cauda equina.

    What it does when it works

    • Motor tracts descend from the brain to the muscles; sensory tracts ascend from the body to the brain.
    • Reflex arcs operate entirely within the cord, without involving the brain at all, which is why a spinal patient can have reflexes below an injury that has cut them off from the brain.
    • The phrenic nerve arises from C3, C4, and C5 and drives the diaphragm.
    • Sympathetic outflow leaves the cord between roughly T1 and L2, while parasympathetic outflow comes from the cranial nerves and the sacral segments.

    Why this structure matters

    The cord is an information cable running through a bony tube, and the level of injury determines exactly what is lost, because everything below is disconnected. Central nervous tissue does not regenerate meaningfully, so what is lost tends to stay lost, which is why preventing further injury during extrication and handling matters so much.

    In the field

    • C3, 4, and 5 keep the diaphragm alive. An injury above that stops spontaneous breathing entirely. Injury in the lower cervical or thoracic cord spares the diaphragm but takes out the intercostals, producing shallow diaphragmatic breathing that fatigues.
    • Injury above roughly T6 cuts sympathetic outflow and causes neurogenic shock: warm, dry, pink skin with hypotension and bradycardia.
    • Much of the final damage is secondary rather than primary, caused by swelling, bleeding, hypoxia, and hypotension in the hours after the injury. Keeping the pressure and the saturation up is genuinely neuroprotective.

    Peripheral nerves

    Failure mode: The signal doesn't reach the muscle

    Neuromuscular respiratory failureCholinergic toxicity

    All the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord: the roots leaving the cord, the plexuses where they reorganise, and the named nerves running to skin and muscle. Motor fibres carry instructions out, sensory fibres carry information in.

    What it does when it works

    • Each spinal nerve root supplies a predictable strip of skin, called a dermatome, and a predictable group of muscles, called a myotome. That mapping is consistent enough to localise an injury.
    • Signals travel as electrical impulses along the axon, then cross to muscle chemically at the neuromuscular junction using acetylcholine.
    • Unlike central nervous tissue, peripheral nerves can regenerate, though slowly, at roughly a millimetre a day.

    Why this structure matters

    Peripheral nerve findings localise injuries with real precision. Numbness in a specific dermatome or weakness in a specific movement tells you which root or nerve is involved, and it distinguishes a peripheral problem from a central one. It also tells you when a fracture or dislocation has damaged something beyond bone.

    In the field

    • Always check sensation and movement distal to any fracture or dislocation, before and after any manipulation or splinting, and document both.
    • Anything that blocks the neuromuscular junction causes paralysis with a completely intact brain. Organophosphate poisoning and nerve agents work here, flooding the junction with acetylcholine and causing weakness alongside massive secretions.
    • Ascending weakness starting in the feet and moving upward, as in Guillain-Barré, threatens the diaphragm and is a respiratory emergency even though the patient may look calm.

    Autonomic nervous system

    Failure mode: Automatic control is lost or overwhelmed

    SyncopeBehavioural emergency and severe agitationStimulant toxicityCholinergic toxicity

    The part of the nervous system running organs without conscious involvement, split into two opposing branches: sympathetic, which prepares for exertion, and parasympathetic, which handles rest and digestion.

    What it does when it works

    • Sympathetic activation raises heart rate and contractility, constricts vessels in skin and gut, dilates bronchioles and pupils, mobilises glucose, and triggers sweating. Its main messengers are noradrenaline and adrenaline.
    • Parasympathetic activation, travelling largely in the vagus nerve, slows the heart, constricts pupils and bronchioles, and stimulates digestion and secretions. Its messenger is acetylcholine.
    • The two are in constant balance, and the resting state is not neutral: there is continuous background tone from both.
    • Adrenergic receptors come in subtypes that matter enormously for pharmacology. Alpha-1 constricts vessels, beta-1 speeds and strengthens the heart, beta-2 relaxes bronchial and vascular smooth muscle.

    Why this structure matters

    Almost every drug an EMR or paramedic gives works on this system, and knowing the receptor subtypes turns drug effects from a memorised list into something you can derive. Salbutamol is beta-2, so it opens airways and, when it spills onto beta-1, causes tachycardia. Epinephrine hits all of them at once, which is why it works in anaphylaxis. Nothing about that has to be memorised if you know the receptors.

    In the field

    • The classic signs of shock are simply the sympathetic response made visible: tachycardia, pale cool clammy skin, dilated pupils, and anxiety.
    • Cholinergic poisoning from organophosphates or nerve agents produces massive parasympathetic overactivity: salivation, tearing, urination, defecation, vomiting, tiny pupils, and bronchial secretions that drown the patient.
    • Vagal stimulation from straining, vomiting, or pain can drop the heart rate and blood pressure sharply enough to cause syncope.

    Body system

    Endocrine and metabolic

    Keep fuel, fluid, and chemistry inside survivable limits.

    Full page →

    Filed by the part that is physically damaged, not by diagnosis. The groups below are really a list of the ways this system fails.

    Pancreas

    Failure mode: Blood sugar control is lost

    HypoglycemiaDiabetic ketoacidosisHyperosmolar hyperglycemic state

    A gland lying behind the stomach with two entirely separate jobs. The bulk of it makes digestive enzymes that drain into the duodenum. Scattered through it are the islets of Langerhans, clusters of hormone-producing cells that release insulin and glucagon straight into the bloodstream.

    What it does when it works

    • Beta cells release insulin when blood glucose rises. Insulin unlocks glucose entry into muscle and fat, and tells the liver to store rather than release.
    • Alpha cells release glucagon when glucose falls, telling the liver to break down glycogen and manufacture new glucose.
    • The two hormones oppose each other continuously, holding blood glucose in a remarkably narrow band around 4 to 7 mmol/L.
    • The digestive portion produces enzymes powerful enough to digest the pancreas itself, which is why they are secreted in an inactive form.

    Why this structure matters

    Insulin is the only hormone that lowers blood glucose, while several raise it. That asymmetry means the body is far better defended against low sugar than high, and it is why type 1 diabetes was uniformly fatal before insulin existed. Understanding that insulin also switches off fat breakdown explains ketoacidosis entirely.

    In the field

    • Without insulin, cells cannot take up glucose even when the blood is full of it. The patient is starving in the middle of plenty, which is why they break down fat and produce ketones.
    • Those digestive enzymes activating inside the gland is acute pancreatitis: the organ digests itself, causing severe pain and massive fluid shifts.
    • Islet cells are a tiny fraction of the gland's mass but their failure is what kills.

    Adrenal glands

    Failure mode: The stress response fails

    Adrenal crisis

    Two small glands capping the kidneys, each with an outer cortex and inner medulla that function as separate organs. The cortex makes steroid hormones; the medulla is effectively a modified sympathetic ganglion that dumps adrenaline directly into the blood.

    What it does when it works

    • The cortex produces cortisol, which maintains blood pressure, mobilises glucose, and modulates inflammation, and aldosterone, which retains sodium and water and excretes potassium.
    • The medulla releases adrenaline and noradrenaline into the circulation during sympathetic activation, producing the body-wide fight-or-flight response.
    • Cortisol output rises several-fold under physical stress such as infection, trauma, or surgery.

    Why this structure matters

    Cortisol is permissive: without it, blood vessels stop responding properly to catecholamines. A patient in adrenal crisis is hypotensive and does not respond to the usual compensations, because the hormone that lets those compensations work is missing.

    In the field

    • Anyone on long-term steroids has a suppressed adrenal response and cannot mount a stress response. Illness, injury, or abruptly stopping the steroid can precipitate a crisis.
    • Adrenal crisis presents as shock with hypotension, vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, and often low sodium and high potassium.
    • Ask about steroid use in any unexplained shock. Many patients carry a steroid card or emergency injection.
    Open adrenal glands on its own page →

    Thyroid gland

    Failure mode: Metabolic rate is set wrong

    Nothing mapped yet

    A butterfly-shaped gland across the front of the trachea, just below the larynx. It produces thyroid hormone, which sets the baseline metabolic rate of essentially every cell in the body.

    What it does when it works

    • Thyroid hormone determines how fast cells consume oxygen and produce heat.
    • It increases the number and sensitivity of beta-adrenergic receptors, which is why thyroid excess looks so much like sympathetic overdrive.
    • Output is regulated by TSH from the pituitary in a feedback loop, and turnover is slow, so changes develop over weeks rather than minutes.

    Why this structure matters

    Because it sets the gain on the whole metabolic system, extremes at either end are life-threatening in opposite directions. Thyroid storm produces fever, tachyarrhythmia, agitation, and cardiovascular collapse. Myxoedema coma produces hypothermia, bradycardia, hypoventilation, and unresponsiveness.

    In the field

    • Thyroid storm is often triggered by infection, trauma, or surgery in someone with untreated overactive thyroid, and it looks like severe sepsis with a very high temperature.
    • Myxoedema coma appears mostly in older women in winter, and the hypothermia does not respond to warming alone.
    • A large goitre can compress the trachea and cause airway compromise when the neck is extended.

    Body fluid and electrolytes

    Failure mode: Cells swell or shrivel, and nerves misfire

    Diabetic ketoacidosisHyperosmolar hyperglycemic stateCrush injury and crush syndromeAlcohol intoxication and withdrawal

    Water makes up roughly sixty percent of an adult, split between fluid inside cells and fluid outside them. The extracellular portion divides again into plasma inside vessels and interstitial fluid bathing the tissues. Dissolved electrolytes set how water distributes between these spaces.

    What it does when it works

    • Sodium is the dominant extracellular ion and is what holds water in the extracellular space. Potassium is the dominant intracellular ion.
    • The difference across the membrane, maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, is what makes nerves and muscle electrically excitable at all.
    • Water moves passively by osmosis toward whichever side has more dissolved solute.
    • The kidneys control the whole system by adjusting what is excreted and what is retained.

    Why this structure matters

    Every nerve impulse and every heartbeat depends on ion gradients across membranes. Derange the electrolytes and the electrical behaviour of the heart and brain changes directly. Potassium is the one that kills fastest: both high and low levels cause lethal arrhythmias, and the therapeutic window is narrow.

    In the field

    • Crush injury, burns, and renal failure all release or retain potassium, which is why those patients arrest suddenly.
    • Sodium changes act on the brain. Falling sodium pulls water into brain cells, causing confusion and seizures, and it is a common cause of seizure in older patients on diuretics.
    • Correcting sodium too quickly is itself dangerous, which is why these patients are managed slowly in hospital rather than fixed in the field.

    Body system

    Abdomen and digestion

    Process food, filter blood, and hold organs that bleed heavily.

    Full page →

    Filed by the part that is physically damaged, not by diagnosis. The groups below are really a list of the ways this system fails.

    Hollow abdominal organs

    Failure mode: Contents spill, or the tube blocks

    Gastrointestinal bleedingPeritonitis and the acute abdomen

    The stomach, small intestine, and large intestine, plus the gallbladder and bladder. All are tubes or sacs with a lumen, a muscular wall, and contents that belong inside them.

    What it does when it works

    • Peristalsis moves contents along in one direction using coordinated waves of smooth muscle contraction.
    • The stomach secretes acid strong enough to require an actively maintained mucus barrier for protection.
    • The small intestine absorbs nutrients; the large intestine reclaims water and houses the bacterial population.
    • Sensation from these organs travels on autonomic fibres, so pain is poorly localised and typically felt in the midline.

    Why this structure matters

    Hollow organs fail in two ways: they perforate, spilling contents that are chemically and bacterially hostile into the peritoneum, or they obstruct, causing distension, vomiting, and massive fluid sequestration into the bowel. Blunt trauma tends to burst them at fixed points; penetrating trauma opens them anywhere.

    In the field

    • Visceral pain from a hollow organ is dull, crampy, and central. When inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum the pain becomes sharp and localises, which is why appendicitis migrates from the umbilicus to the right lower quadrant.
    • Obstruction sequesters litres of fluid into the bowel lumen. These patients are genuinely hypovolemic even though nothing has visibly left the body.
    • A perforated organ causes rigid, board-like guarding and a patient who lies completely still, because any movement hurts.

    Solid abdominal organs

    Failure mode: Massive concealed bleeding

    Solid organ injury

    The liver, spleen, pancreas, and kidneys. Dense, highly vascular organs with a rich blood supply and a fragile capsule holding them together.

    What it does when it works

    • The liver receives about a quarter of cardiac output, manufactures clotting factors and plasma proteins, stores glycogen, and detoxifies.
    • The spleen filters blood and holds a reservoir of it.
    • The kidneys receive roughly a fifth of cardiac output and regulate fluid, electrolytes, acid-base balance, and blood pressure.
    • All are partly protected by the lower ribs, and all are held in place by ligaments that become tearing points during deceleration.

    Why this structure matters

    These organs are essentially blood-filled sponges. When torn, they bleed into the abdominal cavity, which can hold litres without any visible distension until very late. This is where trauma patients bleed to death without a drop appearing on the outside.

    In the field

    • Lower rib fractures are a red flag. Left-sided ones threaten the spleen, right-sided ones the liver.
    • The spleen is the most commonly injured organ in blunt abdominal trauma, and a delayed rupture hours or days later is well described.
    • Referred pain to the left shoulder from a bleeding spleen, called Kehr's sign, comes from blood irritating the diaphragm, which shares nerve roots with the shoulder.
    • Liver injury also destroys clotting factor production, so severe liver trauma bleeds and then keeps bleeding.

    Peritoneum

    Failure mode: Inflammation, and litres of fluid lost into the abdomen

    Peritonitis and the acute abdomenSolid organ injury

    A large serous membrane lining the abdominal cavity and wrapping most of its organs, with a total surface area roughly equal to the skin. The visceral layer covering organs has autonomic innervation; the parietal layer lining the wall has somatic innervation.

    What it does when it works

    • Secretes a small amount of fluid so organs slide against each other without friction.
    • Suspends and anchors the organs.
    • Has substantial immune activity and can wall off local infection.
    • Absorbs and secretes fluid across an enormous surface area.

    Why this structure matters

    The innervation difference explains the classic evolution of abdominal pain. Visceral irritation gives vague central pain; once the process reaches the parietal peritoneum, the pain becomes sharp, localised, and worse with any movement. And because the surface area is so large, generalised peritonitis sequesters several litres of fluid out of the circulation, causing profound hypovolemic shock with nothing lost externally.

    In the field

    • Rigidity, rebound tenderness, and a patient who lies motionless with knees drawn up all indicate parietal peritoneal irritation.
    • Guarding that is involuntary and constant is far more significant than voluntary tensing.
    • Peritonitis patients are hypovolemic. Treat them as shocked even before the blood pressure falls.

    Abdominal aorta and great vessels

    Failure mode: Catastrophic bleeding in seconds

    Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm

    The aorta descends through the abdomen just left of the midline and in front of the spine, dividing around the level of the umbilicus into the iliac arteries. The inferior vena cava runs alongside it, returning blood from everything below the diaphragm.

    What it does when it works

    • Carries the entire cardiac output destined for the abdomen, pelvis, and legs.
    • The wall has three layers, with a strong elastic middle layer bearing the pressure.
    • It lies retroperitoneally, behind the peritoneum and in front of the vertebral bodies.

    Why this structure matters

    Nothing else in the abdomen can kill this fast. A ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm can empty the circulation in minutes. Because it sits retroperitoneally, a leak may initially be contained behind the peritoneum, which produces a window of relative stability that ends abruptly when the containment fails.

    In the field

    • Atherosclerosis weakens the wall over decades and it balloons. Most aneurysms are silent until they leak.
    • The classic triad is abdominal or back pain, hypotension, and a pulsatile abdominal mass, though all three together are uncommon.
    • Ruptured aneurysm is frequently mistaken for renal colic. An older patient with new severe flank or back pain and any hemodynamic instability needs this considered.
    • Do not palpate deeply or repeatedly if you suspect it.

    Musculoskeletal system

    Structure, protection, movement.

    Full page →

    Filed by the part that is physically damaged, not by diagnosis. The groups below are really a list of the ways this system fails.

    Bones

    Failure mode: Structure breaks, and bleeds

    Fractures

    Living tissue with a mineralised matrix, a rich blood supply, and marrow in the centre. Long bones have a hard outer cortex and a spongy interior, wrapped in periosteum that carries nerves and vessels.

    What it does when it works

    • Provides structural support, protects organs, and gives muscles something to pull against.
    • Stores calcium and produces blood cells in the marrow.
    • Continuously remodels, so it can heal a fracture completely rather than scarring.
    • The periosteum is densely innervated, which is why fractures hurt so much.

    Why this structure matters

    A fracture is not just a structural problem, it is a bleeding problem. Bone is highly vascular and the surrounding muscle bleeds too. A closed femur fracture can lose over a litre into the thigh, a pelvic fracture several litres, and both can do so invisibly. Multiple long bone fractures can cause hemorrhagic shock with no external blood at all.

    In the field

    • Splinting is a hemorrhage control measure as much as a comfort measure. Immobilising a fracture reduces movement of the bone ends, which reduces ongoing bleeding.
    • Always check circulation, sensation, and movement distal to a fracture, before and after any manipulation, and document both.
    • An open fracture communicates with the outside and carries a serious infection risk, which changes urgency.
    • In children, growth plates are weaker than ligaments, so where an adult sprains, a child fractures.

    Joints and ligaments

    Failure mode: The hinge comes apart

    Dislocation

    Where two bones meet, with cartilage covering the surfaces, a capsule enclosing the space, synovial fluid lubricating it, and ligaments holding bone to bone.

    What it does when it works

    • Cartilage provides a near-frictionless bearing surface with no direct blood supply, which is why it heals poorly.
    • Ligaments allow movement in intended directions and resist it in others.
    • Position sensors in the capsule and ligaments tell the brain where the limb is.

    Why this structure matters

    Dislocation is a time-critical problem in a way that sprains are not, because the displaced bone can compress or stretch the nerves and vessels that run close to the joint. Certain dislocations threaten specific structures reliably enough to be worth memorising.

    In the field

    • Knee dislocation, as opposed to kneecap dislocation, has a high rate of popliteal artery injury. Check the foot pulses and treat a pulseless dislocated knee as a genuine emergency.
    • Posterior hip dislocation threatens the sciatic nerve and the blood supply to the femoral head.
    • Shoulder dislocation threatens the axillary nerve, so check sensation over the deltoid.
    • Splint dislocations in the position found unless the limb is pulseless. Do not attempt reduction outside your scope.

    Muscles and fascial compartments

    Failure mode: Pressure rises inside a space that can't expand

    Compartment syndromeCrush injury and crush syndrome

    Muscles are bundled into groups wrapped in fascia, a tough inelastic sheet. Each wrapped group is a compartment containing muscle, nerves, and blood vessels. The lower leg has four; the forearm has three.

    What it does when it works

    • Muscle converts chemical energy into movement, and contracting muscle in the limbs also pumps venous blood back toward the heart.
    • Fascia keeps muscle groups organised and transmits force efficiently.
    • Fascia does not stretch meaningfully, which is fine until something inside starts swelling.

    Why this structure matters

    This is the same closed-box problem as the skull and the pericardium, in a limb. Bleeding or swelling inside a compartment raises the pressure, and because the fascia will not give, that pressure squeezes the capillaries shut. The muscle becomes ischemic while a distal pulse is still perfectly palpable, because arterial pressure is far higher than capillary pressure.

    In the field

    • The presence of a pulse does not rule out compartment syndrome and is one of the most dangerous false reassurances in trauma.
    • The earliest and most reliable sign is pain out of proportion to the injury, and pain on passive stretch of the muscles in that compartment.
    • Tight casts, circumferential burns, crush injury, and fractures are the common causes.
    • Muscle dies within about four to six hours of ischemia, and the damage is permanent.

    Pelvis

    Failure mode: A ring breaks and a huge space fills with blood

    Nothing mapped yet

    A bony ring formed by two innominate bones and the sacrum, joined by very strong ligaments. It protects the bladder, rectum, reproductive organs, and a dense plexus of veins and arteries.

    What it does when it works

    • Transfers weight from the spine to the legs.
    • Because it is a ring, it is inherently stable and cannot break in only one place. If you find one fracture, there is another.
    • The retroperitoneal space behind it can accommodate several litres of blood before anything becomes externally obvious.

    Why this structure matters

    Pelvic fractures kill through hemorrhage. The venous plexus over the back of the pelvis bleeds heavily, the space it bleeds into is large, and the bleeding does not tamponade itself the way a limb might. Mortality in unstable pelvic fractures with hemodynamic instability is very high.

    In the field

    • Do not spring or rock the pelvis to test stability. It achieves nothing diagnostically and can disrupt a clot that had formed. Assess by mechanism, pain, and appearance.
    • A binder placed at the level of the greater trochanters, not the iliac crests, reduces pelvic volume and helps clots hold. It is one of the highest-value trauma interventions available at EMR level.
    • Log-rolling a suspected pelvic fracture can restart bleeding. Use a scoop stretcher instead where possible.
    • Blood at the urethral meatus, scrotal or perineal bruising, and leg length or rotation asymmetry all suggest pelvic injury.

    Skin and temperature

    Barrier, thermoregulation, fluid containment.

    Full page →

    Filed by the part that is physically damaged, not by diagnosis. The groups below are really a list of the ways this system fails.

    Skin as a barrier

    Failure mode: The seal is breached

    Thermal burns

    The largest organ in the body, in three layers. The epidermis is a thin outer sheet of dead keratinised cells, the dermis beneath it holds vessels, nerves, and glands, and below that lies subcutaneous fat.

    What it does when it works

    • Keeps water in and organisms out. The dead outer layer is the actual waterproof barrier.
    • The dermis carries the sensory nerve endings, which is why partial thickness burns are agonising and full thickness burns are numb.
    • Sweat glands and hair follicles reach down into the dermis, and skin regenerates from them after a partial thickness injury.

    Why this structure matters

    Breaching the barrier causes two problems at once: fluid pours out, and organisms get in. In a large burn, plasma loss through the damaged surface and into the surrounding tissue is enough to cause hypovolemic shock over hours. Depth matters because it determines whether the structures that regenerate skin still exist.

    In the field

    • Superficial burns are red, painful, and blanch. Partial thickness are blistered, wet, and extremely painful. Full thickness are dry, leathery, white or charred, and painless because the nerves are destroyed.
    • A painless burn is worse than a painful one, which is the opposite of the intuition.
    • Circumferential burns act like a tourniquet as the tissue swells, and around the chest they restrict ventilation.

    Temperature regulation

    Failure mode: Core temperature drifts outside the survivable range

    HypothermiaHeat exhaustion and heat stroke

    A control system with a set point in the hypothalamus, sensors in the skin and core, and effectors that are mostly skin blood vessels, sweat glands, and skeletal muscle.

    What it does when it works

    • Core temperature is held near 37 degrees within a fraction of a degree.
    • To lose heat: vasodilation brings blood to the surface, and sweating removes heat as the water evaporates.
    • To conserve heat: vasoconstriction shunts blood away from the surface, and shivering generates heat by muscular work.
    • Heat is lost by radiation, conduction, convection, and evaporation. Wet skin and wind massively accelerate all of them.

    Why this structure matters

    Enzymes work within a narrow temperature range. Below about 35 degrees, function progressively fails: clotting slows, the heart becomes irritable, and consciousness falls. Above about 40, proteins begin to denature and cells die. Both extremes eventually disable the very mechanisms that were meant to correct them, which is why severe cases do not self-rescue.

    In the field

    • Shivering stops below roughly 32 degrees. A cold patient who has stopped shivering is worse, not better.
    • Sweating stops in classic heat stroke, so hot dry skin in a collapsed patient is an emergency.
    • Trauma patients become hypothermic quickly even in mild weather, and hypothermia impairs clotting directly. Cutting clothes off and leaving them exposed contributes to their death.
    • A severely hypothermic heart is extremely irritable and rough handling can precipitate ventricular fibrillation.

    Soft tissue and wounds

    Failure mode: Tissue is torn, crushed, or devitalised

    Thermal burnsElectrical injuryTraumatic amputation

    Skin, fat, fascia, and muscle considered together as the tissue that gets injured in most trauma. Different mechanisms damage it in characteristically different ways.

    What it does when it works

    • Bleeding is stopped by vessel constriction, then a platelet plug, then a fibrin clot.
    • Inflammation clears debris and organisms, then new tissue is laid down and remodelled over weeks.
    • Healing requires blood supply, so devitalised or heavily contaminated tissue does not heal.

    Why this structure matters

    The mechanism predicts the problem. Clean incisions bleed but heal well. Crush injuries devitalise tissue and release its contents into the circulation. Degloving separates skin from its blood supply so it dies even though it looks intact. Puncture wounds seal over and trap contamination underneath.

    In the field

    • An impaled object should be stabilised in place, not removed. It may be the only thing preventing catastrophic bleeding.
    • Amputated parts go in a dry sealed bag, then on ice. Never directly on ice and never in water, both of which destroy the tissue.
    • Wounds over joints, on the hands, or on the face have functional consequences out of proportion to their size.
    • Anything that bit or punctured deeply carries an infection and tetanus question that changes the destination.

    Pregnancy and childbirth

    Two patients, one of whom you cannot assess directly.

    Full page →

    Filed by the part that is physically damaged, not by diagnosis. The groups below are really a list of the ways this system fails.

    Uterus and placenta

    Failure mode: Bleeding faster than anywhere else in the body

    Normal childbirthObstetric hemorrhagePre-eclampsia and eclampsia

    A muscular organ that grows from the size of a pear to filling the abdomen, with the placenta embedded in its wall acting as the fetus's lungs, gut, and kidneys.

    What it does when it works

    • By term the uterus receives roughly 700 to 900 mL of blood every minute, close to a fifth of cardiac output.
    • The placenta exchanges gases and nutrients between two circulations that never actually mix.
    • After delivery, the uterus clamps down hard, and that muscular contraction is what physically closes the vessels at the placental site.
    • The uterus and placenta are essential to fetal survival but not to maternal survival.

    Why this structure matters

    Two facts govern every obstetric emergency. First, blood flow is enormous, so a bleeding uterus empties a patient faster than almost any other injury. Second, the mechanism that stops postpartum bleeding is muscular contraction rather than clotting, which is why a soft boggy uterus after delivery is a hemorrhage in progress and why massaging it works.

    In the field

    • The commonest cause of postpartum hemorrhage is a uterus that has not contracted. Firm fundal massage is an EMR-level intervention that genuinely stops bleeding.
    • After about twenty weeks, lying flat compresses the inferior vena cava against the spine and drops cardiac output substantially. Tilt the patient to the left or manually displace the uterus.
    • Placental abruption bleeds behind the placenta and may be largely concealed, so a rigid painful uterus with shock and little visible blood is an emergency.
    • Placenta praevia bleeds painlessly and bright red. Never perform a vaginal examination in third-trimester bleeding.

    Maternal physiology

    Failure mode: Compensation hides deterioration until it's late

    Normal childbirthObstetric hemorrhage

    Pregnancy alters almost every system. Understanding those changes is what stops you being falsely reassured by a pregnant patient's vital signs.

    What it does when it works

    • Blood volume rises by 30 to 50 percent, with plasma increasing more than red cells, so the hemoglobin concentration falls even though total oxygen carrying capacity rises.
    • Cardiac output rises by around 40 percent, mostly through increased stroke volume, and resting heart rate rises by 10 to 20 beats.
    • Blood pressure falls slightly in the second trimester because systemic vascular resistance drops.
    • Tidal volume rises substantially, the diaphragm is pushed up, functional residual capacity falls, and oxygen consumption increases by around 20 percent.
    • Blood becomes more prone to clotting, which protects against delivery hemorrhage and raises thromboembolism risk.

    Why this structure matters

    The extra blood volume means a pregnant patient can lose 1,200 to 1,500 mL before showing the usual signs of shock, and the fetus is compromised long before the mother's numbers move. Meanwhile reduced functional residual capacity plus higher oxygen consumption means she desaturates far faster than a non-pregnant patient when breathing is interrupted.

    In the field

    • Normal-looking vital signs in a bleeding pregnant patient are not reassuring. The uterus is sacrificed first to protect the mother, so fetal distress precedes maternal signs.
    • Treat the mother and you treat the fetus. Oxygen, position, and volume for her are the best fetal resuscitation available.
    • Left lateral tilt after twenty weeks, always.
    • The airway is more oedematous and more prone to bleeding, and desaturation is fast. Preoxygenate well before anything that interrupts breathing.
