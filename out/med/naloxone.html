    Medication · Competitive opioid receptor antagonist

    Naloxone (Narcan)

    Physically displaces opioids from their receptors, switching the respiratory drive back on.

    EMR  Widely within EMR scope in Alberta, and available to the public through community take-home kits. Route carried varies by service.

    Mechanism

    Naloxone has a higher affinity for the mu opioid receptor than most opioids do, but no activity of its own once it is there. It simply occupies the site and pushes the opioid off. Because the receptor is now blocked rather than stimulated, the brainstem's response to carbon dioxide is restored and the patient starts breathing again. It is pure competition, which is exactly why the outcome depends on the relative amounts and durations of the two drugs.

    Why it works at that link and not another

    It acts at the top of the cascade rather than partway down. Oxygen treats the hypoxia, ventilation treats the hypoventilation, but naloxone removes the cause. That said, it is not the most urgent intervention: a patient who is being ventilated properly is not dying, whereas a patient waiting for naloxone to work is still apneic for several minutes. Ventilate first, then reverse.

    Dosing

    RouteDoseNotes
    Intranasal2 to 4 mgHalf the volume in each nostril for better absorption. Poor uptake if the nose is full of blood or secretions. Most common route in take-home kits.
    Intramuscular0.4 to 2 mgReliable absorption even when perfusion is poor. Anterolateral thigh or deltoid.
    Intravenous0.04 to 0.4 mg, titratedFastest onset and easiest to titrate. Small increments avoid precipitating violent withdrawal. Outside EMR scope.
    Repeat dosingEvery 2 to 3 minutes as neededFentanyl analogues and carfentanil frequently need several doses. No response after adequate total dose means another cause.

    Target: Adequate spontaneous ventilation, not a fully alert patient. Aim for a respiratory rate above about 10 with reasonable depth, and stop there.

    Timing

    This is the part that tells you when to reassess, and it is the part most people never learn properly.

    Onset
    1 to 2 minutes intravenously, 2 to 5 minutes intramuscular or intranasal.
    Peak
    Around 5 to 10 minutes.
    Duration
    30 to 90 minutes, which is frequently shorter than the opioid it reversed.

    What working looks like

    • Respiratory rate and depth increase, usually the first thing to change
    • Colour improves and saturation climbs
    • Level of consciousness rises, often lagging behind the breathing
    • Pupils dilate from pinpoint back toward normal

    What it means if nothing happens

    If there is no response to an adequate total dose, the diagnosis is wrong or incomplete. Consider benzodiazepines, alcohol, or other sedatives which naloxone does not touch; head injury from the collapse; hypoglycemia; hypoxic brain injury that has already occurred; or a post-arrest state. Keep ventilating regardless.

    Side effects are the drug doing its job elsewhere

    Almost every adverse effect of naloxone is simply the absence of opioid, arriving too abruptly. Nausea, vomiting, sweating, tremor, tachycardia, hypertension, agitation, and pain are all what the patient's nervous system does when the drug it was saturated with vanishes in ninety seconds. None of it is toxicity, and all of it is avoidable by giving less, more slowly, and aiming at the breathing rather than the eyes.

    Cautions

    Its duration is shorter than most opioids

    This is the single most important fact about naloxone. Long-acting opioids, slow-release preparations, and potent fentanyl analogues all outlast it. A patient who wakes up, feels fine, and walks away can re-sedate an hour later with nobody there. Refusal of transport after naloxone is the commonest way these patients die.

    Precipitated withdrawal

    Reversing too much too fast in a dependent patient causes acute withdrawal: vomiting, agitation, aggression, pain, and diarrhea. It is not usually dangerous in itself, but the vomiting threatens an airway that was already compromised, and the agitation frequently ends with the patient leaving. Titrate to breathing, not to consciousness.

    Opioid-dependent patients in pain

    Full reversal removes analgesia entirely, which in a patient with a genuine painful condition is both cruel and clinically counterproductive.

    Pulmonary edema after reversal

    Uncommon but recognised, particularly after rapid full reversal. The mechanism is not fully settled but the catecholamine surge is implicated.
