    Oxygen (O2)

    Raises the pressure gradient driving oxygen across the alveolar membrane. It does not fix anything upstream of that.

    EMR  Core EMR skill in Alberta, including pulse oximetry to guide it.

    Mechanism

    Room air is about 21 percent oxygen. Raising the inspired fraction raises the partial pressure of oxygen in the alveolus, which increases the gradient across the alveolar-capillary membrane. Diffusion is driven purely by that gradient, so more pressure means more oxygen crossing per second. That is the entire mechanism. There is no receptor and no metabolic action.

    Why it works at that link and not another

    It is a downstream intervention. It does not open an airway, remove fluid from an alveolus, restore surface area, or move blood. It compensates for a delivery problem by pushing harder against whatever is in the way, which buys time for the actual fix to arrive or take effect.

    Dosing

    RouteDoseNotes
    Nasal cannula1 to 6 L/minDelivers roughly 24 to 44 percent. Comfortable, allows speech and eating.
    Simple face mask6 to 10 L/minRoughly 35 to 60 percent. Never run below 6 L/min or the patient rebreathes their own CO2.
    Non-rebreather mask10 to 15 L/minRoughly 60 to 90 percent. Inflate the reservoir bag before applying and keep it from collapsing on inspiration.
    Bag-valve-mask with reservoir15 L/minApproaching 100 percent. This is ventilation as well as oxygenation, and it is a different intervention.

    Target: Titrate to an SpO2 of 94 to 98 percent for most patients. Alberta first-response protocols use an SpO2 below 94 percent on room air as the trigger to start oxygen. For patients with COPD who retain CO2, target 88 to 92 percent instead.

    Timing

    This is the part that tells you when to reassess, and it is the part most people never learn properly.

    Onset
    Seconds to a minute, limited mainly by how long it takes to wash out the existing gas in the lungs and airways.
    Peak
    Within a few minutes.
    Duration
    Only while it is being delivered. There is no reservoir effect and no residual action.

    What working looks like

    • SpO2 climbing within one to two minutes
    • Reduced anxiety and restlessness, often before the number moves
    • Improving level of consciousness
    • Cyanosis fading, though this lags well behind the saturation

    What it means if nothing happens

    If saturation does not improve on high-flow oxygen, the problem is not a gradient problem. Suspect a shunt where blood is bypassing ventilated lung entirely, a perfusion problem such as a pulmonary embolism, a mechanical problem such as a tension pneumothorax, or a hemoglobin problem such as carbon monoxide poisoning where the pulse oximeter is lying to you.

    Side effects are the drug doing its job elsewhere

    Oxygen has very few side effects at prehospital timescales, because the doses are short and the mechanism is passive. Problems that do occur come from the same mechanism working too well: suppressed respiratory drive in the CO2 retainer, absorption atelectasis over long exposures, and drying of mucosa when unhumidified gas runs at high flow for a long time.

    Cautions

    COPD with chronic CO2 retention

    A small group of long-standing COPD patients rely more on hypoxic drive and on ventilation-perfusion matching that high oxygen disrupts. Excess oxygen can worsen their CO2 retention. Target 88 to 92 percent rather than withholding oxygen. Never withhold oxygen from a hypoxic patient because they have COPD.

    Carbon monoxide poisoning

    A standard pulse oximeter cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin and will read falsely high. Give high-flow oxygen regardless of what the number says.

    Uncomplicated chest pain and stroke with normal saturation

    Routine oxygen in normoxic patients has not been shown to help and may cause harm. Treat the saturation, not the diagnosis.
