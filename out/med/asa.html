    Medication · Antiplatelet

    Acetylsalicylic acid (Aspirin, ASA)

    Permanently disables the platelets that are building a clot in a coronary artery.

    EMR  Within EMR scope in Alberta for suspected cardiac chest pain. Alberta first-response protocols use 160 mg chewed.

    Mechanism

    Aspirin irreversibly inhibits the cyclo-oxygenase-1 enzyme inside platelets by acetylating it. That enzyme makes thromboxane A2, which is the signal platelets use to recruit and activate each other. Without it, a platelet cannot participate in aggregation. Crucially, platelets have no nucleus and cannot manufacture new enzyme, so the effect lasts for the platelet's entire lifespan of about seven to ten days rather than for the few hours the drug is present.

    Why it works at that link and not another

    It attacks the clot at the moment it is being built, not after. Aspirin cannot dissolve a clot that already exists and cannot reopen the artery. What it does is stop the clot growing and stop it re-forming, which is why timing matters so much and why it is given before almost anything else. It is one of the highest-value single interventions in prehospital medicine, and it is a chewed tablet.

    Dosing

    RouteDoseNotes
    Oral, chewed160 mgAlberta first-response protocol dose. Other jurisdictions use 162 mg as two 81 mg tablets, or up to 324 mg.
    Why chewedNot swallowed wholeChewing gives buccal absorption and a far faster onset than waiting for a tablet to dissolve in the stomach. Enteric-coated tablets should still be chewed if that is all you have.

    Target: Given as early as possible in suspected acute coronary syndrome. There is no titration and no repeat dose.

    Timing

    This is the part that tells you when to reassess, and it is the part most people never learn properly.

    Onset
    Measurable platelet inhibition within about 15 to 30 minutes when chewed.
    Peak
    Around 1 hour.
    Duration
    7 to 10 days, the lifespan of the affected platelets.

    What working looks like

    • Nothing you can see or measure in the field
    • Its benefit is a reduction in mortality, demonstrated in large trials, not a change in the patient in front of you
    • Do not judge it by whether the pain improves, because it will not

    What it means if nothing happens

    This is one of the few drugs where the absence of a visible response tells you nothing at all. Pain relief comes from nitroglycerin and from reperfusion, not from aspirin. Never withhold or repeat it based on symptoms.

    Side effects are the drug doing its job elsewhere

    Everything unwanted about aspirin comes from the same enzyme inhibition working somewhere other than the coronary platelets. Stomach irritation and bleeding happen because cyclo-oxygenase also maintains the gastric mucus barrier. Easy bruising and prolonged bleeding from cuts happen because platelets everywhere are disabled, not just the ones in the coronary artery. Bronchospasm in aspirin-sensitive asthmatics occurs because blocking the cyclo-oxygenase pathway shunts arachidonic acid down the leukotriene pathway instead, and leukotrienes are potent bronchoconstrictors. One enzyme, one mechanism, several different tissues.

    Cautions

    Known true aspirin allergy

    Genuine allergy exists and can cause anaphylaxis or severe bronchospasm. Ask specifically, and distinguish real allergy from the far more common report of stomach upset.

    Active gastrointestinal bleeding

    Aspirin inhibits the same enzyme in the stomach lining that maintains its protective mucus layer, and disables platelets that would otherwise stop the bleeding. A patient actively vomiting blood or passing melaena should not receive it.

    Suspected aortic dissection

    If the chest pain is a dissection rather than an infarct, disabling the clotting response is actively harmful. Tearing pain that is maximal at onset, radiating to the back, or with unequal pulses should give you pause.

    Suspected hemorrhagic stroke

    The same reasoning. Aspirin is for suspected coronary events, not for undifferentiated neurological presentations.

    Children and teenagers with a viral illness

    Reye's syndrome, a rare but often fatal encephalopathy and liver failure. Aspirin is essentially never given to children in this context.

    Reduced consciousness or unsafe swallow

    It is an oral tablet. A patient who cannot protect their airway cannot safely take it.

    Used in

    Acute coronary syndrome
