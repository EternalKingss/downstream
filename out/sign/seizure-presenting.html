    Seizure

    The convulsion is rarely what harms them; the airway and the cause are.

    What produces it, and how to tell them apart

    Ordered roughly by how quickly each will kill. The clue is what separates it from the others on this list.

    Seizure and status epilepticus

    Known epilepsy, missed medication, recognisable pattern, and a postictal phase that steadily improves.

    Hypoglycemia

    Low glucose, sweating. Fully reversible and must be excluded in every case.

    Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia

    Pregnant or delivered within six weeks. Hypertension, headache, visual changes. Needs magnesium rather than a benzodiazepine.

    Traumatic brain injury

    Seizure after head injury is a significant finding and suggests structural damage.

    Hemorrhagic stroke

    Seizure at onset with sudden severe headache.

    Meningitis and meningococcal sepsis

    Fever, headache, neck stiffness, non-blanching rash.

    Alcohol intoxication and withdrawal

    Withdrawal seizures typically 12 to 48 hours after the last drink, and a marker for impending delirium tremens.

    Stimulant toxicity

    Agitation, hyperthermia, dilated pupils, recent use.

    Sudden cardiac arrest

    Arrest commonly causes a brief convulsion. Always check a pulse in anyone who collapsed and convulsed.

    Heat exhaustion and heat stroke

    Hot environment or exertion with altered mental state and high core temperature.

    Cholinergic toxicity

    Pinpoint pupils with copious secretions, fasciculation, and an exposure history.

    Test yourself

    First moves

    • Protect from injury, do not restrain, and put nothing in the mouth.
    • Time it with an actual clock. People consistently overestimate duration and the number determines treatment.
    • Recovery position and suction the moment convulsing stops.
    • Check glucose.
    • In a pregnant or recently delivered patient, treat as eclampsia until proven otherwise.

    What this sign actually means

    Excitation has overwhelmed inhibition somewhere in the brain and the discharge has spread. The seizure itself is usually self-limiting. What matters is protecting the airway during and after, timing it accurately, and finding the cause, because several causes are lethal and reversible.
