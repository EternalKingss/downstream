    Pressure rises inside a closed compartment

    Swelling inside an inelastic fascial sheath squeezes the capillaries shut.

    What is actually happening

    Bleeding or oedema inside a fascial compartment raises the pressure within it. Fascia does not stretch, so the pressure rises until it exceeds capillary pressure and perfusion through the muscle stops. Arteries have far higher pressure and stay open, so a distal pulse persists while the muscle inside is dying.

    Why it follows from almost anything

    It follows fractures, crush injury, tight casts or dressings, circumferential burns, prolonged limb compression, and reperfusion after restored circulation.

    What you see

    • Pain far out of proportion to the apparent injury, and the earliest reliable sign
    • Severe pain on passive stretching of the muscles in that compartment
    • A compartment that feels tense and woody rather than soft
    • Paraesthesia in the nerves running through the compartment
    • Pallor, paralysis, and pulselessness, all of which are very late
    The trap

    The presence of a distal pulse is the single most dangerous false reassurance in limb trauma. Capillary pressure is far lower than arterial pressure, so muscle can be completely ischemic with a strong, easily palpable pulse. Muscle dies within four to six hours and the damage is irreversible. Never rule this out because you can feel the foot.
