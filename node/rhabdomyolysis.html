    Damaged muscle releases its contents

    Dying muscle dumps potassium, myoglobin, and acid into the circulation.

    What is actually happening

    Muscle cells hold enormous amounts of potassium, along with myoglobin, phosphate, and organic acids. When large volumes of muscle are damaged and then reperfused, all of it enters the bloodstream at once. Potassium disrupts cardiac conduction directly, and myoglobin precipitates in the kidney tubules and causes renal failure.

    Why it follows from almost anything

    It follows crush injury, prolonged compression from lying immobile, compartment syndrome, extensive burns, electrocution, severe exertion, prolonged seizure, and stimulant toxicity with hyperthermia.

    What you see

    • Swollen, tender, weak muscles
    • Dark tea-coloured or cola-coloured urine from myoglobin
    • Cardiac arrhythmias from rising potassium, which may be the first sign
    • Shock from fluid sequestering into damaged muscle
    • A patient who was stable while trapped and deteriorates on release
    The trap

    The dangerous moment is release, not entrapment. While a limb is compressed, the toxic load stays locally sequestered. Restoring circulation washes it all into the central circulation at once, and patients have arrested within minutes of being freed. This is why prolonged extrication is coordinated carefully and why the receiving hospital needs warning before the release, not after.
