    Skin turns blue

    Enough hemoglobin is running empty that you can see it through the skin.

    What is actually happening

    Oxygenated hemoglobin is bright red; deoxygenated hemoglobin is dark blue-purple. Cyanosis becomes visible when roughly 50 grams per litre of hemoglobin in the capillary bed is desaturated.

    Why it follows from almost anything

    It is a direct visual readout of desaturated hemoglobin, so it appears downstream of any cause of hypoxia, regardless of which one.

    What you see

    • Central cyanosis around the lips, tongue, and oral mucosa, which reflects true arterial desaturation
    • Peripheral cyanosis in the fingers, toes, and nail beds, which can also be caused by cold or poor perfusion alone
    The trap

    Cyanosis depends on the absolute amount of desaturated hemoglobin, not the percentage. A severely anemic patient may be dangerously hypoxic and never turn blue, because they do not have enough hemoglobin to reach the visible threshold. A patient with carbon monoxide poisoning may be cherry red while their tissues suffocate. Never rule out hypoxia because the patient looks pink.
