    The brain runs out of fuel

    Glucose is the brain's only usable fuel, and it stores almost none.

    What is actually happening

    Unlike muscle, the brain cannot burn fat and cannot store meaningful glycogen. It depends on a continuous supply of glucose from the blood, using around 120 grams a day. When blood glucose falls, brain function fails within minutes, well before any other organ notices.

    Why it follows from almost anything

    It follows too much insulin or oral hypoglycemic medication, missed meals, unusual exertion, alcohol, sepsis, liver failure, and adrenal insufficiency.

    What you see

    • Sympathetic warning signs first: sweating, tremor, pallor, hunger, anxiety, palpitations
    • Then confusion, slurred speech, and odd or aggressive behaviour
    • Focal deficits that can mimic a stroke exactly, including one-sided weakness
    • Seizures and unresponsiveness if it continues
    The trap

    Hypoglycemia imitates almost everything: stroke, intoxication, psychiatric emergency, head injury. It is also completely reversible within minutes. That combination is why glucose is checked on every patient with altered mental status, every time, without exception. Patients on beta blockers may lose the sympathetic warning signs entirely and go straight to confusion, and long-standing diabetics can develop the same hypoglycemia unawareness.
