    Ventilation and perfusion stop matching

    Air arrives where there is no blood, or blood arrives where there is no air.

    What is actually happening

    Gas exchange requires air and blood to meet at the same alveolus at the same time. Normally the lung matches them closely, diverting blood away from poorly ventilated regions by hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. When that matching breaks, some blood passes through the lung without ever meeting air, and some air enters alveoli that no blood is visiting.

    Why it follows from almost anything

    Blocked airways create ventilated-but-unreachable regions. Flooded or collapsed alveoli create perfused-but-unventilated regions, which is shunt. Blocked pulmonary vessels create ventilated-but-unperfused regions, which is dead space. Almost every lung condition produces one or the other.

    What you see

    • Hypoxia that is out of proportion to how the chest sounds or how hard the patient appears to be working
    • A patient who looks worse than their examination suggests, particularly in pulmonary embolism
    • Carbon dioxide that stays normal or low early, because the patient compensates by breathing faster
    • Saturation that improves only partially on oxygen when true shunt is present
    The trap

    How well the patient responds to oxygen tells you which problem you have. Simple mismatch improves substantially with supplemental oxygen. True shunt, where blood passes entirely unventilated lung, barely improves at all no matter how much you give, because the oxygen never reaches that blood. A hypoxic patient who does not respond to high-flow oxygen has a shunt, an obstruction, or a perfusion problem, and needs a different intervention rather than more of the same.
