    Intracranial pressure rises

    Something is taking up space inside a box that cannot expand.

    What is actually happening

    The skull contains brain, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid in a fixed total volume. Adding anything, whether a hematoma, a tumour, or swollen tissue, means something else must be displaced. The body first squeezes out cerebrospinal fluid and venous blood, which buys a surprising amount of room. Once that is used up, pressure climbs very steeply for very small further increases in volume.

    Why it follows from almost anything

    It follows any bleed inside the skull, any significant brain swelling from trauma or hypoxia, any large stroke, any mass, and any infection causing cerebral edema.

    What you see

    • Headache, often described as the worst ever, and vomiting that may be sudden and without nausea
    • Deteriorating level of consciousness, which is the most sensitive sign and the one to track
    • Restlessness and confusion progressing to drowsiness
    • Pupil changes as pressure reaches the brainstem
    • In infants, a bulging fontanelle and a high-pitched cry
    The trap

    The compensation phase is why the lucid interval exists. A patient with an epidural bleed can be knocked out, wake up, hold a conversation, and appear entirely normal while a hematoma quietly expands. When compensation runs out they deteriorate within minutes. A normal exam after a head injury does not mean the patient is safe, and repeated assessment is the whole game.
