    Bleeding you cannot see

    Blood is leaving the circulation into a body cavity, and nothing appears on the outside.

    What is actually happening

    The chest, abdomen, retroperitoneum, pelvis, and thighs can each accommodate litres of blood without visible distension. The blood is as lost to the circulation as if it were on the floor, and there is nothing to apply pressure to.

    Why it follows from almost anything

    It follows solid organ injury, pelvic and femoral fractures, ruptured aortic aneurysm, ectopic pregnancy, gastrointestinal bleeding, and hemothorax.

    What you see

    • Shock that does not match any visible injury
    • Tachycardia with pale, cool, clammy skin and a narrowing pulse pressure
    • Abdominal or flank tenderness, or an abdomen that seems to be distending
    • Bruising over the flanks or umbilicus, which is a late sign
    • A mechanism of injury that should have caused more damage than you can see
    The trap

    The classic error is being reassured by the absence of external blood. If the patient is showing signs of shock and there is nothing on the outside, the bleeding is internal until proven otherwise, and no field intervention will stop it. These patients need a surgeon, and the only variable you control is time.
