    The peritoneum becomes inflamed

    Something has spilled or ruptured, and the membrane lining the abdomen is reacting to it.

    What is actually happening

    Blood, bowel contents, bile, pancreatic enzymes, urine, or pus contact the peritoneum. The visceral layer gives vague central pain; once the parietal layer lining the abdominal wall is involved, the pain becomes sharp, localised, and worsened by any movement that stretches it.

    Why it follows from almost anything

    It follows perforation of any hollow organ, rupture of a solid organ, appendicitis, pancreatitis, ectopic pregnancy, and penetrating abdominal trauma.

    What you see

    • Pain that started vague and central and has become sharp and localised
    • Involuntary guarding, where the abdominal wall tenses without the patient choosing to
    • Rigidity, described as board-like in advanced cases
    • A patient who lies completely still, often with knees drawn up, and does not want to be moved
    • Pain on coughing, on speed bumps, on any jolt
    The trap

    The peritoneum has a surface area comparable to the skin, and inflamed peritoneum weeps fluid across all of it. Several litres can be sequestered into the abdomen, producing genuine hypovolemic shock with nothing lost externally. Treat peritonitis as a volume loss problem as well as a pain problem.
