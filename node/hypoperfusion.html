    Tissue perfusion falls

    Blood is no longer arriving fast enough to meet what the tissue is spending.

    What is actually happening

    Perfusion is flow through the capillary bed, and it depends on cardiac output and on the vessel tone directing that output. When flow falls below metabolic demand, cells begin drawing down reserves. This is the actual definition of shock, and note that blood pressure does not appear in it anywhere.

    Why it follows from almost anything

    There are only four ways to get here, and every kind of shock is one of them: not enough volume in the tank, a pump that will not pump, pipes that are too wide, or something mechanically obstructing the circuit.

    What you see

    • Delayed capillary refill, the one bedside sign that looks directly at this level rather than at a proxy
    • Pale, cool, clammy skin as flow is diverted away from the periphery
    • Rising heart rate
    • Anxiety and restlessness from early cerebral hypoperfusion
    • Falling urine output, which you will rarely measure but which is happening
    The trap

    Blood pressure is not perfusion. It is a proxy, and in early shock it is a proxy that lies. A patient can clamp down hard enough to keep a textbook-normal pressure while their gut, kidneys, and skin are already starving. Treat the patient in front of you, not the number.
