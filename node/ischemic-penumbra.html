    Brain tissue starves but isn't dead yet

    A ring of tissue around the dead core that is salvageable, and shrinking.

    What is actually happening

    When a cerebral artery blocks, the tissue at the centre of its territory loses flow completely and dies within minutes. Around it lies a zone kept marginally alive by collateral vessels: not working, but not yet dead. This is the penumbra, and it is what reperfusion treatment saves.

    Why it follows from almost anything

    It follows any acute occlusion of a cerebral vessel, and it exists for a limited and shrinking window afterward.

    What you see

    • A neurological deficit that is present but has not yet become permanent
    • Deficits that fluctuate slightly, as marginal collateral flow varies with blood pressure
    • Nothing visible that distinguishes salvageable tissue from dead tissue at the bedside, which is exactly why imaging and time matter
    The trap

    This node is the entire reason stroke is a time-critical emergency rather than a diagnosis to be sorted out at hospital. Roughly two million neurons die every minute in a large vessel occlusion. Everything prehospital, including establishing an accurate last-known-well time, exists to protect this tissue.
