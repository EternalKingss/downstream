    Condition · Meninges and cerebrospinal fluid, Cerebral blood vessels

    Hemorrhagic stroke

    A vessel in or around the brain ruptures, and blood becomes a space-occupying lesion.

    In one breath

    Bleeding into a closed box does two things at once: it deprives tissue downstream of supply, and it takes up room that nothing has. The pressure problem usually kills faster than the ischemia does.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    A vessel ruptures
    Hypertensive bleed or aneurysm

    Two main patterns. Intracerebral hemorrhage is bleeding directly into brain tissue, usually from small vessels damaged by years of hypertension. Subarachnoid hemorrhage is bleeding into the space around the brain, usually from a ruptured berry aneurysm, and classically announces itself with a thunderclap headache that is maximal at the instant of onset.

    anatomy → Cerebral blood vessels
    Blood occupies space
    Nothing can be displaced fast enough

    The skull is full. Blood arriving inside it displaces cerebrospinal fluid and venous blood until that compensation is exhausted, then pressure rises steeply. Unlike an ischemic stroke, this creates a pressure emergency within minutes rather than an edema problem over days.

    shared node → Intracranial pressure rises

    Head elevated 30 degrees, neck neutral EMR

    Improves venous drainage from the head, which lowers intracranial blood volume and therefore pressure. Keep collars and straps from compressing the jugular veins, because a tight collar can measurably raise intracranial pressure.

    Keep them calm and minimise stimulation EMR

    Pain, agitation, coughing, gagging, and suctioning all spike intracranial pressure. Quiet handling is a treatment.

    Tissue beyond the rupture starves
    Deficit appears

    The ruptured vessel no longer delivers blood to its territory, so there is an ischemic component on top of the pressure component. Subarachnoid bleeds additionally cause vasospasm in surrounding arteries over the following days, producing delayed ischemia.

    Cerebral perfusion pressure falls
    Squeezed from both sides

    Perfusion pressure is mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. Intracranial pressure is now climbing, so perfusion falls unless arterial pressure rises to match. The body's answer is to raise the blood pressure, which is exactly why these patients are hypertensive.

    shared node → Brain blood flow falls

    Oxygen EMR

    Maintain saturation above 94 percent. Hypoxia causes cerebral vasodilation, which increases blood volume inside the skull, which raises intracranial pressure further.

    Avoid hypotension absolutely EMR

    With intracranial pressure already raised, any drop in arterial pressure collapses cerebral perfusion. A single hypotensive episode measurably worsens outcome.

    Avoid overventilating EMR

    Hyperventilation lowers CO2, which constricts cerebral vessels and lowers pressure, but it does so by reducing blood flow to a brain that is already starving. Routine hyperventilation causes harm. Ventilate at a normal rate.

    Brain tissue is forced out of place
    Pupil changes, posturing, Cushing's

    Sustained pressure pushes brain past the rigid folds of dura and toward the foramen magnum, compressing the brainstem on the way. This is what actually kills these patients.

    shared node → Brain tissue is forced out of place

    Serial neurological assessment EMR

    Level of consciousness, pupils, and limb movement, repeated and documented with times. The trend matters more than any single reading, and it is the information the neurosurgical team needs most.

    Brainstem function stops
    Breathing ceases

    The respiratory and cardiovascular centres are the last thing compressed and the point at which the process becomes irreversible.

    shared node → Breathing stops
    Stop and escalate
    • Sudden severe headache described as the worst of their life, or as instantly maximal
    • Vomiting with headache
    • Any deterioration in level of consciousness
    • Unequal or unreactive pupils
    • Rising blood pressure with falling pulse and irregular breathing, which is late herniation
    • Seizure at onset
    • Posturing

    Telling it apart

    Not Ischemic stroke?

    Not reliably distinguishable in the field, and both go to a stroke centre urgently. Hemorrhage is suggested by sudden severe headache, vomiting, rapid decline in consciousness, and seizure at onset.

    Not Migraine?

    Migraine builds over minutes to hours and usually has a history of similar episodes. A headache that is instantly maximal is a subarachnoid hemorrhage until proven otherwise.

    Not Meningitis?

    Fever, neck stiffness, photophobia, and a rash, developing over hours. Subarachnoid hemorrhage can also cause neck stiffness from blood irritating the meninges, but without fever and with an abrupt onset.
