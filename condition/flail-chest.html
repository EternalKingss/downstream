    Condition · Chest wall and diaphragm

    Flail chest

    A segment of ribs broken in two places moves opposite to the rest of the chest, wrecking the bellows.

    In one breath

    A free-floating chunk of chest wall gets sucked in when it should move out. Ventilation suffers from the mechanics, but the real damage is the bruised lung underneath, which worsens over hours.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Ribs break in two places each
    Three or more adjacent ribs

    Significant blunt force fractures three or more adjacent ribs in at least two places, creating a segment no longer connected to the rest of the rib cage. It takes considerable energy in a young person; in an elderly patient with osteoporotic bone, far less.

    anatomy → Chest wall and diaphragm
    The segment moves paradoxically
    In on inspiration, out on expiration

    Because the segment is no longer anchored, it follows pressure rather than the rest of the chest wall. It is drawn inward when intrathoracic pressure drops during inspiration and pushed outward during expiration. This may be hidden initially by muscle spasm and become apparent only as the patient tires.

    Ventilation becomes inefficient
    Effort without volume

    Some of the pressure the patient generates is wasted moving the flail segment instead of moving air. Tidal volume falls for the same effort, and the work of breathing rises substantially.

    Do not strap or tape the segment EMR

    Old teaching, long abandoned. Strapping the chest restricts ventilation of the whole side and worsens the underlying contusion. Support the segment with a hand or a soft pad if it helps the patient, but do not bind the chest.

    Position with the injured side down if tolerated EMR

    Splints the flail segment against the stretcher and lets the uninjured lung ventilate more freely. Only if there is no spinal concern and the patient tolerates it.

    Pain prevents deep breaths
    Massively underestimated

    Multiple rib fractures are severely painful, and the patient splints: shallow breaths, suppressed cough, no deep inspiration. Retained secretions and progressive collapse of small airways follow, which is why analgesia is genuinely a respiratory intervention here.

    Analgesia PCP

    Genuinely a respiratory intervention rather than a comfort measure. A patient who can take a deep breath and cough clears secretions and avoids collapse. Outside EMR scope but worth requesting early.

    The lung underneath is bruised
    The actual problem

    The force that broke the ribs also bruised the lung beneath them. Blood and fluid leak into the alveoli and interstitium, and this worsens progressively over the first twelve to twenty-four hours. Pulmonary contusion, not the flail segment, is the main determinant of how these patients do.

    anatomy → Alveoli

    Expect deterioration and reassess EMR

    The contusion peaks over twelve to twenty-four hours. A patient who looks acceptable on scene may be substantially worse by arrival.

    Contused lung is perfused but not ventilated
    Hypoxia

    Blood flows past bruised, fluid-filled alveoli and returns unoxygenated. Hypoxia is often progressive over hours rather than immediate, so an initially stable patient can deteriorate significantly during a long transport.

    shared node → Ventilation and perfusion stop matching

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration. The contusion will worsen over hours, so reassess frequently rather than assuming a stable early saturation will hold.

    The patient tires
    Rising CO2

    High work of breathing, pain, and worsening lung injury together exhaust the patient. When effort falls and carbon dioxide climbs, they need their breathing supported.

    shared node → Respiratory muscles fail

    Assist ventilation if they tire EMR

    Positive pressure splints the flail internally and supports the failing bellows. Watch closely for pneumothorax, which is common alongside flail chest and can become a tension under positive pressure.

    Stop and escalate
    • Visible paradoxical chest movement
    • Falling saturation despite oxygen
    • Rising respiratory rate then falling effort
    • Unilateral absent breath sounds, suggesting an associated pneumothorax
    • Shock, which suggests hemothorax or another bleeding source
    • Any deterioration after starting positive pressure ventilation

    Telling it apart

    Not Simple rib fractures?

    Simple fractures hurt and cause splinting but do not move paradoxically. The management overlap is large, and both need good analgesia and monitoring for contusion.

    Not Tension pneumothorax?

    Both deteriorate after trauma. Tension has unilaterally absent breath sounds with distended neck veins and shock; flail chest has visible paradoxical movement with progressive hypoxia.

    Not Isolated pulmonary contusion?

    Contusion can occur with no flail segment at all, particularly in children whose flexible ribs transmit force without breaking. Suspect it from mechanism and worsening hypoxia.
