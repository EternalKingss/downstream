    Condition · Myocardium, Coronary arteries

    Acute coronary syndrome

    A plaque ruptures in a coronary artery, a clot forms on it, and the muscle downstream begins to die.

    In one breath

    Cholesterol plaque has been narrowing a coronary artery for decades without symptoms. Its cap tears, the body treats the tear like an injury and clots over it, and that clot blocks the artery in minutes. Everything the artery fed now has a countdown on it.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Plaque has been building for years
    Silent until it isn't

    Lipid, inflammatory cells, and fibrous tissue accumulate in the arterial wall over decades. A vessel can narrow by half without causing any symptom at all, because flow at rest is still adequate. Some patients get warning in the form of exertional angina; many get none whatsoever, and the infarct is the first thing that happens to them.

    anatomy → Coronary arteries
    The plaque cap tears
    The trigger event

    The fibrous cap over the plaque ruptures or erodes, exposing the thrombogenic material underneath to flowing blood. Crucially, the plaques that rupture are often not the most severely narrowed ones. This is why a patient with a normal stress test last year can still infarct today.

    A clot forms over the tear
    Platelets arrive within seconds

    The body cannot distinguish a torn plaque from a cut vessel, so it does exactly what it does for any injury: platelets adhere, activate, and aggregate, and the clotting cascade lays fibrin over them. In a coronary artery, that perfectly appropriate response is the thing that kills the patient. A partial clot causes unstable angina or a NSTEMI; a complete occlusion causes a STEMI.

    Acetylsalicylic acid EMR

    Blocks platelet aggregation irreversibly, which limits how much bigger the clot can get. It cannot dissolve what is already there. This is the single highest-value drug an EMR gives, and it is given early for exactly that reason.

    The muscle starves
    Pressure, radiation, sweating, doom

    Myocardium already extracts most of the oxygen available to it, so it has no reserve to draw on. Within seconds of occlusion the affected muscle switches to anaerobic metabolism, lactate builds, and the tissue signals pain through autonomic fibres, which is why the pain is poorly localised and often referred to the jaw, arm, or back.

    shared node → Heart muscle starves

    Nitroglycerin scope varies

    Dilates veins, drops preload, and reduces the work the heart has to do, which narrows the gap between supply and demand. Also dilates coronary vessels somewhat. Check right-sided involvement and PDE5 inhibitor use first.

    Oxygen EMR

    Only if the saturation is below 94 percent. Routine oxygen in a normoxic cardiac patient has not been shown to help and may cause harm.

    Rest and stop all exertion EMR

    Reduces myocardial oxygen demand directly. Carry the patient. Do not let them walk to the ambulance, climb stairs, or move themselves onto the stretcher.

    Calm and reassure EMR

    Anxiety drives catecholamine release, which raises rate and contractility, which raises oxygen demand in a heart that cannot meet it. This is a physiological intervention.

    The muscle becomes electrically unstable
    The main killer in hour one

    Ischemic myocardium has an unstable membrane potential and conducts unevenly. Some regions are dead, some are dying, some are normal, and impulses circle between them. This is the substrate for ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation, and it is why most deaths from infarction happen in the first hour, before any pump problem has developed.

    anatomy → Conduction system

    AED on the patient early EMR

    The most likely thing to kill this patient in the next hour is a rhythm you can shock. Pads on before they arrest, not after.

    Muscle dies
    Roughly 20 minutes to 6 hours

    Cells begin dying within twenty to thirty minutes of complete occlusion, working outward from the inner layer of the ventricle where pressure is highest and supply worst. Infarction is largely complete by around six hours. What is lost becomes scar: it does not contract and it conducts badly. Everything prehospital is about shortening this window.

    Rapid transport to a PCI centre EMR

    Nothing you carry reopens the artery. The intervention is time, and the destination decision matters more than almost anything you do on scene.

    The pump weakens
    If enough muscle is lost

    Once roughly a quarter of the left ventricle is affected, cardiac output falls measurably. Beyond about forty percent, cardiogenic shock develops and mortality climbs steeply.

    shared node → Cardiac output falls
    Circulation stops
    Usually VF, sometimes pump failure

    Most commonly through ventricular fibrillation arising from the irritable ischemic tissue. Less commonly through progressive pump failure, or through mechanical catastrophe such as ventricular wall or papillary muscle rupture.

    shared node → Circulation stops
    Stop and escalate
    • Pain unrelieved after rest and the patient's own nitroglycerin
    • Hypotension, especially if it worsens after nitroglycerin, which suggests right ventricular involvement
    • New irregular pulse, very slow pulse, or very fast pulse
    • Crackles, frothy sputum, or breathlessness suggesting the pump is already failing
    • Syncope, which in a cardiac patient may mean a rhythm that self-terminated and will return

    Telling it apart

    Not Aortic dissection?

    Tearing pain, maximal at onset rather than building, often radiating to the back, sometimes with unequal pulses or blood pressures between arms. Aspirin and any anticoagulation are harmful here, so it matters.

    Not Pulmonary embolism?

    Sudden breathlessness dominates over pain, the chest is clear, the pain is often pleuritic and sharp rather than pressure-like, and there may be risk factors such as recent surgery, immobility, or leg swelling.

    Not Pericarditis?

    Sharp pain that is worse lying flat and better sitting forward, often in a younger patient, often after a viral illness.

    Not Reflux or musculoskeletal pain?

    Reproducible on palpation or on movement points away from cardiac cause, but never rule out ACS on that basis alone. Chest wall tenderness does not exclude an infarct.
