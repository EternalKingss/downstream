    Foreign body airway obstruction

    A physical object blocking the airway, where the whole management depends on whether any air is still moving.

    In one breath

    Something is stuck. If the patient can cough, their own cough generates more pressure than anything you can do and you should leave them to it. If they cannot, you have a few minutes and everything depends on mechanical removal.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    An object lodges in the airway
    Food, dentures, toys, blood, vomit

    Most commonly food in adults, especially with alcohol, poor dentition, or a swallowing problem. In children it is small objects and food such as grapes, nuts, and hot dog pieces. The narrowest points are the larynx in adults and the cricoid ring in young children.

    anatomy → Upper airway

    Magill forceps under direct vision PCP

    Direct removal with laryngoscopy is definitive but outside EMR scope. Rapid escalation matters if basic manoeuvres are failing.

    Partial or complete
    The single most important distinction

    In partial obstruction air still moves: the patient coughs forcefully, may wheeze or make noise, and can often speak. In complete obstruction nothing moves: no cough, no sound, no air. This distinction determines everything you do next, and it can change in either direction within seconds.

    If they can cough, encourage coughing EMR

    Do not intervene. Stay with them, encourage the cough, and be ready. Back blows and thrusts during an effective cough can dislodge a partial obstruction into a complete one.

    The cough weakens or stops
    Silence is the emergency

    A forceful cough generates far higher airway pressure than any back blow or thrust, so an effective cough is the best treatment available and should not be interrupted. When the cough becomes weak or silent, the patient has lost the ability to move air and has moved from partial to complete obstruction.

    Ventilation ceases
    Clutching the throat, panic, silence

    No gas exchange occurs at all. The patient typically clutches the throat, cannot speak, and rapidly becomes distressed then cyanotic. There is usually two to three minutes of useful consciousness.

    Back blows then abdominal thrusts EMR

    For complete obstruction in a conscious adult or child over one year: five sharp back blows between the shoulder blades with the patient leaning forward, then five abdominal thrusts, alternating. The mechanism is generating a sudden rise in intrathoracic pressure to expel the object.

    Chest thrusts for infants, pregnancy, or obesity EMR

    Infants under one get five back blows and five chest thrusts, never abdominal thrusts, because the liver is large and unprotected. The same applies in late pregnancy and where the abdomen cannot be encircled.

    Oxygen falls fast
    No reserve at all

    Unlike a gradual respiratory failure, there is no compensation available. Saturation falls steeply and consciousness follows within a couple of minutes.

    They lose consciousness
    Which changes the treatment

    This is a pivot point rather than an ending. Muscle tone relaxes as consciousness is lost, which can loosen a wedged object, and chest compressions generate airway pressure. Management changes entirely: lower them to the ground and start CPR, checking the mouth each time you open the airway.

    shared node → Level of consciousness drops

    Start CPR if they become unresponsive EMR

    Lower them to the ground and begin compressions. Compressions generate higher airway pressures than abdominal thrusts do. Check the mouth each time you open the airway and remove anything you can actually see. Never sweep blindly, which pushes objects deeper.

    Hypoxic cardiac arrest
    Highly reversible if the object comes out

    The heart stops from hypoxia rather than from any cardiac problem. If the obstruction is relieved, these patients can recover completely, which makes this one of the most rewarding arrests you will ever attend.

    Transport everyone who received thrusts EMR

    Abdominal thrusts can injure internal organs, and a partially dislodged object can move again. Anyone who received effective thrusts should be assessed, even if they now feel fine.

    Stop and escalate
    • Silence in a patient who was choking, which means complete obstruction
    • Weak or ineffective cough
    • Clutching the throat
    • Cyanosis
    • Stridor after apparent relief, suggesting a remaining partial obstruction or swelling
    • Any deterioration in a patient who was managing

    Telling it apart

    Not Anaphylaxis?

    Both close the airway. Anaphylaxis has urticaria, angioedema, and often hypotension, and develops over minutes rather than instantly during eating. Thrusts will not help swelling.

    Not Asthma?

    Asthma gives expiratory wheeze with a history of similar attacks. Foreign body obstruction typically starts abruptly, often during a meal, with a witnessed event.

    Not Epiglottitis or croup?

    In children, gradual onset with fever, drooling, and stridor points to infection rather than a foreign body. Do not put anything in the mouth of a child with suspected epiglottitis.
