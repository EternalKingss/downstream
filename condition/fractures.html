    Fractures

    Broken bone is a bleeding injury with a splinting problem attached, not just a structural one.

    In one breath

    Bone is living, vascular tissue wrapped in a nerve-rich membrane. Break it and you get severe pain, significant blood loss into the surrounding tissue, and sharp ends that keep moving and keep damaging things until you stop them.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    The bone fails structurally
    Direct force, indirect force, or pathological

    Direct force breaks bone at the impact point. Indirect force, such as twisting, breaks it away from the impact. Pathological fractures occur through bone weakened by osteoporosis or tumour, sometimes with trivial force. In children, growth plates are weaker than ligaments, so where an adult sprains, a child fractures.

    anatomy → Bones
    The periosteum is torn
    Severe pain

    The membrane covering bone is densely innervated. Tearing it, and then moving the fragments against it, is what makes fractures so disproportionately painful. Pain drives a catecholamine response that raises heart rate and blood pressure and worsens shock.

    Analgesia PCP

    Fracture pain is severe and drives a physiological stress response. Beyond EMR scope pharmacologically, but splinting well is itself excellent analgesia.

    Bone and muscle bleed
    Litres, in the big ones

    Bone is highly vascular, and the surrounding muscle is torn too. A closed forearm fracture loses a few hundred millilitres, a humerus or tibia up to about 750, a femur one to two litres, and a pelvis several litres. Multiple long bone fractures alone can cause hemorrhagic shock with no external blood.

    Splint it, and understand why EMR

    Splinting is hemorrhage control, not just comfort. Immobilising the fragments stops them tearing more tissue and lets a clot form. Splint the joint above and below, and check circulation, sensation, and movement before and after.

    Traction splint for a mid-shaft femur EMR

    Realigns the bone and reduces the volume of the thigh compartment, which both reduces bleeding and dramatically reduces pain from muscle spasm pulling the fragments together. Not for hip, knee, or pelvic injuries, or for open fractures with bone exposed.

    The limb swells
    Blood and oedema into the compartment

    Bleeding and inflammatory oedema accumulate within the fascial compartments. This is where fractures become more than a bone problem.

    Nerves and vessels are threatened
    By fragments, or by swelling

    Sharp fragments can lacerate or compress arteries and nerves directly. Certain fractures reliably threaten specific structures: supracondylar humerus fractures in children threaten the brachial artery, and mid-shaft humerus fractures threaten the radial nerve.

    Realign a grossly deformed pulseless limb EMR

    Gentle in-line traction to restore alignment when the limb is pulseless or the skin is tenting and about to break. Restoring perfusion outweighs the theoretical risk. Reassess pulses immediately after.

    Compartment pressure can rise
    Pulse present, muscle dying

    Bleeding into an inelastic fascial compartment raises pressure until capillary perfusion stops, while the distal pulse remains perfectly palpable. Tibial fractures and forearm fractures are the classic sites.

    An open fracture is contaminated
    Infection risk changes everything

    If bone communicates with the outside, even through a small puncture, bacteria reach bone that has poor defences and often poor blood supply. Osteomyelitis is difficult to treat and can end in amputation, which is why open fractures are urgent surgical problems rather than just bigger fractures.

    Cover open fractures with sterile moist dressing EMR

    Do not push protruding bone back in deliberately, though it may retract when the limb is aligned. Note if it does, so the surgical team knows contamination went inward.

    Blood loss causes shock
    Especially femur and pelvis

    Cumulative loss from multiple fractures, or a single femoral or pelvic fracture, is enough to produce hemorrhagic shock on its own.

    Oxygen EMR

    If hypoxic or in significant trauma.

    Pelvic binder at the greater trochanters EMR

    Reduces pelvic volume so clots can hold. Placed at trochanter level, not the iliac crests. Do not spring or rock the pelvis to test it first, because that achieves nothing and can dislodge a clot.

    Warmth and horizontal for major fractures EMR

    Femoral and pelvic fractures are bleeding injuries. Treat them as such.

    Stop and escalate
    • Absent or diminished distal pulse
    • Pain out of proportion to the injury, or pain on passive stretch
    • Numbness or inability to move fingers or toes
    • Open fracture
    • Femoral or pelvic fracture
    • Multiple long bone fractures
    • Tenting or blanching skin over a fragment
    • A supracondylar humerus fracture in a child

    Telling it apart

    Not Dislocation?

    Dislocation deforms at the joint and the limb is usually held rigidly in an abnormal position. Fractures deform along the shaft. They frequently occur together.

    Not Sprain?

    Not reliably distinguishable in the field, and it does not much matter, because both are splinted and both need imaging. In children, assume fracture.

    Not Compartment syndrome?

    A complication rather than an alternative. Pain out of proportion, a tense compartment, and pain on passive stretch, with a pulse still present.
