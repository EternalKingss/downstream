    Condition · Pleural space

    Simple pneumothorax

    Air in the pleural space breaks the vacuum seal, so the elastic lung recoils and collapses.

    In one breath

    The lung is held expanded only by negative pressure in a sealed space. Let air in and the seal is gone, so the lung springs back toward its own resting size. Nothing is wrong with the lung tissue at all; it simply has nothing holding it out.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Air enters the pleural space
    Traumatic or spontaneous

    Either from outside through a chest wall wound, or from inside through a tear in the lung surface. Spontaneous pneumothorax classically occurs in tall thin young men from a ruptured apical bleb, and in COPD patients from ruptured emphysematous bullae. Rib fractures can puncture the lung from within.

    anatomy → Pleural space

    Vented chest seal for open wounds EMR

    An open chest wound needs a seal that lets air escape but not enter. A fully occlusive dressing can create the one-way valve you are trying to avoid.

    The vacuum seal is lost
    Negative pressure gone

    Pleural pressure rises toward atmospheric. The only force holding the lung against the chest wall disappears.

    The lung recoils inward
    Absent or reduced breath sounds

    Lung tissue is elastic and under tension when inflated. Without the outward pull of negative pressure it collapses toward the hilum. Breath sounds are reduced or absent over the collapsed area, and percussion is hyperresonant because air has replaced lung.

    Blood passes unventilated lung
    Shunt on the affected side

    The collapsed lung still receives some blood flow, which returns unoxygenated. Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction reduces this over time, which is why a small pneumothorax may cause surprisingly little desaturation.

    shared node → Ventilation and perfusion stop matching

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration. Beyond correcting hypoxia, breathing a high oxygen concentration speeds reabsorption of the trapped air, because nitrogen is washed out of the blood and the gas in the pleural space is absorbed faster.

    Work of breathing rises
    Pleuritic pain, splinting

    The patient breathes faster and shallower, partly from reduced functional lung and partly because pleuritic pain makes deep breaths hurt. Splinting further reduces tidal volume.

    shared node → Work of breathing rises

    Position of comfort, affected side down if tolerated EMR

    Lying on the affected side can splint it and improve ventilation of the good lung. Let the patient's own comfort guide it.

    It may become a tension
    If a one-way valve forms

    This is the reason a simple pneumothorax is never simply observed in the field. If tissue flaps so that air enters but cannot leave, pressure climbs with every breath and the condition converts into a tension pneumothorax. Positive pressure ventilation makes that conversion dramatically more likely.

    Monitor relentlessly for tension EMR

    Repeated observations of breath sounds, saturation, pulse, and blood pressure. A stable simple pneumothorax that becomes unstable is a tension until proven otherwise, and it can happen in minutes.

    Ventilate cautiously if you must EMR

    Positive pressure forces more air through the leak. If assisted ventilation is unavoidable, use the smallest volumes that achieve chest rise and watch continuously for deterioration.

    Stop and escalate
    • Any progressive deterioration, which suggests tension
    • Increasing breathlessness or falling saturation
    • Rising pulse with falling blood pressure
    • Distended neck veins
    • Sudden deterioration after starting positive pressure ventilation
    • Subcutaneous emphysema, which feels like bubble wrap under the skin

    Telling it apart

    Not Tension pneumothorax?

    Tension is progressive and comes with shock. If the patient is deteriorating in front of you rather than holding steady, treat it as tension.

    Not Hemothorax?

    Blood is dull to percussion and collects at the base; air is hyperresonant and collects at the apex. Hemothorax also causes hypovolemia, so those patients are shocked from blood loss as well.

    Not Pulmonary embolism?

    Both give sudden pleuritic pain and breathlessness with a relatively unremarkable chest, but breath sounds are equal in PE.
