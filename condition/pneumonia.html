    Condition · Alveoli

    Pneumonia

    Infection fills the alveoli with fluid and pus, so the sacs that should hold air hold liquid instead.

    In one breath

    Organisms reach the alveoli and the immune response floods them. Those alveoli still receive blood but no longer receive air, so blood passes through and comes out unoxygenated. The patient becomes hypoxic in a way that oxygen alone only partly fixes.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Organisms reach the alveoli
    Inhaled, aspirated, or bloodborne

    Usually inhaled or micro-aspirated from the upper airway. Aspiration pneumonia follows a swallowing failure from stroke, intoxication, seizure, or reduced consciousness, and tends to affect dependent parts of the lung.

    anatomy → Alveoli
    The immune response floods the sacs
    Consolidation

    Neutrophils, fluid, and cellular debris fill the alveolar spaces. This is consolidation, and it is why the affected lung becomes solid rather than airy, why it is dull to percussion, and why crackles appear as fluid-filled alveoli pop open on inspiration.

    Blood passes unventilated alveoli
    True shunt

    The flooded alveoli still receive blood flow, but no air can reach them. That blood returns to the left heart still deoxygenated and mixes with well-oxygenated blood from healthy lung. This is shunt, and it is why the response to supplemental oxygen is often disappointing.

    shared node → Ventilation and perfusion stop matching

    Position with the good lung down EMR

    Gravity increases blood flow to the dependent lung. Putting the healthy side down sends more blood where the air actually is, which improves matching. Counterintuitive but effective in clearly unilateral disease.

    The diffusion barrier thickens
    In partially involved regions

    In regions not yet completely consolidated, inflammatory fluid lengthens the distance oxygen must travel from alveolus to capillary. These regions do improve with oxygen, which is why the overall response is partial rather than absent.

    shared node → The diffusion barrier thickens

    Oxygen EMR

    Titrate to target. Expect a partial response: the shunted regions cannot be reached by oxygen at all, so a patient who stays hypoxic on high flow has extensive consolidation, not a broken oxygen supply.

    Oxygen falls
    Often out of proportion to the exam

    Hypoxia develops while carbon dioxide often stays normal or low, because carbon dioxide diffuses far more readily and the patient is breathing fast.

    shared node → Tissue oxygen falls
    Work of breathing rises
    Stiff, wet lung

    Consolidated lung is stiff and requires more pressure to inflate. Pleuritic pain from inflamed pleura overlying the infection also causes splinting, which reduces tidal volume and worsens clearance of secretions.

    shared node → Work of breathing rises

    Salbutamol EMR

    Only if there is genuine bronchospasm, which many pneumonia patients do have alongside the infection. It does nothing for the consolidation itself.

    Sit upright EMR

    Improves mechanics and lets the diaphragm descend.

    The response goes systemic
    Sepsis

    Pneumonia is the single most common source of sepsis. Once the inflammatory response becomes systemic, the patient has both a gas exchange problem and a circulatory one, and mortality rises steeply.

    shared node → Tissue perfusion falls

    Screen for sepsis EMR

    Temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and mental state. Confusion in an older adult with pneumonia is sepsis until proven otherwise, and it changes the urgency completely.

    Warmth and fluids as tolerated EMR

    Fever raises metabolic demand and fluid loss. Hypothermia in an infected patient is a bad sign rather than a comfort problem.

    Stop and escalate
    • Confusion, especially in an older adult
    • Respiratory rate over 30
    • Hypotension
    • Saturation that stays low on high-flow oxygen
    • Temperature below normal in an infected patient
    • Exhaustion or reduced effort
    • Aspiration risk factors such as recent stroke, seizure, or heavy intoxication

    Telling it apart

    Not Pulmonary edema?

    Edema is bilateral and basal with frothy sputum, orthopnea, ankle swelling, and a cardiac history. Pneumonia is usually focal, febrile, with purulent sputum and pleuritic pain.

    Not COPD exacerbation?

    Heavy overlap and they frequently coexist. Pneumonia is more likely with fever, focal findings, and pleuritic pain; a simple exacerbation with generalised wheeze and increased sputum without consolidation.

    Not Pulmonary embolism?

    Sudden onset, clear chest, no fever early, and risk factors. Both can cause pleuritic pain, which makes this a genuinely hard call.
