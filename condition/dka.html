    Diabetic ketoacidosis

    Without insulin the cells starve amid high blood sugar, so the body burns fat and acidifies itself.

    In one breath

    Insulin does two jobs: it lets glucose into cells and it stops fat being broken down. Lose it and both fail at once. The patient is starving with a bloodstream full of sugar, burning fat for fuel, producing acid, and urinating away litres of water at the same time.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Insulin is absent
    Missed doses, pump failure, or infection

    Almost always type 1 diabetes. The trigger is a missed or reduced dose, an insulin pump failure, or an intercurrent illness such as infection that raises insulin requirement beyond what was given. DKA is sometimes the presentation of previously undiagnosed diabetes, particularly in children.

    anatomy → Pancreas

    Look for the trigger EMR

    Infection is the commonest precipitant and may be the more urgent problem. Check for fever, cough, urinary symptoms, and skin infections. Also ask specifically about pump function if they wear one.

    Cells cannot take up glucose
    Starving in a sea of sugar

    Glucose requires insulin to enter muscle and fat. Without it, blood glucose climbs while the cells that need it register starvation. The body responds as if it were starving, which is precisely the wrong response here: the liver manufactures still more glucose.

    The body burns fat instead
    Ketones are acids

    Insulin normally suppresses fat breakdown. Without it, fatty acids flood the liver and are converted to ketone bodies. These are usable fuel, but they are acids, and they accumulate faster than the blood can buffer them.

    shared node → Fat breakdown acidifies the blood
    Blood pH falls
    Kussmaul breathing, fruity breath

    Metabolic acidosis develops. The body compensates by breathing deeply and rapidly to blow off carbon dioxide, which is Kussmaul respiration. Acetone is volatile and excreted through the lungs, producing the characteristic sweet or nail-polish smell.

    Do not suppress their breathing EMR

    The most important principle here. Kussmaul respiration is holding the pH survivable. If you assist ventilation, you must match their high minute volume or the pH will crash. Never sedate or slow these patients down.

    Glucose drags water out through the kidneys
    Litres lost over days

    Above the renal threshold, glucose remains in the urine and holds water with it. The patient passes huge volumes and becomes profoundly thirsty. A typical adult in established DKA is five to eight litres depleted.

    shared node → Sugar drags water out of the body

    Recognise the dehydration EMR

    These patients are litres down and need fluid resuscitation, which is beyond EMR scope. Communicating the severity clearly and getting them moving is the intervention.

    Potassium leaves the cells
    Total body depleted, blood level misleading

    Acidosis and lack of insulin drive potassium out of cells into the blood, while the osmotic diuresis excretes it. So the measured blood potassium may be normal or high while total body stores are severely depleted. When insulin is given in hospital, potassium rushes back into cells and can fall dangerously, which is why these patients are monitored closely.

    Circulating volume collapses
    Tachycardia, then hypotension

    The fluid deficit eventually overwhelms compensation. Tachycardia, dry mucous membranes, sunken eyes, and eventually hypotension. Reduced perfusion adds a lactic acidosis on top of the ketoacidosis.

    shared node → Tissue perfusion falls

    Oxygen EMR

    If hypoxic. These patients are usually not hypoxic and their deep breathing is compensation rather than respiratory distress, so do not treat the respiratory rate as the problem.

    Consciousness falls
    Acidosis plus dehydration

    Drowsiness progressing to unresponsiveness, driven by acidosis, hyperosmolarity, and reduced cerebral perfusion together. Cerebral oedema is a specific and serious complication, particularly in children, and can occur during treatment.

    shared node → Level of consciousness drops

    Nothing by mouth EMR

    Vomiting is very common and consciousness may fall. Do not give oral fluids despite the thirst.

    Handle children with extra care EMR

    Cerebral oedema in paediatric DKA is uncommon but is a leading cause of death. Any headache, deterioration in consciousness, or unusual behaviour in a child with DKA needs urgent escalation.

    Stop and escalate
    • Reduced level of consciousness
    • Hypotension
    • Headache or deteriorating consciousness in a child, suggesting cerebral oedema
    • Signs of infection as the trigger
    • Vomiting that prevents any oral intake
    • Very rapid deep breathing that is beginning to slow, suggesting exhaustion
    • Chest pain, since DKA can be precipitated by an infarct

    Telling it apart

    Not Hypoglycemia?

    Opposite problem, opposite treatment, and a glucometer separates them in seconds. Never treat a diabetic emergency on clinical impression alone.

    Not Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state?

    Typically type 2 diabetes, older patients, developing over a week or more, with much higher glucose, far greater dehydration, and little or no acidosis or ketones because some insulin is still present.

    Not Surgical abdomen?

    DKA causes genuine abdominal pain, vomiting, and even guarding, and is regularly mistaken for appendicitis. The reverse also happens: a surgical problem can precipitate DKA.

    Not Sepsis?

    Both cause tachycardia, tachypnea, and altered consciousness. They also frequently coexist, since infection is the commonest precipitant of DKA.
