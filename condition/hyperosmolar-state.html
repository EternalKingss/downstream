    Condition · Pancreas, Body fluid and electrolytes

    Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state

    Extreme blood sugar and extreme dehydration developing over a week, with little acidosis because some insulin remains.

    In one breath

    The slow-motion version of DKA, in type 2 diabetes. Enough insulin is present to suppress fat breakdown, so there is no ketoacidosis and no warning signal. Glucose climbs for days, water pours out through the kidneys, and the patient becomes profoundly dehydrated before anyone notices.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Insulin is reduced but not absent
    Type 2 diabetes

    There is enough insulin to suppress fat breakdown but not enough to control blood glucose. That single difference from DKA explains everything: no ketones, no acidosis, no fruity breath, no Kussmaul breathing, and therefore no early alarm.

    anatomy → Pancreas
    Glucose climbs over days to weeks
    Often triggered by illness

    Infection, a stroke, an infarct, steroids, or simply reduced fluid intake in an older person. Blood glucose can reach 40 or 50 mmol/L, far higher than typical DKA, because there was no acidosis to make the patient feel unwell and seek help.

    Look for the trigger and the complications EMR

    Infection, infarct, or stroke may be the precipitant or the consequence. Assess neurologically and check for chest pain, and do not stop looking once you have the glucose reading.

    Enormous fluid loss through the kidneys
    Often 8 to 12 litres

    The osmotic diuresis runs for days rather than hours. Deficits are typically far larger than in DKA. Older patients have a blunted thirst response and may be unable to get themselves a drink, which accelerates it.

    shared node → Sugar drags water out of the body

    Treat as profoundly dehydrated EMR

    Deficits are larger than almost anything else you will see. These patients need substantial IV fluid, which is outside EMR scope, so escalate and transport.

    Blood becomes concentrated
    Water pulled out of brain cells

    High glucose and rising sodium make the blood hyperosmolar. Water is drawn osmotically out of cells, including neurons, which shrink. This is what produces the neurological presentation and it is the defining feature of the condition.

    Consciousness falls, sometimes with focal signs
    Another stroke mimic

    Confusion, drowsiness, and unresponsiveness. Seizures and focal neurological deficits including hemiparesis occur and resolve with treatment, making this yet another condition that imitates stroke convincingly.

    shared node → Level of consciousness drops

    Recognise it in a confused older diabetic EMR

    The typical presentation is an older type 2 diabetic found confused or drowsy at home. Check the glucose on every confused older patient, because this is missed constantly and often labelled as a urinary infection or dementia.

    The blood becomes prone to clotting
    Stroke, MI, PE

    Severe dehydration concentrates the blood and raises viscosity, substantially increasing the risk of arterial and venous thrombosis. Many of these patients arrive with a stroke, an infarct, or a pulmonary embolism as well.

    anatomy → Blood
    Circulation fails
    Profound hypovolemia

    The fluid deficit eventually exceeds compensation. Mortality is considerably higher than in DKA, largely because these are older patients with a longer illness and more complications by the time they present.

    shared node → Compensation fails

    Oxygen EMR

    If hypoxic. Consider that a concurrent pulmonary embolism or pneumonia may be present.

    Handle and position carefully EMR

    Often frail, often with pressure injuries from lying for days, and often hypotensive. Keep them horizontal and warm.

    Stop and escalate
    • Glucose above about 30 mmol/L
    • Reduced consciousness
    • Focal neurological signs
    • Hypotension
    • Evidence of having been down for a long period, such as pressure sores or incontinence
    • Signs of concurrent stroke, infarct, or pulmonary embolism

    Telling it apart

    Not DKA?

    DKA has ketones, acidosis, Kussmaul breathing, and a fruity smell, develops over hours to days, and typically affects younger type 1 patients. Hyperosmolar state has none of those, develops over a week or more, and has higher glucose with worse dehydration.

    Not Stroke?

    Focal deficits occur in both. Check the glucose. Note that these patients also genuinely have strokes, so it is not always either-or.

    Not Sepsis?

    Both give confusion, tachycardia, and dehydration in an older patient, and infection is often the trigger. Treat for both.
