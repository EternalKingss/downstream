    Condition · Muscles and fascial compartments

    Compartment syndrome

    Pressure inside a fascial compartment squeezes the capillaries shut while the artery stays open and the pulse stays palpable.

    In one breath

    The same closed-box physics as the skull, in a limb. Swelling inside an inelastic sheath raises pressure past what capillaries can overcome, so the muscle starves. The pulse is fine, because arteries run at far higher pressure. Muscle dies in four to six hours.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Something swells inside the compartment
    Bleeding, oedema, or external constriction

    Fracture bleeding, crush injury, reperfusion oedema after restored circulation, burns, or a tight cast or circumferential dressing squeezing from outside. The lower leg and the forearm are the classic sites.

    Remove anything constricting EMR

    Loosen or split casts, bandages, and circumferential dressings, and remove rings and watches. If you applied it and the limb has deteriorated, take it off. This is often curative in early cases.

    Pressure climbs in an inelastic sheath
    Fascia won't stretch

    Fascia is tough and does not accommodate. Volume added inside translates almost entirely into pressure. This is exactly the Monro-Kellie problem in a limb.

    Keep the limb at heart level EMR

    Counterintuitive. Elevating it reduces arterial inflow pressure without reducing compartment pressure, which worsens the perfusion gradient. Do not elevate a suspected compartment syndrome; keep it level.

    Fasciotomy ACP

    The only definitive treatment is surgically opening the fascia. It is time-critical, which makes early recognition and rapid transport the entire prehospital contribution.

    Capillary perfusion stops
    Pulse still present

    Capillary perfusion pressure is low, in the region of 25 to 30 mmHg. Compartment pressure exceeds that long before it approaches arterial pressure, so the tissue is ischemic while the artery remains open and the distal pulse is easily felt.

    Oxygen EMR

    Supportive. Does not fix a mechanical perfusion problem.

    Never rule it out because there's a pulse EMR

    The single most important point. Pulselessness is a very late finding. A strong distal pulse in a patient with disproportionate pain and a tense compartment is entirely compatible with muscle dying right now.

    Severe, disproportionate pain
    The earliest and best sign

    Ischemic muscle produces pain that is far worse than the injury should explain, and that is worse on passive stretching of the muscles in that compartment. Escalating opioid requirement in a limb injury should raise this immediately.

    Test passive stretch and document it EMR

    Gently extend the toes or fingers. Severe pain on passive stretch is one of the most specific findings available, and your documentation of when it appeared matters to the surgical decision.

    Nerves running through it are starved
    Paraesthesia, then numbness

    Nerve tissue is even more sensitive to ischemia than muscle. Altered sensation in the distribution of a nerve passing through the compartment is an early and important sign.

    Muscle necroses
    4 to 6 hours

    Irreversible muscle death begins within four to six hours of the onset of ischemia. The damage cannot be undone, and survivors are left with contractures and permanent functional loss.

    Dead muscle poisons the body
    Potassium, myoglobin, acid

    Necrotic muscle releases potassium, myoglobin, phosphate, and acid. Potassium can cause fatal arrhythmias and myoglobin causes renal failure. A limb problem becomes a systemic one.

    Stop and escalate
    • Pain out of proportion to the injury
    • Pain on passive stretch of the compartment
    • A tense, woody, firm compartment
    • Escalating analgesia requirement
    • Paraesthesia or numbness distally
    • Any of the above with a completely normal pulse, which is the expected finding
    • Crush injury or prolonged limb compression
    • Deterioration after a cast or tight dressing was applied

    Telling it apart

    Not Arterial injury?

    Arterial injury gives a genuinely pulseless, cold, pale limb from the outset. Compartment syndrome gives a warm, swollen, painful limb with a normal pulse.

    Not Simple fracture pain?

    Fracture pain settles with splinting and immobilisation. Compartment pain escalates despite them, and that trajectory is the key.

    Not Deep vein thrombosis?

    Also gives a swollen painful calf, but without the trauma history and without pain on passive stretch of that severity.
