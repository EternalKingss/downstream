    External hemorrhage

    Blood is leaving the circuit through a breach you can see, and therefore one you can close.

    In one breath

    The only survivable major bleeding is the bleeding you can reach. Everything about managing it is mechanical: put pressure exactly on the hole, hard enough and long enough that a clot can form, and do not keep lifting the dressing to check.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    A vessel is opened
    Arterial, venous, or capillary

    Arterial bleeding is bright red and pulsatile, driven by systolic pressure, and loses volume fastest. Venous bleeding is darker and steady but can still be substantial from a large vein. Capillary bleeding oozes and generally stops on its own.

    anatomy → Arteries and arterioles

    Direct pressure, hard and precise EMR

    Firm pressure with a gloved hand directly over the bleeding point, not spread vaguely over a large dressing. Most external bleeding stops with pressure applied properly. If it is not working, you are probably not pressing hard enough or not pressing in the right place.

    Don't lift the dressing EMR

    A forming clot is fragile and lifting the dressing tears it off, restarting the bleed. Add more dressings on top instead of replacing what is there.

    Tourniquet for uncontrolled limb bleeding EMR

    Apply high and tight, tighten until the bleeding stops, and write the time on it. Modern evidence is clear that a properly applied tourniquet is safe for a considerable period and that hesitation costs lives. Do not loosen it once applied.

    Hemostatic dressing packed into the wound EMR

    For junctional wounds where a tourniquet cannot go: groin, axilla, neck. Pack firmly into the cavity and hold pressure for the full recommended time.

    Circulating volume falls
    Whole blood, not just fluid

    Hemorrhage loses plasma and red cells together, so the patient loses volume and oxygen-carrying capacity simultaneously. This is why bleeding is worse than dehydration of equivalent volume, and why replacing volume alone dilutes what carrying capacity remains.

    anatomy → Blood
    Venous return drops
    Less coming back means less going out

    With less volume in the system, less blood returns to the heart. Stroke volume falls in direct proportion, because a ventricle can only eject what it received.

    anatomy → Veins
    The body compensates
    Tachycardia, pale cool clammy skin

    Veins constrict to squeeze stored blood back into circulation, arterioles clamp down in skin and gut, and heart rate rises. This is remarkably effective and can hold a normal blood pressure through roughly thirty percent volume loss in a healthy adult.

    shared node → The body compensates
    Tissue perfusion falls
    Despite a normal blood pressure

    The compensation that preserves blood pressure does so by sacrificing flow to skin, gut, muscle, and kidney. Those tissues are already in trouble while the numbers still look acceptable.

    shared node → Tissue perfusion falls
    Compensation fails
    Blood pressure finally drops

    Acidosis from anaerobic metabolism blunts the vascular response to catecholamines, the vasoconstriction fails, and pressure falls. In an adult this typically corresponds to around thirty to forty percent volume loss.

    shared node → Compensation fails

    Keep them warm EMR

    Hypothermia impairs clotting enzymes directly, which worsens bleeding, which worsens shock, which worsens hypothermia. Cutting clothing off and leaving a patient exposed on a cold Alberta scene actively contributes to their death.

    Circulation stops
    PEA from an empty tank

    The heart continues to generate organised electrical activity with nothing to pump. This is hypovolemic PEA, and it is not fixed by defibrillation or by drugs. It is fixed by stopping the bleeding and restoring volume.

    shared node → Circulation stops
    Stop and escalate
    • Bright red pulsatile bleeding, or bleeding that soaks through dressings faster than you can apply them
    • Tachycardia with pale, cool, clammy skin and a normal blood pressure, which is shock before hypotension
    • Narrowing pulse pressure
    • Anxiety or agitation in a bleeding patient
    • Any fall in blood pressure, which means compensation has already failed

    Telling it apart

    Not Internal hemorrhage?

    Same physiology, no visible blood. Suspect it from the mechanism, from abdominal or pelvic tenderness, from femur fractures, and from shock that does not match any visible injury.

    Not Neurogenic shock?

    Both are hypotensive after trauma, but neurogenic shock is warm, dry, and pink below the injury and often bradycardic rather than tachycardic. A patient who is hypotensive and not tachycardic after trauma should make you think spinal cord.
