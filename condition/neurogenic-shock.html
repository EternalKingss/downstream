    Neurogenic shock

    The nerves that keep blood vessels tight are cut, so the vessels relax and the circulation loses its tone.

    In one breath

    A high spinal cord injury severs the sympathetic outflow. Below that level the blood vessels dilate and stay dilated, the heart loses its ability to speed up, and the patient becomes hypotensive while remaining warm, dry, and pink.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    The cord is injured above T6
    Usually cervical or high thoracic

    Sympathetic outflow leaves the cord between roughly T1 and L2. An injury above that level, most often cervical, disconnects the brain from the entire sympathetic chain below it.

    Spinal motion restriction EMR

    Prevents further mechanical injury to an already unstable cord. Core EMR skill and the one thing here that addresses the actual cause.

    Watch the breathing closely EMR

    Injury at or above C3 to C5 takes out the diaphragm. Injury lower in the cervical or thoracic cord takes out the intercostals, leaving diaphragmatic breathing only, which fatigues. Be prepared to ventilate.

    Sympathetic tone is lost
    Vessels stop being told to constrict

    Arterioles and veins depend on continuous background sympathetic signalling to maintain tone. Without it they dilate to their resting diameter, and systemic vascular resistance falls sharply.

    anatomy → Arteries and arterioles
    Blood pools in dilated veins
    Relative hypovolemia

    Since the veins hold most of the blood volume and are now dilated, a large proportion of circulating volume sits in the periphery rather than returning to the heart. Preload collapses even though not a millilitre of blood has been lost.

    anatomy → Veins

    Keep them supine and warm EMR

    Horizontal maximises venous return from dilated peripheral veins. Warming matters more than usual because these patients cannot vasoconstrict or shiver below the injury and will become hypothermic remarkably fast, even indoors.

    The heart can't compensate
    Bradycardia instead of tachycardia

    The cardiac accelerator fibres arise from T1 to T4 and are also cut. Vagal tone to the heart travels in the vagus nerve, which is a cranial nerve and is completely unaffected. So the patient is left with unopposed parasympathetic input: the heart slows down instead of speeding up. This is the single most distinctive feature of neurogenic shock.

    Perfusion falls
    Warm, dry, pink, and shocked

    Blood pressure falls with no compensatory tachycardia and no vasoconstriction. The skin below the injury stays warm, dry, and well-coloured because the vessels there are dilated, which makes the patient look far better than they are.

    Do not assume this diagnosis EMR

    A trauma patient is far more likely to be hypotensive from bleeding than from their cord. Treat for hemorrhage first and look hard for a bleeding source; neurogenic shock is a diagnosis of exclusion in the field.

    The cord itself is starved
    Injury worsens

    The injured spinal cord needs perfusion to survive the swelling that follows trauma. Hypotension deprives it, converting salvageable tissue into permanent damage. Managing the pressure is managing the cord.

    shared node → Brain blood flow falls

    Oxygen EMR

    The injured cord is exquisitely sensitive to both hypoxia and hypotension. Keeping saturation up is directly neuroprotective.

    Stop and escalate
    • Hypotension without tachycardia after trauma
    • Bradycardia in a hypotensive trauma patient
    • Warm, dry, pink skin below an injury with cold clammy skin above it
    • Priapism, which is an uncommon but highly specific sign of cord injury
    • Diaphragmatic breathing with no chest wall movement
    • Falling temperature in a patient who is not exposed

    Telling it apart

    Not Hemorrhagic shock?

    The critical distinction. Hemorrhagic shock is cold, clammy, pale, and tachycardic. Neurogenic shock is warm, dry, pink, and bradycardic. When in doubt, assume bleeding and treat accordingly, because getting that wrong is far more dangerous.

    Not Spinal shock?

    Different thing entirely, despite the similar name. Spinal shock is the temporary loss of all cord function below an injury, causing flaccid paralysis and areflexia. It is a neurological phenomenon, not a circulatory one, and the two can occur together.

    Not Vasovagal syncope?

    Also bradycardic and hypotensive, but transient, resolves on lying flat, and has no neurological deficit or traumatic mechanism.
