    Behavioural emergency and severe agitation

    Agitation severe enough to threaten the patient or others, which is a medical presentation until proven otherwise.

    In one breath

    Every behavioural emergency starts as a medical assessment, because hypoxia, hypoglycemia, head injury, and toxins all present as disturbed behaviour. And severe agitation is itself physiologically dangerous, because struggling patients generate acidosis fast and can arrest during restraint.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Something disturbs brain function
    Medical, toxic, or psychiatric

    Hypoxia, hypoglycemia, head injury, stroke, infection, post-ictal state, stimulant intoxication, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, or a primary psychiatric illness. The list is important because the first several are lethal and reversible, and they are routinely misattributed to behaviour.

    anatomy → Cerebrum

    Oral glucose EMR

    If hypoglycemic and able to swallow safely. Aggression is a classic presentation of low blood sugar and it resolves completely with treatment.

    Rule out the medical causes first EMR

    Glucose, oxygen saturation, temperature, pupils, and a look for head injury. Diagnosing a psychiatric emergency without checking these is how hypoxic and hypoglycemic patients get restrained instead of treated.

    Agitation escalates
    Fear, aggression, disorganised behaviour

    The patient becomes frightened, confused, or aggressive. Whether this is a psychiatric crisis or a hypoxic brain, the presentation looks the same from the doorway, and de-escalation is the correct first response either way.

    De-escalate before anything else EMR

    Calm voice, open posture, space, one person speaking, no crowding, no cornering. Remove your own audience. This works far more often than people expect and it costs nothing.

    Scene safety and police coordination EMR

    Your safety comes first and you cannot help anyone from inside an ambulance you have been pulled out of. But a patient in police custody is still your patient medically, and their physiology is your responsibility.

    The sympathetic system floods
    Tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia

    Extreme agitation, especially with stimulant use, produces a massive catecholamine surge: heart rate and blood pressure climb, temperature rises, and the patient may be diaphoretic and appear to feel no pain.

    anatomy → Autonomic nervous system

    Cool an overheated patient EMR

    Hyperthermia is a major contributor to death in stimulant-associated agitation. Remove excess clothing, cool actively, and treat a high temperature as an emergency.

    Struggling produces severe acidosis
    Lactate builds fast

    Sustained maximal muscular exertion against restraint produces lactic acid faster than the body can buffer it, on top of any acidosis from stimulants or hyperthermia. This is the mechanism that turns a restraint into a resuscitation.

    shared node → Cells switch to anaerobic metabolism
    Restraint restricts breathing
    Prone position, weight on the chest

    A patient held prone, or with weight across the back or chest, cannot expand the chest to compensate for a profound acidosis that demands enormous minute ventilation. Restriction of ventilation at exactly the moment ventilation matters most is how these deaths happen.

    shared node → Tissue oxygen falls

    Oxygen EMR

    Agitation is hypoxia until proven otherwise. Apply oxygen early rather than treating restlessness as purely behavioural.

    Never restrain prone EMR

    The most important rule here. No weight on the chest or back, no hobbling ankles to wrists, no sandwiching between boards. If restraint is unavoidable, get them supine or on their side as fast as possible and keep the chest completely free.

    Sudden collapse and arrest
    Often just as they go quiet

    Severe acidosis, hyperthermia, catecholamine excess, and impaired ventilation combine to produce arrest, frequently at the moment the patient stops struggling. Anyone becoming suddenly calm during a violent struggle is deteriorating, not complying.

    shared node → Circulation stops

    Monitor continuously during restraint EMR

    A restrained patient is a critically ill patient. Watch the breathing, the colour, and the level of consciousness continuously. Sudden calm is a warning sign, not a resolution.

    Stop and escalate
    • Extreme agitation with hyperthermia and diaphoresis
    • Apparent insensitivity to pain
    • Sudden calm after a violent struggle
    • Any prone restraint, at any point
    • Abnormal vital signs of any kind, which reframes this as medical
    • Low or unrecorded blood glucose
    • Head injury, however minor it appears
    • Hypoxia

    Telling it apart

    Not Hypoglycemia?

    Aggression, confusion, and sweating with a low glucose. Reverses completely within minutes of treatment. Check it before anything else.

    Not Hypoxia?

    Restlessness and combativeness are classic early hypoxia. Check the saturation and give oxygen before concluding that behaviour is the problem.

    Not Head injury?

    Agitation is common in traumatic brain injury and is regularly attributed to intoxication. Look for external signs, ask about mechanism, and reassess the level of consciousness repeatedly.

    Not Post-ictal state?

    Confusion and combativeness after a seizure is normal and self-limiting. It should steadily improve. If it does not, look for another cause.
