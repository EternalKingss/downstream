    Gastrointestinal bleeding

    Bleeding into the gut, where blood is lost from the circulation and often not seen until much later.

    In one breath

    The gut is a long tube with a rich blood supply and no way to compress a bleeding point. Blood entering it either comes back up or travels down, and either way it takes hours, so the patient can be several litres down before anything visible happens.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    A vessel is breached in the gut wall
    Ulcer, varices, or lower GI source

    Upper sources above the ligament of Treitz account for most severe bleeds: peptic ulcers, gastritis, and oesophageal varices. Varices are the most dangerous, because they are high-flow veins under portal hypertension in a patient whose liver disease has already impaired their clotting. Lower sources include diverticular disease, cancer, and inflammatory bowel disease.

    anatomy → Hollow abdominal organs

    Ask about liver disease and alcohol EMR

    Varices in a patient with cirrhosis is a completely different level of emergency, because the bleeding is high-volume and their clotting is already impaired. It changes the destination and the pre-alert.

    Ask about anticoagulants and NSAIDs EMR

    Warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants, aspirin, and other NSAIDs all substantially change the severity and the treatment. This information is genuinely valuable to the receiving team.

    Blood enters the gut lumen
    Nowhere to apply pressure

    Unlike an external wound, there is no way to compress the bleeding point. The gut also distends to accommodate a considerable volume, so bleeding continues without any external evidence for a long time.

    Circulating volume falls
    Concealed until it appears

    Whole blood is lost, so both volume and oxygen-carrying capacity fall together. Because it takes hours for blood to travel through the gut and appear, the visible evidence always lags well behind the actual loss.

    shared node → Bleeding you cannot see
    Blood eventually appears
    Vomit or stool, and the colour tells you

    Bright red vomited blood means brisk upper bleeding. Coffee-ground vomit means blood has sat in stomach acid and the bleeding is slower or has stopped. Melaena, black tarry foul-smelling stool, means blood has been digested during transit and indicates an upper source. Bright red rectal bleeding usually means a lower source, but a very brisk upper bleed can also present this way.

    Look at what came out EMR

    Colour and volume of vomit or stool tell the hospital a great deal about source and rate. Describe it accurately rather than just recording bleeding.

    The body compensates
    Tachycardia, pallor, postural drop

    Tachycardia, vasoconstriction, and a narrowing pulse pressure. Postural dizziness is often the first thing the patient noticed. Beta blockers, which many of these patients take, blunt the tachycardia and remove your best early warning.

    shared node → The body compensates

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration. With reduced hemoglobin, maximising saturation of what remains matters more than usual.

    The airway is threatened
    In massive haematemesis

    A patient vomiting large volumes of blood can aspirate or obstruct their airway, particularly if consciousness is falling. In variceal bleeding this is a genuine mode of death, and positioning and suction matter enormously.

    shared node → Airway stops protecting itself

    Protect the airway aggressively EMR

    Suction ready and working before you need it. Recovery position or sitting forward for active vomiting. In massive haematemesis this is the most immediately life-saving thing you will do.

    Nothing by mouth EMR

    They will likely need endoscopy, and they may vomit again.

    Compensation fails
    Hypotension, confusion

    Blood pressure falls once roughly a third of volume is gone. In a patient with liver disease, impaired clotting means the bleeding does not slow down on its own.

    shared node → Compensation fails

    Keep them horizontal and warm EMR

    Maximises venous return, and warmth preserves what clotting function remains.

    Stop and escalate
    • Bright red vomited blood in any volume
    • Known liver disease, cirrhosis, or heavy alcohol use
    • Anticoagulant therapy
    • Tachycardia or postural dizziness, even with a normal lying blood pressure
    • A beta-blocked patient who looks pale but is not tachycardic
    • Melaena, which always means significant loss
    • Confusion or syncope

    Telling it apart

    Not Haemoptysis?

    Coughed blood is frothy, bright, and mixed with sputum. Vomited blood is darker, may contain food, and follows nausea. It matters because the source and treatment are entirely different.

    Not Swallowed blood from a nosebleed?

    A heavy epistaxis swallowed and then vomited looks identical. Ask, and look in the nose.

    Not Ruptured aortic aneurysm?

    Both cause shock in an older patient. GI bleeding has visible blood; an aneurysm has severe pain and no external evidence.
