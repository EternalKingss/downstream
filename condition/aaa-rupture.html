    Condition · Abdominal aorta and great vessels

    Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm

    The largest artery in the abdomen tears, and the circulation empties in minutes.

    In one breath

    Decades of atherosclerosis balloon the aortic wall silently. When it finally tears, the only thing between the patient and death is whether the retroperitoneum can hold the blood back long enough to reach an operating theatre.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    The aortic wall weakens and balloons
    Silent for decades

    Atherosclerosis degrades the elastic layer of the wall. Under constant arterial pressure it stretches outward. Risk climbs with age, smoking, hypertension, and family history. The great majority cause no symptoms at all until the day they leak.

    anatomy → Abdominal aorta and great vessels
    Wall tension rises as it grows
    Bigger means faster growth

    Wall tension increases with radius, so the larger the aneurysm gets, the higher the tension on an already weakened wall, and the faster it expands. Growth is self-accelerating, which is why size thresholds drive elective repair.

    The wall tears
    Sudden severe pain

    Abrupt onset of severe abdominal, flank, or back pain, often described as tearing. Around eighty percent tear posteriorly into the retroperitoneal space, which is what creates the survivable version. The remainder tear into the peritoneal cavity and those patients rarely reach hospital.

    Think of it in older patients with back or flank pain EMR

    The commonest misdiagnosis is renal colic. An older patient with new severe flank or back pain, especially with any hemodynamic instability or a first-ever episode, needs this considered. Renal colic patients writhe; aneurysm patients are often still and grey.

    The retroperitoneum contains it, briefly
    The window

    The retroperitoneal tissues and the pressure of accumulated blood can temporarily tamponade the leak. The patient may be in severe pain but hemodynamically stable, or transiently hypotensive and then recovered. This is the entire window in which surgery can save them, and it can close at any moment.

    Do not palpate deeply or repeatedly EMR

    Repeated deep palpation risks disturbing a fragile containment. A single gentle assessment for a pulsatile mass is reasonable; prodding it repeatedly is not.

    Minimal scene time and a surgical destination EMR

    Nothing you carry helps. The only variable that changes survival is how fast they reach a vascular surgeon, and whether that team is standing by when you arrive.

    Move them gently, and only once EMR

    Movement and straining can break the tamponade. Plan the extrication so the patient is moved as few times as possible.

    Blood pours into the retroperitoneum
    Litres, invisibly

    The retroperitoneal space accommodates a very large volume. Nothing appears externally, and the abdomen may not even look distended. The patient is exsanguinating with no visible sign of it.

    Circulation fails
    Often after a stable period

    When the containment fails, deterioration is precipitous. A patient who has been talking to you can lose their pulse within a minute. Do not be reassured by an initially stable set of observations.

    shared node → Compensation fails

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration. Maximise the oxygen content of what blood remains.

    Keep them warm and horizontal EMR

    Hypothermia impairs clotting in a patient whose survival depends on a clot holding.

    Circulation stops
    Hypovolemic PEA

    The heart continues generating organised electrical activity with nothing to pump. Compressions accomplish very little in a truly empty circulation, and survival from prehospital arrest with a ruptured aneurysm is close to zero.

    Stop and escalate
    • Sudden severe abdominal, flank, or back pain in anyone over 50
    • A pulsatile abdominal mass
    • Hypotension, even transient
    • Syncope with abdominal pain
    • Absent, reduced, or unequal femoral pulses
    • Known aneurysm under surveillance
    • A first episode of what looks like renal colic in an older patient

    Telling it apart

    Not Renal colic?

    The critical distinction, and the commonest fatal error. Renal colic patients are typically younger, restless, and hemodynamically normal. Anyone over 50 with a first episode of severe flank pain should be assumed to have an aneurysm until it is excluded.

    Not Aortic dissection?

    Dissection often involves the chest, may cause unequal blood pressures between arms, and can cause stroke or limb ischemia. Both are catastrophic vascular emergencies with similar prehospital management.

    Not Acute coronary syndrome?

    Both can present with pain and shock in an older patient. Getting this wrong matters because aspirin is harmful in a bleeding aneurysm.

    Not Perforated ulcer or pancreatitis?

    Both give severe abdominal pain with shock. Peritonitis gives rigidity; an aneurysm often gives a surprisingly soft abdomen because the blood is behind the peritoneum.
