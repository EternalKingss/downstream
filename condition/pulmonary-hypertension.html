    Pulmonary hypertension

    Chronically raised pressure in the pulmonary circulation, which eventually destroys the right ventricle.

    In one breath

    The right ventricle was built as a low-pressure pump because the lungs are a low-resistance circuit. Raise that resistance permanently and the right ventricle thickens, dilates, and eventually fails. The lungs cause the problem; the heart is what actually gives out.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Pulmonary vascular resistance rises
    Lung disease, clots, or the vessels themselves

    Several routes. Chronic lung disease such as COPD or fibrosis causes sustained hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. Repeated pulmonary emboli obstruct the circuit permanently. Left heart failure raises pressure backward. Or the pulmonary arteries themselves thicken and narrow in idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension.

    anatomy → Pulmonary vessels

    Oxygen EMR

    Genuinely therapeutic rather than just supportive. Hypoxia is a potent pulmonary vasoconstrictor, so correcting it lowers pulmonary artery pressure directly and reduces the load on the right ventricle.

    The vessels thicken and stiffen
    Progressive and largely irreversible

    Chronically constricted pulmonary arterioles remodel: the muscle layer thickens and the lumen narrows permanently. What began as a reversible response to hypoxia becomes fixed structural disease, which is why treating the hypoxia late does not undo it.

    Ask about their treatment EMR

    Many are on specialist pulmonary vasodilators, sometimes by continuous infusion through an indwelling line. Interrupting that infusion is a genuine emergency, and these patients usually know it better than you do. Listen to them.

    The right ventricle works against high pressure
    It was never designed for this

    The right ventricle normally pumps against about a fifth of systemic pressure and has a thin wall to match. Faced with sustained high resistance it hypertrophies, which raises its own oxygen demand while its coronary supply stays the same.

    anatomy → Myocardium

    Ventilate carefully if you must EMR

    Positive pressure raises intrathoracic pressure, which both reduces venous return and increases pulmonary vascular resistance. Both are bad here. Use the lowest pressures that achieve chest rise.

    The right ventricle dilates and fails
    Cor pulmonale

    Eventually the hypertrophied ventricle cannot keep up, dilates, and its tricuspid valve begins to leak, which makes it less efficient still. Output through the lungs falls.

    shared node → Cardiac output falls
    Blood backs up into the body
    Neck veins, liver, legs

    Venous pressure rises throughout the systemic circulation. Distended neck veins, an enlarged tender liver, abdominal distension, and swollen legs. The lungs themselves often sound clear, which surprises people expecting crackles.

    shared node → Fluid backs up into the body
    Left-sided filling falls
    Preload dependent, and fragile

    Less blood crosses the lungs, so less reaches the left heart. Cardiac output falls despite a healthy left ventricle. These patients become extremely preload dependent, and exertional syncope is a characteristic and ominous symptom.

    Handle them gently and avoid exertion EMR

    Exertional syncope in these patients means the right ventricle cannot increase output on demand. Carry them, do not let them walk, and be aware they can arrest with sudden exertion or straining.

    Do not drop the preload EMR

    A failing right ventricle needs a high filling pressure to produce any output. Nitroglycerin, sitting them upright when hypotensive, and aggressive positive pressure ventilation can all cause abrupt collapse.

    Small insults cause collapse
    No reserve at all

    A chest infection, dehydration, an arrhythmia, or a small pulmonary embolism can tip these patients into profound shock, because the right ventricle has nothing left to give. Once right heart failure decompensates, it is very difficult to reverse.

    shared node → Tissue perfusion falls
    Stop and escalate
    • Syncope on exertion, which indicates severe disease
    • Hypotension, which in these patients is very difficult to reverse
    • Chest pain, which may be right ventricular ischemia
    • Any interruption to a continuous prostacyclin infusion
    • Rapidly worsening leg swelling or abdominal distension
    • New arrhythmia

    Telling it apart

    Not Left heart failure?

    Left-sided failure gives crackles and pulmonary edema. Pulmonary hypertension with right heart failure gives distended neck veins, liver congestion, and leg swelling with often clear lungs.

    Not Pulmonary embolism?

    PE is sudden and can occur on a background of chronic pulmonary hypertension. Sudden deterioration in a known pulmonary hypertension patient should raise it.

    Not COPD?

    COPD commonly causes pulmonary hypertension, so they coexist. Prominent right heart failure signs in a COPD patient means cor pulmonale has developed.
