    Normal childbirth

    A physiological process you assist rather than perform, with two predictable danger points at the end.

    In one breath

    Labour does itself. Your job is to catch, keep the baby warm and breathing, and then watch the uterus, because the two things that go wrong afterwards are a baby that does not start breathing and a uterus that does not clamp down.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    First stage: the cervix opens
    Hours, usually

    Regular contractions dilate the cervix from closed to fully open. This is the longest stage, typically many hours in a first pregnancy and much faster in subsequent ones. Most patients are transported during this stage without difficulty.

    anatomy → Uterus and placenta

    Left lateral tilt before delivery EMR

    After twenty weeks, a supine patient compresses the vena cava and drops cardiac output substantially. Tilt left or manually displace the uterus.

    Delivery becomes imminent
    The decision point

    Contractions every two to three minutes, an urge to push or to bear down, a sensation of needing to open the bowels, and crowning. If the head is visible, you are delivering here. Attempting to transport during crowning risks delivering in a moving vehicle with no space to work.

    Decide whether to stay or go EMR

    If the head is crowning, deliver where you are. Assess by asking how many previous births, how frequent the contractions, whether there is an urge to push, and by looking. Multiparous patients deliver fast.

    Second stage: the baby is delivered
    Support, don't pull

    The head crowns and delivers, usually facing downward, then rotates to face one thigh as the shoulders align. The anterior shoulder delivers with gentle downward guidance, then the posterior with upward guidance, then the body comes quickly. Support throughout; never pull.

    Support, control, never pull EMR

    Gentle counter-pressure on the head to prevent an explosive delivery, which reduces tearing. Check for a cord around the neck and slip it over if loose. Guide the shoulders; do not traction the head or neck.

    The newborn must switch to breathing air
    The most important minute

    The fetal circulation must convert to adult circulation, driven by the first breaths inflating the lungs and dropping pulmonary vascular resistance. Most babies do this themselves given warmth, drying, and stimulation. Drying vigorously is both warming and stimulation, and it is usually all that is needed.

    Dry, warm, stimulate EMR

    The three things that resuscitate almost every newborn. Dry vigorously with a warm towel, discard the wet one, cover the head, and place skin to skin. Newborns lose heat extraordinarily fast and hypothermia impairs the transition.

    Ventilate a newborn who won't breathe EMR

    If not breathing after drying and stimulation, give inflation breaths. Newborn resuscitation is overwhelmingly about the airway and lungs, not the heart, because the problem is almost always failure to establish ventilation. Compressions only if the heart rate stays below 60 despite effective ventilation.

    Third stage: the placenta delivers
    Usually within 30 minutes

    The uterus contracts down and shears the placenta off the wall. Signs are a gush of blood, lengthening of the cord, and the uterus becoming firm and globular. Never pull on the cord: it can invert the uterus, which causes catastrophic hemorrhage and shock.

    Do not pull the cord, and bring the placenta EMR

    Cord traction can invert the uterus. Let it deliver, then transport it with the patient so it can be checked for completeness, because retained fragments cause ongoing bleeding.

    The uterus must clamp down
    This is what stops the bleeding

    The single most important physiological fact in obstetrics. At term the uterus receives 700 to 900 mL per minute. Bleeding from the placental site is stopped by muscular contraction physically compressing those vessels, not by clotting. A soft boggy uterus is an open tap.

    Massage the fundus after delivery EMR

    The highest-value intervention available to an EMR in obstetrics. Firm circular massage of the uterine fundus through the abdomen stimulates contraction and physically stops the bleeding. Putting the baby to the breast releases oxytocin and helps too.

    Postpartum hemorrhage
    If it doesn't contract

    Failure of the uterus to contract is the commonest cause of postpartum hemorrhage. Blood loss can be rapid and enormous, and because the patient has expanded blood volume she compensates well and then crashes.

    Oxygen EMR

    For the mother if she is bleeding or shocked. Routine oxygen in uncomplicated labour is not needed.

    Stop and escalate
    • Anything other than the head presenting first
    • Cord visible before the baby, which is a prolapse and an emergency
    • Heavy bleeding before delivery
    • The head delivers but the shoulders do not
    • A soft boggy uterus after delivery
    • Blood loss over about 500 mL, or any loss with maternal symptoms
    • Seizure in a pregnant patient
    • Gestation under about 36 weeks
    • Green or brown stained fluid, suggesting meconium

    Telling it apart

    Not False labour?

    Braxton Hicks contractions are irregular, do not intensify, and settle with rest and position change. True labour contractions become regular, longer, stronger, and closer together.

    Not Placental abruption?

    Painful bleeding with a rigid tender uterus and constant rather than intermittent pain. This is an emergency, not labour.

    Not Placenta praevia?

    Painless bright red bleeding. Never perform a vaginal examination in third-trimester bleeding, because it can provoke catastrophic hemorrhage.
