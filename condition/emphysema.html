    Condition · Alveoli

    Emphysema

    The walls between alveoli are digested away, destroying both surface area and the scaffolding that holds small airways open.

    In one breath

    Inflammatory enzymes eat the lung's internal architecture. Many small sacs merge into few large ones, so there is far less membrane to exchange gas across, and the elastic tissue that used to tether the bronchioles open is gone, so they collapse whenever the patient breathes out.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Enzymes start digesting lung tissue
    Smoking, or alpha-1 deficiency

    Inflammatory cells release proteases that break down elastin and other structural proteins. Normally alpha-1 antitrypsin neutralises them. Smoking both increases protease release and inactivates alpha-1 antitrypsin, so the balance tips toward destruction. A minority of patients have an inherited alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency and develop severe emphysema young, often in their forties, without ever smoking.

    anatomy → Alveoli
    Alveolar walls break down
    Many small sacs become few large ones

    The partitions between alveoli dissolve, merging them into large air spaces and sometimes into bullae several centimetres across. A given volume of lung now has a fraction of its former internal surface area. The destruction is permanent; alveoli do not regrow.

    Diffusion surface area collapses
    Less membrane to cross

    Diffusion depends on gradient, barrier thickness, and surface area. This condition attacks the third one specifically. The barrier is not thickened and the gradient is normal, so oxygen simply has fewer places to cross. That is why supplemental oxygen helps but never fully corrects it.

    Oxygen EMR

    Target 88 to 92 percent if they are a known carbon dioxide retainer, otherwise 94 to 98. Never withhold oxygen from a hypoxic patient over retention fears: hypoxia kills in minutes, retention takes far longer.

    Small airways lose their scaffolding
    Nothing holding them open

    This is the part people miss. Bronchioles have no cartilage; they are held open partly by the outward elastic pull of the surrounding alveolar walls. Destroy those walls and the airways lose their tethering. The disease of the alveoli becomes a disease of the airways.

    anatomy → Bronchioles
    Airways collapse on exhalation
    Air trapping, pursed-lip breathing

    During expiration the pressure around the untethered bronchioles exceeds the pressure inside them and they close, trapping air behind. Patients discover pursed-lip breathing independently because it raises internal airway pressure and splints them open, and it genuinely works.

    Salbutamol EMR

    Limited benefit, because the airway collapse here is structural rather than muscular. Most patients still have some reversible component worth treating, so expect partial improvement rather than none.

    Coach pursed-lip breathing EMR

    In through the nose, out slowly through pursed lips. It creates back-pressure that splints collapsing airways open, and it is one of the few interventions here that directly addresses the mechanism.

    Ventilate slowly with long expiratory times EMR

    If assisting ventilation, give plenty of time to exhale. Bagging fast stacks breaths on trapped air, drops the blood pressure, and can rupture a bulla.

    The chest hyperinflates
    Barrel chest, flat diaphragm

    Trapped air accumulates over years. The chest becomes barrel-shaped and the diaphragm is pushed down flat, which is mechanically terrible: a flat diaphragm generates far less pressure per contraction than a domed one. The patient does more work with a worse lever.

    anatomy → Chest wall and diaphragm

    Sit upright, leaning forward on braced arms EMR

    Gives a flattened diaphragm the best mechanics available and fixes the shoulder girdle so accessory muscles can pull against a stable base.

    Gas exchange deteriorates
    Breathless, thin, pink

    The classic emphysema patient is thin, breathless, and relatively pink, because they maintain their oxygen by working extremely hard at breathing. That effort burns enormous calories, which is why weight loss is characteristic and why these patients have so little reserve.

    shared node → Ventilation and perfusion stop matching

    Establish their baseline EMR

    Ask their usual saturation, usual walking distance, home oxygen, and whether they have ever been ventilated. Treat the change from baseline, not the absolute number.

    A bulla can rupture
    Sudden pneumothorax

    Large thin-walled air spaces near the lung surface can burst spontaneously, producing a pneumothorax in a patient with no reserve to absorb it. Any sudden deterioration in an emphysema patient should raise this before you assume an infective exacerbation.

    Stop and escalate
    • Sudden deterioration, which may be a ruptured bulla rather than an exacerbation
    • Unilateral reduced breath sounds
    • Drowsiness or confusion suggesting carbon dioxide narcosis
    • Falling respiratory effort in a patient who was working hard
    • Saturation well below their own stated baseline
    • Inability to complete a sentence

    Telling it apart

    Not Chronic bronchitis?

    Two halves of COPD and most patients have both. Emphysema classically produces a thin, pink, breathless patient with little sputum. Chronic bronchitis produces a heavier, cyanotic patient with a chronic productive cough and earlier right heart failure.

    Not Asthma?

    Asthma is episodic and reversible with full recovery in between. Emphysema is permanent structural destruction with a baseline the patient never returns from.

    Not Pneumothorax?

    Emphysema patients get pneumothoraces easily. Sudden unilateral pain with reduced breath sounds on one side is a pneumothorax until proven otherwise.
