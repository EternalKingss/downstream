    Condition · Pulmonary vessels

    Pulmonary embolism

    A clot blocks pulmonary blood flow, so perfectly ventilated alveoli have no blood arriving to collect the oxygen.

    In one breath

    Ventilation is fine, the chest sounds clear, and the patient is profoundly hypoxic. The air is getting in; the blood is not getting to it. Large emboli kill by obstructing the right ventricle rather than by causing hypoxia at all.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    A clot forms, usually in a leg vein
    Stasis, injury, hypercoagulability

    Most pulmonary emboli originate as deep vein thromboses in the legs or pelvis. Risk factors are stasis from immobility, surgery, or long travel; vessel injury; and increased clotting from pregnancy, oestrogen-containing contraception, cancer, or inherited disorders.

    Look for risk factors deliberately EMR

    Recent surgery or immobility, long-haul travel, a cast, pregnancy or recent delivery, oestrogen-containing contraception, cancer, previous clots, and unilateral leg swelling or calf pain. This history is often the only supporting evidence you can gather.

    It breaks free and travels
    Straight to the lungs

    The clot travels up the venous system, through the right heart, and into the pulmonary arteries, where the vessels narrow and it lodges. There is nowhere else for it to go, because the pulmonary circulation is the first capillary bed it meets.

    Alveoli are ventilated but not perfused
    Dead space

    The alveoli beyond the blockage receive air perfectly well but no blood arrives to collect the oxygen. This is dead space ventilation, and it is the mirror image of a shunt. It explains the single most distinctive feature of PE: severe hypoxia with a completely clear chest.

    shared node → Ventilation and perfusion stop matching

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration, though expect a limited response. Oxygen cannot fix a perfusion problem: the alveoli already have plenty of oxygen in them and no blood is arriving to take it. A hypoxic patient with a clear chest who does not respond to oxygen should raise this diagnosis.

    Think of it when the picture doesn't fit EMR

    Sudden breathlessness, a clear chest, and hypoxia out of proportion to the examination is the signature. This diagnosis is missed constantly because there is so little to find.

    Mediators constrict remaining vessels
    It gets worse than the clot alone

    Platelets in the clot release serotonin and thromboxane, which constrict pulmonary vessels and bronchioles more widely. The physiological impact is therefore greater than the mechanical blockage alone would predict, and some patients wheeze.

    The right ventricle strains
    The real killer

    The right ventricle is a thin-walled, low-pressure pump that was never built to work against high resistance. Faced with a suddenly obstructed pulmonary circulation, it dilates and fails quickly. This, not hypoxia, is what kills in a massive PE.

    anatomy → Myocardium

    Keep them still and horizontal EMR

    Exertion increases demand on a right ventricle that is already failing, and movement may dislodge further clot. Carry them.

    Rapid transport and pre-alert EMR

    Definitive treatment is anticoagulation or thrombolysis, neither of which is yours. Recognition and speed are the intervention.

    Left-sided filling falls
    Obstructive shock

    Blood cannot get through the lungs, so it cannot reach the left heart. Left ventricular filling collapses and cardiac output falls despite a completely healthy left ventricle. Neck veins distend as blood backs up behind the obstruction.

    shared node → Cardiac output falls
    Circulation stops
    Usually PEA

    Organised electrical activity continues with no output, because the obstruction is mechanical. PE is one of the classic reversible causes of PEA arrest and should be considered in any sudden collapse with risk factors.

    shared node → Circulation stops
    Stop and escalate
    • Hypoxia with a clear chest
    • Sudden onset breathlessness with no obvious cause
    • Hypotension or syncope with breathlessness, suggesting a massive PE
    • Unilateral leg swelling or calf tenderness
    • Pleuritic chest pain with risk factors
    • Coughing blood
    • PEA arrest in a patient with risk factors

    Telling it apart

    Not Acute coronary syndrome?

    PE is dominated by breathlessness rather than pressure-like chest pain, and the pain when present is often sharp and pleuritic. A clear chest with severe hypoxia favours PE.

    Not Pneumonia?

    Fever, purulent sputum, and focal crackles favour pneumonia. Both can cause pleuritic pain, and PE can cause a low-grade fever, so this is a genuinely difficult call.

    Not Pneumothorax?

    Unilateral absent breath sounds and hyperresonance in pneumothorax; equal breath sounds in PE.

    Not Anxiety or hyperventilation?

    A dangerous mistake. Anxiety does not cause hypoxia. A breathless patient with a low saturation is not hyperventilating from anxiety, whatever else is going on.
