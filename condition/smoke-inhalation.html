    Smoke inhalation

    Three separate injuries arriving together: heat burning the airway, chemicals burning the lung, and gases poisoning the cells.

    In one breath

    Smoke is not one problem. Hot gas swells the upper airway shut over hours, particles and chemicals inflame the alveoli, and carbon monoxide and cyanide stop the cells using oxygen at all. The patient can look completely well and still be in serious trouble.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    The patient breathes smoke
    Enclosed space is the key risk

    Risk is enormously higher in an enclosed space, in a patient found unconscious, or where escape was delayed. Modern building contents are largely synthetic, so the smoke contains a far more toxic mix than wood smoke: cyanide from burning plastics, wool, and foam, alongside carbon monoxide from any incomplete combustion.

    Get them out and into fresh air EMR

    Removal from ongoing exposure is the first treatment, but only from a safe area with appropriate protection. Firefighters remove patients from fire scenes; you do not.

    Look for the other injuries EMR

    Fire patients also have burns, trauma from escaping or from collapse, and sometimes the medical event that caused the fire in the first place.

    Heat burns the upper airway
    Swelling develops over hours

    Hot gases deposit their heat in the upper airway, which is efficient at cooling inspired air, so thermal injury below the vocal cords is uncommon unless steam was involved. The upper airway swells progressively over the following hours. This is the single most important thing about smoke inhalation: the airway that is patent when you arrive may not be patent later.

    anatomy → Upper airway

    Assume the airway will get worse EMR

    The defining principle. Soot around the mouth or nose, singed nasal hairs, hoarseness, stridor, facial burns, or a history of being trapped in an enclosed space all mean the airway is at risk even if it looks fine now. Escalate early rather than waiting for the swelling.

    Chemicals inflame the lower airways
    Bronchospasm, then edema

    Particulate matter and irritant chemicals deposit throughout the airways, causing bronchospasm early and an inflammatory chemical burn that develops over hours. Surfactant is destroyed, alveoli collapse, and a non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema develops.

    anatomy → Bronchioles

    Salbutamol EMR

    For the bronchospastic component, which is common early. It does nothing for the chemical burn or the swelling.

    Carbon monoxide occupies the hemoglobin
    Oximeter reads falsely normal

    Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin around 250 times more avidly than oxygen. It also shifts the dissociation curve so that whatever oxygen is bound is released less readily to tissue. A standard pulse oximeter cannot distinguish it and will read reassuringly high while the patient is severely hypoxic at tissue level.

    anatomy → Blood

    Oxygen EMR

    High-flow, non-rebreather at 15 L/min, regardless of what the pulse oximeter says. The half-life of carboxyhemoglobin is around four hours on room air and roughly one hour on high-flow oxygen, so this is genuinely therapeutic rather than supportive. Do not titrate it down.

    Do not trust the pulse oximeter EMR

    A normal saturation in a smoke inhalation patient means nothing. Treat on clinical grounds and give high-flow oxygen regardless of the number.

    Cyanide blocks oxygen use
    Cells suffocate in oxygenated blood

    Cyanide from burning synthetics disables cytochrome oxidase, the final step of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Oxygen arrives at the cell in normal amounts and the cell cannot use it. Profound lactic acidosis and altered consciousness with normal-looking oxygenation is the signature.

    shared node → Cells can't use the oxygen

    Cyanide antidote ACP

    Hydroxocobalamin binds cyanide and is carried by some services. Outside EMR scope, so flagging the enclosed-space fire and the altered consciousness early is what gets it to the patient.

    Tissue oxygen collapses
    By three mechanisms at once

    Reduced carrying capacity from carbon monoxide, impaired utilisation from cyanide, and impaired gas exchange from lung injury, all simultaneously. The brain and heart, being the most oxygen-hungry organs, fail first.

    The upper airway closes
    Hours after the fire

    Progressive swelling in the loose tissue of the pharynx and larynx narrows the airway. Because flow falls with the fourth power of the radius, the transition from hoarse voice to complete obstruction can be rapid. Once it closes, basic manoeuvres will not open it.

    shared node → Airway stops protecting itself

    Transport everyone with a significant exposure EMR

    The dangerous features develop over hours. A patient who feels fine at the roadside can have a closing airway later. Anyone from an enclosed-space fire needs assessment even if asymptomatic.

    Stop and escalate
    • Enclosed space fire, or found unconscious
    • Soot in the mouth, nose, or sputum
    • Hoarse voice, stridor, or drooling
    • Singed facial or nasal hair, or facial burns
    • Altered consciousness with a normal-looking saturation
    • Headache, nausea, and confusion in multiple people from the same scene
    • Any respiratory symptom, however mild

    Telling it apart

    Not Isolated carbon monoxide poisoning?

    From a faulty furnace or a generator indoors, with no fire and no airway injury. Same cellular problem, same falsely normal oximeter, but no burn risk. Multiple household members unwell together is the giveaway.

    Not Anaphylaxis?

    Both cause airway swelling. Anaphylaxis has urticaria, hypotension, and an allergen exposure rather than a fire.

    Not Asthma?

    Smoke can trigger a genuine asthma attack in a susceptible patient. The presence of soot, hoarseness, or an enclosed space changes the priority entirely.
