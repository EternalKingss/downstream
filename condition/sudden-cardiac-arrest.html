    Condition · Myocardium, Conduction system

    Sudden cardiac arrest

    The heart's electrical activity disorganises and coordinated contraction stops instantly.

    In one breath

    This is an electrical event, not a plumbing one. The muscle is quivering rather than pumping, the patient collapses within seconds, and the only thing that reliably fixes it is a shock delivered fast. Everything else you do is buying minutes until that shock arrives.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    An unstable substrate exists
    Ischemia, scar, or electrolytes

    Something has made the myocardium electrically uneven: acute ischemia, old infarct scar, cardiomyopathy, an inherited channel disorder, or a derangement of potassium, magnesium, or calcium. Areas conduct at different speeds, which is exactly what a re-entrant circuit needs.

    anatomy → Conduction system

    Treat reversible causes EMR

    Hypoxia, hypovolemia, hypothermia, hypo- and hyperkalemia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, and thrombus. Recognition of these is within your scope even when the treatment is not.

    A trigger beat lands badly
    Ectopic beat in a vulnerable window

    A premature ventricular beat arriving during the repolarisation phase, when parts of the muscle are recovered and parts are not, can start a circuit that spirals into ventricular tachycardia.

    Coordinated contraction stops
    VF or pulseless VT

    The ventricles receive hundreds of disorganised impulses per second. Individual fibres contract, but they do so out of phase with each other, so the chamber quivers rather than squeezes. Output is effectively zero from the first moment.

    Defibrillation as early as possible EMR

    This is the only intervention that actually reverses the problem. It depolarises the whole myocardium at once so the natural pacemaker can resume control. Every minute of delay costs survival.

    Circulation stops instantly
    Collapse within seconds

    Unlike hypoxic arrest, which arrives at the end of a long deterioration, this happens with no warning. Consciousness is lost in around ten seconds. Agonal gasping is common in the first minutes and is frequently mistaken for breathing by bystanders and by responders.

    Immediate high-quality compressions EMR

    Roughly 100 to 120 per minute, 5 to 6 cm deep, full recoil, minimal interruption. Compressions do not restart the heart. They keep the brain and the myocardium alive long enough for the shock to have something to work with.

    The brain starts dying
    Minutes, not hours

    The brain has essentially no oxygen reserve. Irreversible damage begins within about four to six minutes of zero flow. Survival with intact neurology falls by roughly seven to ten percent for every minute without CPR and defibrillation.

    shared node → Brain blood flow falls

    Oxygen EMR

    Via bag-valve-mask with reservoir. Ventilation matters less in the first minutes of a primary cardiac arrest than compressions do, but it matters increasingly as time passes.

    The shockable rhythm decays
    VF coarsens, then fades to asystole

    Ventricular fibrillation consumes energy. Without compressions to deliver some substrate to the myocardium, it becomes progressively finer and eventually flatlines into asystole, which is far less survivable. Good compressions preserve the window in which a shock can still work.

    Minimise pauses around the shock EMR

    Coronary perfusion pressure builds over successive compressions and collapses the moment you stop. Long pauses to analyse, charge, or check pulses undo work you already did.

    Stop and escalate
    • Agonal gasping mistaken for breathing, which delays compressions in a large proportion of arrests
    • Any pause in compressions longer than ten seconds
    • Prolonged pulse checks
    • Delaying defibrillation to complete other tasks
    • Assuming a hypoxic arrest in a child is primarily cardiac, when it almost never is

    Telling it apart

    Not Hypoxic cardiac arrest?

    Hypoxic arrest is preceded by a visible respiratory deterioration, tends toward PEA and asystole rather than VF, and needs airway and ventilation alongside compressions. Sudden cardiac arrest happens without warning and is more likely to be shockable.

    Not Syncope?

    A syncopal patient regains consciousness within seconds once horizontal, and has a pulse throughout. If they do not wake up, treat it as arrest.

    Not Seizure?

    Arrest often causes a brief tonic or myoclonic episode from cerebral hypoxia, and it is regularly reported as a seizure. Always check the pulse in anyone who has collapsed and convulsed.
