    Condition · Myocardium, Pericardium

    Cardiac tamponade

    Fluid fills the sac around the heart and squeezes it until it can no longer fill.

    In one breath

    The pericardium does not stretch quickly. Blood or fluid accumulating inside it raises the pressure around the heart until the thin-walled right side collapses during diastole. The heart is undamaged and beating normally; it simply cannot fill.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Fluid enters the pericardial sac
    Blood, pus, or effusion

    Penetrating trauma to the chest, blunt cardiac injury, myocardial rupture after infarct, aortic dissection leaking backwards, or non-traumatic causes such as malignancy, uremia, or pericarditis.

    anatomy → Pericardium
    Pressure rises fast in a rigid sac
    As little as 100 to 150 mL acutely

    The fibrous pericardium cannot stretch acutely. Rate matters far more than volume: a small amount arriving quickly is lethal, while a slowly growing chronic effusion can reach a litre with the patient still ambulatory. This is the same lesson as the pleural space in a smaller container.

    Recognise it in a penetrating chest wound EMR

    Any wound between the nipples and the costal margin can reach the heart. Shock with distended neck veins and equal breath sounds after penetrating chest trauma is tamponade until proven otherwise.

    Pericardiocentesis or thoracotomy ACP

    Definitive treatment is drainage, which is well outside EMR scope. Recognition and rapid transport to a surgical facility is the intervention available to you.

    The right side can't fill
    Thin-walled chambers collapse first

    The right atrium and ventricle have the lowest pressures in the heart, so external pressure collapses them first, and it does so during diastole when they should be filling. Note that this is a filling problem, not a contraction problem: the myocardium itself is working perfectly.

    Preload collapses
    Neck veins distend

    Blood cannot enter the heart, so it backs up into the venae cavae and the jugular veins. Neck vein distension is the visible evidence of that backup, and it is what separates obstructive shock from hypovolemic shock at the bedside.

    anatomy → Veins

    Keep them supine and avoid overventilating EMR

    These patients are entirely preload dependent. Sitting them up or bagging them aggressively raises intrathoracic pressure and further reduces the little filling they have left.

    Cardiac output collapses
    Obstructive shock

    A ventricle can only eject what it received. Stroke volume falls dramatically, the heart compensates by racing, and the shortened diastole gives even less time to fill against the external pressure. The compensation makes it worse.

    shared node → Cardiac output falls

    Immediate transport, minimal scene time EMR

    Nothing on your truck fixes this. Scene time is the variable you control.

    Perfusion fails
    Hypotension, narrow pulse pressure

    Pulse pressure narrows markedly. Pulsus paradoxus, an exaggerated fall in systolic pressure during inspiration, is classically described and rarely measured in the field.

    shared node → Tissue perfusion falls

    Oxygen EMR

    Maximises the oxygen content of whatever output remains.

    Circulation stops
    PEA with a rhythm on the monitor

    Electrical activity continues perfectly while output is zero. Compressions are largely futile against a heart that cannot fill, and defibrillation is irrelevant. Only drainage helps.

    shared node → Circulation stops
    Stop and escalate
    • Penetrating chest trauma with shock and equal breath sounds
    • Distended neck veins with a clear chest
    • Muffled or distant heart sounds
    • Narrow pulse pressure
    • Shock that does not improve, or worsens, with fluid
    • PEA arrest after chest trauma

    Telling it apart

    Not Tension pneumothorax?

    Both give obstructive shock with distended neck veins. Tension has unilaterally absent breath sounds and hyperresonance; tamponade has equal breath sounds and muffled heart sounds. If in doubt in a trauma patient, tension is more common and more treatable in the field.

    Not Hemorrhagic shock?

    Flat neck veins point to volume loss. Distended neck veins point to obstruction. Note that a patient bleeding heavily and tamponading can have flat neck veins, which is a genuine and dangerous trap.

    Not Cardiogenic shock?

    Both have distended neck veins, but cardiogenic shock usually has pulmonary crackles from backward left-sided failure. Tamponade classically has clear lungs.
