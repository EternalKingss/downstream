    Neuromuscular respiratory failure

    The lungs are perfect and the nerves or muscles that move them are not.

    In one breath

    Breathing is a muscular act driven by nerves. Damage the nerve, the junction, or the muscle and ventilation fails with completely healthy lungs. These patients look deceptively calm right up until they stop breathing.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Nerve or muscle function is lost
    Cord, nerve, junction, or muscle

    Anywhere along the chain can fail. High spinal cord injury cuts the phrenic nerve supply. Guillain-Barré syndrome attacks peripheral nerves in an ascending pattern. Myasthenia gravis blocks the neuromuscular junction. Organophosphates and nerve agents flood the junction. Muscular dystrophy and ALS destroy the muscle or its motor neurons. Botulism blocks acetylcholine release.

    Ask the patient and their family EMR

    These patients often have years of experience with their own condition and know exactly what their deterioration looks like. Many use home ventilation. Their assessment of whether this is worse than usual is more reliable than any number you can measure.

    The signal to the muscle weakens
    Or never arrives

    The brainstem is issuing a perfectly normal command to breathe. It simply is not reaching the diaphragm and intercostals with enough strength to move air. This is why these patients feel breathless and cannot do anything about it.

    Naloxone EMR

    Only relevant if opioids are contributing, which they sometimes are in a patient with chronic weakness who has been given analgesia or sedation.

    Tidal volume falls
    Rate rises to compensate

    Weak muscles move less air per breath. The patient compensates by breathing faster, which works for a while. A rising respiratory rate with shallow breaths in a neuromuscular patient is a warning, not reassurance.

    Sit them upright unless the cord is injured EMR

    Upright gives weak muscles the best mechanical advantage and keeps abdominal contents off the diaphragm. The exception is a diaphragm-dependent spinal patient, some of whom breathe better supine because the abdominal contents push the diaphragm into a better resting position.

    The cough becomes ineffective
    An early and reliable sign

    Coughing requires a deep breath, a closed glottis, and a forceful abdominal and intercostal contraction. It fails before ventilation does. Ask the patient to cough: a weak, breathy cough is one of the earliest and most useful bedside indicators of impending failure.

    Test the cough and the breath count EMR

    Ask them to cough and ask them to count out loud on one breath. A weak breathy cough, or being able to count only to ten or fifteen, indicates significant loss of vital capacity. These take seconds and are far more useful than the pulse oximeter here.

    Secretions accumulate and lung collapses
    Atelectasis, then pneumonia

    Without an effective cough or deep breaths, mucus pools and small airways collapse. Aspiration is also common, because the same weakness usually affects swallowing.

    Watch for aspiration EMR

    Swallowing weakness usually accompanies respiratory weakness. Suction available, nothing by mouth, and be alert for a gurgling airway.

    Alveolar ventilation becomes inadequate
    CO2 rises before oxygen falls

    This is the crucial physiological point. In a ventilation problem with healthy lungs, carbon dioxide rises early. Oxygen saturation often stays near normal until very late, especially on supplemental oxygen. A normal saturation in a tiring neuromuscular patient is meaningless.

    shared node → Carbon dioxide accumulates

    Oxygen EMR

    Use it, but understand its limitation clearly. Oxygen corrects the saturation and masks the real problem, which is carbon dioxide retention. A patient with a normal saturation on oxygen can still be in severe ventilatory failure. Never use the saturation to decide these patients are stable.

    The remaining muscles fatigue
    Then fail suddenly

    Weakened muscles working at maximum capacity for hours fail, and they fail abruptly. There is often very little warning between a patient who is coping and a patient who is apneic.

    shared node → Respiratory muscles fail

    Assist ventilation early EMR

    The whole management. Do not wait for the saturation to fall or for apnea, because by then you have missed the window. If the patient is tiring, has a weak cough, or cannot count far in one breath, start supporting them.

    Ventilation stops
    Respiratory arrest with a pulse

    Breathing ceases while circulation continues. These patients are entirely salvageable with assisted ventilation and often make a full recovery, because the underlying problem is frequently treatable.

    shared node → Breathing stops
    Stop and escalate
    • Weak or absent cough
    • Unable to count past about fifteen in one breath
    • Rising respiratory rate with shallow breaths
    • Difficulty swallowing or a wet-sounding voice
    • Any use of accessory muscles in a weak patient
    • Ascending weakness reaching the trunk
    • A normal saturation in a patient who is clearly tiring, which is a trap rather than reassurance
    • Inability to lift the head off the pillow, which correlates closely with diaphragm weakness

    Telling it apart

    Not Primary lung disease?

    Neuromuscular failure has clear lungs, normal breath sounds, and no wheeze or crackles. The problem is entirely mechanical, and oxygen fixes the number while doing nothing for the ventilation.

    Not Opioid overdose?

    Also a ventilation problem with healthy lungs, but with reduced consciousness and pinpoint pupils. Neuromuscular patients are usually fully alert and frightened, which is a distressing thing to witness.

    Not Anxiety or hyperventilation?

    A dangerous misattribution. These patients are alert, anxious, and breathing fast, which is easy to dismiss. The weak cough, the inability to count, and the difficulty swallowing separate them.

    Not Cholinergic poisoning?

    Organophosphates and nerve agents cause weakness alongside massive secretions, tiny pupils, salivation, and diarrhea. The secretions themselves are a major part of the airway problem.
