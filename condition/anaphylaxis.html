    Anaphylaxis

    A body-wide allergic reaction that closes the airway and empties the circulation at the same time.

    In one breath

    Mast cells across the whole body degranulate at once. The airway swells shut, the bronchioles constrict, the blood vessels dilate, and the capillaries leak circulating volume into the tissues. It is an airway emergency and a shock emergency simultaneously, and one drug fixes all of it if given early enough.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    A previous exposure primed the system
    IgE antibodies already in place

    The first exposure produced IgE antibodies that attached to mast cells throughout the body and did nothing visible. This is why the first contact with an allergen is often uneventful and the second is catastrophic, and why a patient can say they have eaten peanuts safely before.

    Re-exposure triggers mass degranulation
    Food, sting, drug, latex

    The allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells everywhere at once, and they release histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins into the circulation. Onset is usually within minutes; injected and stung exposures act fastest, ingested ones somewhat slower.

    Epinephrine EMR

    The only drug that addresses every limb at once: alpha-1 constricts vessels and reduces swelling, beta-1 supports the heart, beta-2 opens the airways, and it stabilises mast cells to slow further release. Give it early, intramuscular into the anterolateral thigh, and repeat every 5 to 15 minutes if needed. Delay kills far more people than the drug ever has.

    Remove the trigger if you can EMR

    Scrape a bee sting out rather than squeezing it, stop an infusion, remove latex. Do not delay epinephrine to do this.

    Observe for a biphasic reaction EMR

    A significant minority of patients relapse hours after apparently recovering, sometimes without further exposure. Every anaphylaxis patient goes to hospital, including those who look completely well after their auto-injector.

    The upper airway swells
    Lips, tongue, throat, voice change

    Histamine causes vasodilation and capillary leak in the loose tissue of the lips, tongue, and larynx, which swells rapidly. Because flow through a tube falls with the fourth power of the radius, modest swelling causes catastrophic obstruction. A hoarse voice, a lump-in-the-throat sensation, or stridor means the airway is already narrowing.

    anatomy → Upper airway

    Prepare for airway loss EMR

    A hoarse voice or stridor means you may have minutes. Get suction and a bag-valve-mask ready, position for the best airway, and escalate immediately, because once the larynx closes basic manoeuvres will not open it.

    The lower airways constrict
    Wheeze, chest tightness

    The same mediators constrict bronchial smooth muscle and swell the mucosa, producing an asthma-like picture on top of the upper airway problem. Patients with existing asthma have worse and more resistant bronchospasm and a higher risk of dying.

    anatomy → Bronchioles

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration. The patient is hypoxic from both airway narrowing and poor perfusion.

    Salbutamol EMR

    Adjunct only, for persistent wheeze after epinephrine. It does nothing for the swelling or the shock, so it must never be given instead of epinephrine or used to delay it.

    Vessels dilate everywhere
    Flushing, hypotension

    Systemic vascular resistance collapses. The same cardiac output now has to fill a much larger vascular space, so blood pressure falls. The patient is flushed and warm rather than pale and cold, which is the opposite of what most people expect shock to look like.

    anatomy → Arteries and arterioles
    Capillaries dump plasma into tissue
    Up to 35% of volume in minutes

    Increased permeability lets plasma pour out of the circulation into the interstitium. A substantial fraction of intravascular volume can be lost this way within minutes without a single drop of blood leaving the body. This is where the profound hypotension comes from.

    anatomy → Capillaries
    Perfusion collapses
    Distributive shock

    Dilated vessels and reduced volume together produce profound hypotension. Cardiac output falls, consciousness drops, and the patient may collapse suddenly, particularly if they are stood or sat up.

    shared node → Tissue perfusion falls

    Lie them flat, legs raised EMR

    One of the genuinely critical instructions in anaphylaxis. Patients have arrested on standing up or being walked to the ambulance, because an empty heart suddenly loses what venous return it had. If they cannot tolerate flat because of breathing, sit them up but never let them stand or walk.

    Airway obstruction or circulatory collapse
    Both routes kill

    Death comes either from complete upper airway obstruction or from circulatory collapse, and which one dominates depends on the trigger. Food-triggered reactions tend to kill through the airway; sting and drug reactions more often through shock.

    shared node → Circulation stops
    Stop and escalate
    • Any voice change, hoarseness, or stridor
    • Tongue or lip swelling
    • Hypotension
    • Wheeze in a known asthmatic after an exposure
    • Rapid progression over minutes
    • Any suggestion the patient wants to stand up or walk
    • A patient who used their own auto-injector and improved, who still needs transport

    Telling it apart

    Not Asthma?

    Anaphylaxis involves more than the airway. Look for urticaria, angioedema, gastrointestinal symptoms, hypotension, and a clear exposure. A wheezing hypotensive patient after exposure is anaphylaxis, and treating it as asthma is a fatal error.

    Not Panic attack?

    Panic can cause breathlessness, tingling, and a sense of doom, but not urticaria, angioedema, stridor, or hypotension. If in any doubt at all, treat as anaphylaxis.

    Not Vasovagal syncope after an injection?

    Fainting after a needle is pale, bradycardic, and resolves on lying flat. Anaphylaxis is flushed, tachycardic, and does not resolve.

    Not Septic shock?

    Both are distributive and warm. Sepsis develops over hours with fever and a source; anaphylaxis develops over minutes after an exposure with skin and airway signs.
