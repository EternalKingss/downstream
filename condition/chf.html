    Condition · Myocardium, Heart valves

    Congestive heart failure

    A chronically weakened heart that has run out of the compensations keeping it stable.

    In one breath

    The heart has been failing for years and the body has been hiding it by retaining fluid and remodelling the muscle. Those compensations eventually make things worse, and something tips the balance so that fluid backs up faster than the heart can clear it.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    The heart weakens over years
    Old infarcts, hypertension, valves

    Accumulated damage from previous infarcts, decades of hypertension forcing the ventricle to work against high afterload, valve disease, or cardiomyopathy. Output falls gradually and the body adapts.

    anatomy → Myocardium
    The body compensates, then overshoots
    Fluid retention and remodelling

    The kidneys interpret low output as low volume and retain salt and water to raise preload. The ventricle dilates and thickens. Both help briefly and then hurt: a dilated ventricle contracts less efficiently, and a thickened one needs more oxygen and fills less easily. The compensations become part of the disease.

    Something tips the balance
    Infection, arrhythmia, salt, missed meds

    Chest infection, new atrial fibrillation, a dietary salt load, missed diuretics, a new infarct, or uncontrolled hypertension. There is nearly always a trigger, and identifying it is part of the assessment.

    Look for the trigger EMR

    Fever, a new irregular pulse, chest pain, or a history of missed medication changes what the receiving team does. Ask about it.

    Fluid backs up into the lungs
    Breathless, worse lying flat

    Left ventricular filling pressure rises, transmits back through the pulmonary veins, and forces fluid into the alveoli. Lying flat redistributes fluid from the legs into the chest, which is why orthopnea and waking breathless at night are such characteristic features.

    shared node → Fluid backs up into the lungs

    Nitroglycerin scope varies

    Dilates veins, drops preload, and reduces the pressure driving fluid into the alveoli. Genuinely effective in acute pulmonary edema, provided the blood pressure supports it.

    Sit them fully upright, legs dependent EMR

    Pools blood in the legs, reduces venous return, and lets fluid settle away from the upper lung fields. It is a preload reduction you can achieve with a stretcher and no drug at all, and it works within minutes. Never lay these patients flat.

    Do not force a supine position for anything EMR

    Including for transport or assessment convenience. A patient in acute pulmonary edema laid flat can deteriorate within a minute.

    Fluid backs up into the body
    Swollen legs, distended neck veins

    Long-standing left-sided failure eventually raises pulmonary pressures enough to overload the right ventricle. Then venous pressure rises systemically, producing dependent edema, an enlarged tender liver, and abdominal distension.

    shared node → Fluid backs up into the body
    Oxygen falls
    Fluid across the diffusion barrier

    Fluid in the interstitium and alveoli thickens the diffusion barrier and reduces available surface area. The patient is working hard to breathe and getting progressively less for it.

    shared node → Tissue oxygen falls

    Oxygen EMR

    High concentration if hypoxic. Interrupting the hypoxia limb is the fastest way to slow the spiral with the tools an EMR carries.

    Hypoxia weakens the heart further
    The loop closes

    Hypoxic, overworked myocardium contracts less well, which raises filling pressures, which worsens the edema, which worsens the hypoxia. Acute pulmonary edema is a genuine emergency because of how fast this loop turns.

    shared node → Heart muscle starves

    Keep them calm and minimise exertion EMR

    Anxiety and effort raise oxygen demand and catecholamine levels in a heart already failing.

    Stop and escalate
    • Frothy or pink sputum
    • Unable to lie flat at all, or sleeping upright in a chair at home
    • Crackles rising above the bases
    • Hypotension in a patient with pulmonary edema, which means cardiogenic shock
    • Chest pain accompanying the breathlessness
    • New irregular pulse

    Telling it apart

    Not COPD exacerbation?

    The commonest hard call in prehospital medicine, and many patients have both. Favour heart failure with orthopnea, ankle swelling, frothy sputum, cardiac history, and crackles. Favour COPD with a smoking history, chronic productive cough, prolonged expiration, and a barrel chest.

    Not Pneumonia?

    Fever, focal crackles rather than bilateral basal ones, purulent rather than frothy sputum, and pleuritic pain.

    Not Pulmonary embolism?

    Sudden onset, clear chest, no orthopnea, and risk factors such as immobility or recent surgery.
