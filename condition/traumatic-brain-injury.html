    Condition · Cerebrum, Brainstem, Meninges and cerebrospinal fluid

    Traumatic brain injury

    Impact damages brain tissue directly, and then the body's response to that damage causes a second, larger injury.

    In one breath

    The primary injury happened before you arrived and you cannot change it. The secondary injury, driven by hypoxia, hypotension, and rising pressure, unfolds over the following hours and is almost entirely what prehospital care influences.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Impact damages tissue directly
    Contusion, shearing, vessel tearing

    The brain is soft tissue floating in fluid inside a hard box with bony ridges. Rapid deceleration slams it against the inside of the skull, producing bruising at the impact site and often on the opposite side too. Rotational forces shear axons apart diffusely. This damage is instantaneous and irreversible.

    anatomy → Cerebrum
    Blood collects inside the skull
    Epidural, subdural, or intracerebral

    Epidural bleeds are arterial, usually from the middle meningeal artery torn by a temporal skull fracture, and they expand fast. Subdural bleeds are venous, from torn bridging veins, and are especially common in older adults and heavy drinkers whose brains have shrunk and stretched those veins. Intracerebral bleeds are within the tissue itself.

    anatomy → Meninges and cerebrospinal fluid
    Injured tissue swells
    Peaks over hours to days

    Damaged cells lose their ability to control water and ions, so they swell. Inflammatory mediators increase capillary permeability. This is cerebral edema, and it adds volume to a container that has none to spare.

    Intracranial pressure climbs
    Compensation, then a cliff

    Cerebrospinal fluid and venous blood are displaced first, which buys time and produces the lucid interval. Once that reserve is spent, pressure rises steeply for tiny further increases in volume, and the patient deteriorates suddenly.

    shared node → Intracranial pressure rises

    Head up 30 degrees, neck neutral, collar not tight EMR

    Improves jugular venous drainage and lowers intracranial blood volume. A collar applied too tightly obstructs those veins and measurably raises intracranial pressure, so a poorly applied collar can actively harm a head injury.

    Serial GCS with times EMR

    The trend is the diagnosis. A patient who was 15 and is now 13 is deteriorating, and that trajectory is what determines whether they need a neurosurgeon now. Document actual times.

    Minimise stimulation EMR

    Pain, suctioning, coughing, and rough handling all spike intracranial pressure. Calm, gentle, efficient care is genuinely therapeutic here.

    Cerebral perfusion pressure falls
    The secondary injury

    Perfusion pressure is mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. As intracranial pressure rises, perfusion falls. If the patient is also hypotensive or hypoxic from other injuries, the effect compounds catastrophically. This is where prehospital care actually changes outcomes.

    shared node → Brain blood flow falls

    Prevent hypotension at all costs EMR

    The other highest-value intervention. Control external bleeding, keep them warm, and treat shock aggressively. In a head-injured patient the blood pressure target is deliberately higher than usual, because the brain needs a higher driving pressure to overcome the raised intracranial pressure.

    Ventilate at a normal rate, not fast EMR

    Hyperventilation lowers CO2 and constricts cerebral vessels, which reduces pressure by reducing blood flow to a brain that is already starving. Routine hyperventilation worsens outcomes. Ventilate to a normal rate and avoid overdoing it.

    Hypoxia and swelling reinforce each other
    The loop closes

    Hypoxia causes cerebral vasodilation, which increases intracranial blood volume, which raises pressure, which further reduces perfusion, which worsens hypoxia. A single episode of hypoxia or hypotension in traumatic brain injury measurably increases mortality.

    shared node → Tissue oxygen falls

    Oxygen EMR

    One of the two highest-value interventions in head injury. Keep saturation above 94 percent, and above 90 at absolute minimum. Hypoxia in traumatic brain injury roughly doubles mortality.

    Check glucose and keep them warm EMR

    Hypoglycemia mimics and worsens brain injury. Hypothermia worsens coagulopathy and bleeding.

    Brain is forced out of place
    Blown pupil, posturing, Cushing's

    The uncal portion of the temporal lobe is pushed down over the edge of the tentorium, compressing the third cranial nerve and then the brainstem. This is the endpoint and it is usually fatal without immediate surgical decompression.

    shared node → Brain tissue is forced out of place
    Stop and escalate
    • Any deterioration in GCS, especially a drop of two or more points
    • Unequal, sluggish, or fixed pupils
    • Lucid interval followed by decline, which is the classic epidural pattern
    • Rising blood pressure with falling pulse and irregular breathing
    • Posturing, either flexion or extension
    • Clear or bloody fluid from the nose or ears
    • Seizure after head injury
    • Vomiting, particularly repeated
    • Anticoagulant use, which turns a minor head injury into a major one

    Telling it apart

    Not Intoxication?

    The most dangerous assumption in prehospital medicine. Intoxicated patients fall and hit their heads constantly. Alcohol never explains a deteriorating GCS, unequal pupils, or a focal deficit. Assume brain injury and let the hospital exclude it.

    Not Hypoglycemia?

    Can cause identical confusion and reduced consciousness. Check the glucose, every time.

    Not Hypoxia or shock from other injuries?

    Both cause altered consciousness in a trauma patient. Fix the airway, breathing, and circulation first and then reassess the neurology, because the confusion may resolve.
