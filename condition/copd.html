    Condition · Bronchioles, Alveoli

    COPD

    Decades of airway inflammation and alveolar destruction, producing obstruction that never fully reverses.

    In one breath

    Two diseases in one label. Chronic bronchitis clogs and inflames the airways; emphysema destroys the alveolar walls and the elastic scaffolding that holds small airways open. Most patients have both, and neither reverses. What you treat is the acute worsening on top of a permanently altered baseline.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Years of irritation
    Smoking, occupational dust, biomass smoke

    Repeated inhaled injury drives chronic inflammation. In Canada smoking is the dominant cause, though occupational exposures and rarely alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency also produce it. The damage accumulates for decades before symptoms become limiting.

    Airways thicken and fill with mucus
    The chronic bronchitis component

    Mucus glands enlarge and multiply, the airway wall thickens with scar tissue, and cilia are destroyed so secretions cannot be cleared. Unlike asthma, this narrowing is structural rather than muscular, which is exactly why bronchodilators help far less.

    anatomy → Bronchioles

    Salbutamol EMR

    Helps less than in asthma, because most of the obstruction is structural rather than muscular, but there is usually a reversible component worth treating. Expect a partial response.

    Alveolar walls are destroyed
    The emphysema component

    Inflammatory enzymes digest the walls between alveoli, merging many small sacs into fewer large ones. Two things are lost at once: surface area for diffusion, and the elastic tissue whose outward pull holds the small airways open. That second loss is why these airways collapse on exhalation.

    anatomy → Alveoli
    Small airways collapse on exhalation
    Pursed-lip breathing is the fix

    Without elastic tethering, bronchioles collapse when the pressure around them rises during expiration. Air is trapped behind the collapse. Patients discover pursed-lip breathing on their own because it raises airway pressure from the inside and splints the airways open, and it genuinely works.

    Encourage pursed-lip breathing EMR

    Coach it explicitly: breathe in through the nose, out slowly through pursed lips. It creates back-pressure that splints collapsing airways open. Patients who already do it instinctively are telling you they have advanced disease.

    The chest hyperinflates
    Barrel chest, flattened diaphragm

    Trapped air accumulates. Over years the chest becomes barrel-shaped and the diaphragm is pushed down flat, which puts it at a severe mechanical disadvantage: a flattened diaphragm generates far less pressure per contraction. The patient is doing more work with a worse lever.

    Sit upright, forward-leaning EMR

    Lets the flattened diaphragm work with the best mechanical advantage available and fixes the shoulder girdle so accessory muscles can pull effectively.

    Gas exchange deteriorates
    Chronic hypoxia, sometimes CO2 retention

    Lost surface area and poor matching of air to blood produce chronic hypoxia. In some patients carbon dioxide also rises chronically, and the kidneys compensate by retaining bicarbonate, so the pH is near normal despite an abnormal carbon dioxide. These are the patients whose oxygen target is lower.

    shared node → Ventilation and perfusion stop matching

    Oxygen EMR

    Target 88 to 92 percent in a known retainer, rather than 94 to 98. Titrate carefully. But never withhold oxygen from a hypoxic COPD patient: hypoxia kills within minutes, carbon dioxide retention takes far longer, and fear of the latter has killed people.

    Something tips the balance
    Infection, usually

    Most acute presentations are triggered by respiratory infection, sometimes by air quality, heart failure, or a pneumothorax. Increased secretions and inflammation on top of a system with no reserve produce rapid decompensation.

    Ask what their normal is EMR

    A saturation of 89 percent may be this patient's baseline. Ask about their usual saturation, usual exercise tolerance, home oxygen, and whether they have been ventilated before. Treat the change from baseline, not the absolute number.

    Respiratory muscles fail
    Rising CO2, drowsiness

    A mechanically disadvantaged diaphragm working against high resistance for a sustained period fails. Carbon dioxide climbs acutely on top of the chronic level, pH falls, and the patient becomes drowsy. Carbon dioxide narcosis in a COPD patient is a pre-arrest state.

    shared node → Respiratory muscles fail

    Slow, gentle assisted ventilation if needed EMR

    Long expiratory times, low volumes, slow rate. Bagging fast stacks breaths on already trapped air and can drop the blood pressure or cause a pneumothorax.

    The right heart eventually fails
    Cor pulmonale

    Chronic hypoxia causes sustained pulmonary vasoconstriction, raising pulmonary artery pressure. Over years the right ventricle fails against it, producing distended neck veins, swollen legs, and an enlarged liver.

    Stop and escalate
    • Drowsiness or confusion, suggesting carbon dioxide narcosis
    • Silent chest or markedly reduced air entry
    • Inability to speak in sentences
    • Falling respiratory rate or reduced effort
    • Sudden severe deterioration, which may be a pneumothorax rather than an exacerbation
    • New chest pain or unilateral absent breath sounds
    • Saturation well below the patient's own stated baseline

    Telling it apart

    Not Asthma?

    Asthma is episodic and reversible with a clear trigger and full recovery between attacks, often in a younger patient. COPD is chronic and progressive with a smoking history, daily productive cough, and a baseline they never return from.

    Not Heart failure?

    Genuinely difficult, and many older patients have both. Favour heart failure with orthopnea, ankle swelling, frothy sputum, and basal crackles. Favour COPD with a smoking history, prolonged expiration, barrel chest, and purulent sputum.

    Not Pneumothorax?

    COPD patients rupture bullae and develop pneumothoraces relatively easily. Sudden deterioration with unilateral chest pain and reduced breath sounds on one side is a pneumothorax until proven otherwise.
