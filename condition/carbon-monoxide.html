    Carbon monoxide poisoning

    A gas with no smell occupies the hemoglobin, so blood circulates normally while carrying almost no oxygen.

    In one breath

    Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin hundreds of times more tightly than oxygen and will not let go. The patient looks pink, the oximeter reads fine, and the tissues are suffocating. Every tool you normally trust is lying to you.

    The chain

    Each step causes the one below it. Interventions are attached to the link they break, and greyed where they sit outside EMR scope in Alberta.

    Incomplete combustion produces CO
    Colourless, odourless, tasteless

    Faulty furnaces and water heaters, blocked flues, generators or barbecues used indoors, vehicle exhaust, and any fire. In Alberta the classic winter presentation is a whole household unwell with a faulty furnace. Because the gas has no warning properties at all, exposure continues until someone collapses.

    Remove from the source safely EMR

    Do not enter an unventilated space without appropriate breathing apparatus. Responders have been poisoned entering to retrieve patients. This is a fire service task.

    Get the source addressed EMR

    Ensure fire service or the utility attends. Sending a patient home to the same faulty furnace is a preventable second call.

    CO occupies the hemoglobin
    About 250 times the affinity of oxygen

    Carbon monoxide binds the same site oxygen uses, with vastly greater affinity, forming carboxyhemoglobin. Oxygen simply cannot compete. Effective oxygen-carrying capacity falls in proportion to how much hemoglobin is occupied.

    anatomy → Blood

    Oxygen EMR

    The treatment, not just supportive care. High-flow via non-rebreather at 15 L/min regardless of the saturation reading. Carboxyhemoglobin half-life is about four hours on room air, roughly one hour on high-flow oxygen, and around twenty minutes in a hyperbaric chamber. Do not titrate down.

    Remaining oxygen is held too tightly
    The second, worse injury

    This is the part that gets forgotten. Carbon monoxide also shifts the oxygen dissociation curve leftward, so the oxygen still bound to unoccupied sites is released far less readily to tissue. The patient is worse off than the carboxyhemoglobin level alone would suggest.

    It also poisons the mitochondria
    Binds cytochrome oxidase

    Carbon monoxide binds cytochrome oxidase in the mitochondria as well, so even oxygen that does reach the cell cannot be used properly. This is why symptoms sometimes exceed what the blood level predicts and why recovery can lag behind clearance.

    Every instrument lies
    Oximeter high, skin pink

    A standard pulse oximeter uses two wavelengths and cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin, so it reads falsely high, often 98 to 100 percent. Carboxyhemoglobin is bright red, so the patient looks well-perfused rather than cyanotic. The cherry-red appearance is a late and unreliable sign; pink and normal-looking is the usual one.

    Suspect it when several people are unwell together EMR

    The most useful diagnostic clue available. Multiple people in one building with headache and nausea, or a household plus their pets, is carbon monoxide until proven otherwise. Symptoms that improve when they leave the building and return when they go back is close to diagnostic.

    Ignore the pulse oximeter entirely EMR

    It cannot detect this and will actively mislead you. A CO-oximeter measures it correctly if your service carries one. Treat on clinical suspicion.

    Brain and heart starve first
    Headache, nausea, confusion

    The highest-demand organs fail first. Headache, nausea, dizziness, and fatigue at lower levels, progressing to confusion, chest pain, breathlessness, and collapse. It is routinely misdiagnosed as influenza, migraine, or food poisoning, particularly when a whole family presents together.

    shared node → Tissue oxygen falls
    Consciousness and circulation fail
    Seizure, arrhythmia, arrest

    Severe poisoning causes seizures, myocardial ischemia even in young healthy hearts, arrhythmias, and death. Anyone found unconscious at the scene has had a severe exposure regardless of how they look once removed.

    shared node → Level of consciousness drops
    Delayed neurological injury
    Days to weeks later

    A significant minority of survivors develop delayed neurological problems weeks later: memory impairment, personality change, movement disorders. This risk is part of why hyperbaric therapy is considered even in patients who recover quickly.

    Transport everyone exposed EMR

    Including those who feel better after removal, and specifically including pregnant patients, because fetal hemoglobin binds carbon monoxide even more avidly and the fetus is at far greater risk than the mother.

    Flag candidates for hyperbaric therapy EMR

    Loss of consciousness, neurological signs, cardiac ischemia, pregnancy, or very high levels. This changes the destination decision, so raise it early.

    Stop and escalate
    • Multiple people or pets from one location unwell together
    • Any loss of consciousness
    • Symptoms that resolve away from the building and recur on return
    • Chest pain or arrhythmia, particularly in a young patient
    • Pregnancy
    • Neurological signs of any kind
    • Winter, an enclosed space, and a combustion appliance

    Telling it apart

    Not Influenza or viral illness?

    The classic misdiagnosis. Headache, nausea, fatigue, and myalgia are shared. Flu does not affect everyone in the house simultaneously within hours, and it does not improve when you step outside.

    Not Cyanide poisoning?

    Frequently coexists in fire smoke. Cyanide causes profound lactic acidosis with rapid collapse and normal-looking oxygenation. High-flow oxygen treats both; cyanide additionally needs a specific antidote.

    Not Food poisoning?

    Another common misattribution when a family is unwell together. Nausea without diarrhoea, plus headache and confusion, should raise carbon monoxide.
