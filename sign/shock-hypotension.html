    Shock and hypotension

    Four mechanisms, and telling them apart determines whether fluid helps or harms.

    What this sign actually means

    Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion, not a blood pressure number. There are only four ways to get there: not enough volume, a pump that will not pump, vessels that are too wide, or something mechanically obstructing the circuit. Blood pressure falls late in all four, so a normal pressure never excludes shock.

    First moves
    • Horizontal, warm, and oxygen.
    • Look at the neck veins. Flat means volume loss; distended means obstruction or pump failure. This one observation splits the four categories in half.
    • Listen to the chest. Crackles suggest the pump; unilateral absence suggests tension pneumothorax.
    • Feel the skin. Cold and clammy suggests hypovolemic or cardiogenic; warm and flushed suggests distributive.
    • Look for bleeding you can control, then consider the places you cannot see: chest, abdomen, pelvis, thighs, and the floor.

    What produces it, and how to tell them apart

    Ordered roughly by how quickly each will kill. The clue column is what separates it from the others on this list.

    Hemorrhagic and hypovolemic shock

    Flat neck veins, clear chest, cold clammy skin, tachycardia. Look hard for concealed bleeding.

    External hemorrhage

    Visible and controllable. Pressure, tourniquet, packing.

    Cardiogenic shock

    Distended neck veins with crackles, cardiac history, cold peripheries. Fluid makes this worse.

    Septic shock

    Fever or hypothermia, warm flushed skin early, wide pulse pressure, a source of infection.

    Anaphylaxis

    Exposure, urticaria, angioedema, wheeze. Lie them flat and give epinephrine early.

    Neurogenic shock

    Warm, dry, pink, and bradycardic after spinal trauma. A field diagnosis of exclusion; assume bleeding first.

    Tension pneumothorax

    Distended neck veins with unilaterally absent breath sounds and hyperresonance.

    Cardiac tamponade

    Distended neck veins with equal breath sounds and muffled heart sounds.

    Pulmonary embolism

    Distended neck veins with a clear chest and severe hypoxia.

    Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm

    Older patient, severe back or flank pain, often a deceptively soft abdomen.

    Adrenal crisis

    Hypotension that does not respond to fluid, in a patient on long-term steroids.

    Obstetric hemorrhage

    Pregnant or recently delivered. Normal-looking observations are not reassuring, and a soft uterus after delivery is an open tap.

    Peritonitis and the acute abdomen

    Rigid abdomen, lies still, litres sequestered into the abdomen with nothing lost externally.
