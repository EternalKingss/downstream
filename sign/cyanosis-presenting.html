    Reverse lookup

    Cyanosis

    A late sign that requires enough desaturated hemoglobin to be visible, so its absence proves nothing.

    What this sign actually means

    Deoxygenated hemoglobin is dark blue-purple and becomes visible through the skin at roughly 50 grams per litre in the capillary bed. That is an absolute amount, not a percentage, which is why an anemic patient can be profoundly hypoxic and never turn blue, and why a carbon monoxide patient can be cherry pink while suffocating.

    First moves
    • High-flow oxygen immediately and assess whether they are ventilating adequately.
    • Distinguish central from peripheral: check the lips, tongue, and oral mucosa, which reflect true arterial desaturation.
    • If there is no response to oxygen, think shunt, obstruction, or a perfusion problem rather than turning the flow up further.
    • Look at the chest for symmetry and effort.

    What produces it, and how to tell them apart

    Ordered roughly by how quickly each will kill. The clue column is what separates it from the others on this list.

    Asthma

    Wheeze, prolonged expiration, accessory muscle use. Cyanosis here means a severe attack.

    COPD

    Chronic disease with an established baseline, often chronically slightly cyanotic.

    Foreign body airway obstruction

    Sudden onset during eating, clutching the throat, silence.

    Opioid overdose

    Respiratory rate under 10 with pinpoint pupils.

    Drowning

    Submersion history, frothy secretions, profound hypoxia poorly responsive to oxygen.

    Tension pneumothorax

    Unilateral absent breath sounds with shock.

    Pulmonary edema

    Frothy sputum, crackles, orthopnea.

    Cardiogenic shock

    Peripheral cyanosis from poor flow rather than poor oxygenation.

    Hypothermia

    Peripheral cyanosis from vasoconstriction, with a cold patient.

    Seizure and status epilepticus

    Cyanosis during the tonic phase is expected and resolves when the seizure stops.
