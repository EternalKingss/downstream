    Abdominal pain

    Where it started and where it moved to is worth more than where it hurts now.

    What this sign actually means

    Organs themselves have only autonomic innervation, so pain from them is dull, crampy, and felt in the midline regardless of which organ is involved. Once inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum lining the wall, which has somatic innervation, the pain becomes sharp, localised, and worse with movement. That migration is the single most useful thing you can document.

    First moves
    • Position of comfort, usually knees flexed, and drive gently.
    • Vital signs including a heart rate trend, because tachycardia with a normal pressure is compensated shock.
    • Ask about the onset: sudden and maximal suggests perforation or a vascular catastrophe; gradual suggests inflammation.
    • In any woman of childbearing age, consider ectopic pregnancy regardless of what she says about the possibility.
    • Check the glucose, because diabetic ketoacidosis mimics a surgical abdomen convincingly.

    What produces it, and how to tell them apart

    Ordered roughly by how quickly each will kill. The clue column is what separates it from the others on this list.

    Peritonitis and the acute abdomen

    Rigid abdomen, involuntary guarding, lies completely still, pain that migrated from central to localised.

    Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm

    Over 50, severe back or flank pain, pulsatile mass, hypotension, often a soft abdomen.

    Gastrointestinal bleeding

    Vomited blood or melaena, liver disease or anticoagulants, postural dizziness.

    Solid organ injury

    Trauma mechanism, lower rib fractures, seat belt marks, left shoulder tip pain, shock with no external blood.

    Diabetic ketoacidosis

    Genuine abdominal pain with vomiting, deep sighing breathing, high glucose. Check it before assuming surgery.

    Obstetric hemorrhage

    Pregnancy, or possible early pregnancy with shoulder tip pain and shock suggesting ruptured ectopic.

    Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia

    Right upper quadrant or epigastric pain in pregnancy with hypertension. HELLP syndrome is regularly mistaken for gastritis.

    Acute coronary syndrome

    Inferior infarcts present with epigastric pain, nausea, and vomiting. Consider it in older patients with upper abdominal pain.

    Adrenal crisis

    Abdominal pain, vomiting, and shock in a steroid-dependent patient.

    Septic shock

    An abdominal source with fever, confusion, and hemodynamic instability.
