    Skill

    Chest compressions

    Compressions do not restart the heart. They keep the brain and the myocardium alive until something that can restart it arrives.

    EMR

    Why it works

    Compressing the chest raises intrathoracic pressure and directly compresses the heart, forcing blood forward. Even excellent compressions produce only about a quarter to a third of normal cardiac output, which is enough to preserve tissue but not enough to be casual about. Coronary perfusion pressure builds progressively over successive compressions and collapses instantly when you stop.

    How to do it, and why each step exists

    Every step below has a reason. If you know the reason, you can adapt when the situation does not match the textbook.

    Confirm arrest quickly and start

    Agonal gasping is mistaken for breathing in a large proportion of arrests and delays compressions. Not responsive and not breathing normally is enough. Do not spend time on a prolonged pulse check.

    Centre of the chest, lower half of the sternum

    This position compresses the ventricles most effectively. Too high compresses the atria and achieves little; too low risks the liver.

    5 to 6 cm deep in an adult

    Shallow compressions do not generate meaningful output. Depth is the most commonly under-delivered element and it directly correlates with survival.

    100 to 120 per minute

    Faster than 120 reduces depth and prevents the heart refilling between compressions. Slower than 100 does not maintain perfusion pressure.

    Allow full recoil between compressions

    Recoil is when the heart refills. Leaning on the chest between compressions prevents filling and there is nothing to eject on the next one. It is a very common and invisible error.

    Minimise every pause

    Coronary perfusion pressure takes several compressions to rebuild and falls to zero the moment you stop. Pauses for analysis, charging, and pulse checks should be measured in seconds.

    Swap compressors roughly every two minutes

    Quality falls measurably from fatigue well before the person doing it notices. Swap during a rhythm analysis so no additional pause is created.

    Common errors
    • Delaying compressions to assess agonal gasping
    • Insufficient depth, which is the commonest quality failure
    • Leaning on the chest and preventing recoil
    • Long pauses around defibrillation
    • Continuing past the point of fatigue rather than swapping
    • Compression-only CPR in drowning, children, or any hypoxic arrest, where ventilation is essential
