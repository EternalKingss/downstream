    Skill

    Splinting and traction

    Splinting is hemorrhage control and analgesia at the same time, not just tidiness.

    EMR

    Why it works

    Fracture ends moving against each other tear muscle and vessels continuously and prevent a clot forming. Immobilising them stops that ongoing damage, allows clot formation, and dramatically reduces pain, which itself reduces the catecholamine response driving up heart rate and oxygen demand.

    How to do it, and why each step exists

    Every step below has a reason. If you know the reason, you can adapt when the situation does not match the textbook.

    Check circulation, sensation, and movement first

    Before and after every intervention, documented with times. If a pulse disappears after you splint, you need to know it was there before.

    Immobilise the joint above and below

    Movement at either adjacent joint transmits movement to the fracture. Splinting only the fracture site does not immobilise it.

    Pad generously and splint in the position found, unless pulseless

    Padding prevents pressure injury over a long transport. Moving a fracture hurts and can cause further damage, so accept the position unless perfusion demands otherwise.

    Apply gentle in-line traction to a pulseless, grossly deformed limb

    Restoring alignment often restores perfusion. Tissue distal to an occluded artery dies within hours, so restoring flow outweighs the theoretical risk of realignment. Reassess pulses immediately after.

    Traction splint for an isolated mid-shaft femur

    Thigh muscles are powerful and spasm pulls the fragments past each other, causing severe pain and increasing bleeding into the thigh. Traction realigns them and reduces the compartment volume. Not for hip, knee, or pelvic injuries.

    Pelvic binder at the greater trochanters

    Reduces pelvic volume so accumulating blood can tamponade. Trochanter level, not the iliac crests, which is the commonest placement error. Do not spring the pelvis to test it first.

    Cover open fractures with sterile moist dressing

    Bone exposed to the environment risks osteomyelitis, which is difficult to treat and can end in amputation. Note if bone retracts inside during alignment, so the surgical team knows contamination went inward.

    Common errors
    • Not checking or documenting distal neurovascular status
    • Splinting only the fracture without the adjacent joints
    • Pelvic binder placed too high on the iliac crests
    • Springing or rocking the pelvis to test stability
    • Traction splint on a suspected pelvic or hip injury
    • Elevating a limb with suspected compartment syndrome
    • Splinting so tightly that it becomes a constricting band as swelling develops
