    Skill

    Spinal motion restriction

    The goal is preventing further movement of an unstable column, not strapping every patient to a board.

    EMR

    Why it works

    Much of the final damage in spinal cord injury is secondary, caused by movement of an unstable column, plus swelling, bleeding, and poor perfusion in the hours afterward. You cannot undo the primary injury; you can prevent the additional one, and you can protect the cord's perfusion.

    How to do it, and why each step exists

    Every step below has a reason. If you know the reason, you can adapt when the situation does not match the textbook.

    Manual in-line stabilisation from first contact

    Hands on the head immediately, before any assessment or movement, and maintained until the patient is secured. This is the actual intervention; the equipment is just a way of maintaining it.

    Document a baseline neurological exam

    Movement and sensation in all four limbs and the level at which sensation changes, recorded before and after every move. Whether an injury is complete or incomplete changes prognosis enormously and you may be the only one who documented the early picture.

    Apply a correctly sized collar, not tight

    A collar limits gross movement but does not immobilise. Critically, a tight collar obstructs jugular venous drainage and measurably raises intracranial pressure, which actively harms a head-injured patient.

    Use a scoop or vacuum mattress rather than a long board

    Rigid long boards cause pressure injuries within a short time, are painful, and can impair ventilation. Modern practice uses them for extrication and transfers the patient onto a padded surface.

    Minimise log rolls, especially with a suspected pelvic fracture

    Log rolling a fractured pelvis can disrupt clots and restart heavy bleeding. A scoop stretcher achieves the same result with far less movement.

    Prioritise airway, breathing, and blood pressure over immobilisation

    The injured cord needs oxygen and perfusion to survive the swelling. Hypoxia and hypotension convert salvageable tissue into permanent deficit, so a patent airway and an adequate pressure matter more than a perfect collar.

    Common errors
    • Immobilising reflexively without considering whether the mechanism warrants it
    • A collar applied too tightly on a head-injured patient
    • Prolonged time on a rigid board
    • Failing to document neurology before and after movement
    • Log rolling a patient with a suspected pelvic fracture
    • Struggling with immobilisation while the airway is compromised
