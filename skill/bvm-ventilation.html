    Skill

    Bag-valve-mask ventilation

    Harder than it looks, and the single most important skill for the most salvageable emergencies you will attend.

    EMR

    Why it works

    You are replacing the negative-pressure system with a positive-pressure one. Normally the chest expands and atmospheric pressure pushes air in. With a bag you push air in directly, which works but has consequences: it raises intrathoracic pressure, which impedes venous return, and it will inflate the stomach if the pressure exceeds what the oesophageal sphincter holds.

    How to do it, and why each step exists

    Every step below has a reason. If you know the reason, you can adapt when the situation does not match the textbook.

    Open and position the airway first

    A bag squeezed against an obstructed airway inflates the stomach, not the lungs. Positioning is not a preliminary, it is the intervention.

    Get a genuine mask seal, ideally two-person

    Leak is the commonest reason ventilation fails. Two-person technique, one holding the mask with both hands using a thenar-eminence grip while the other squeezes, is substantially more effective and should be the default whenever staffing allows.

    Attach a reservoir and run 15 L/min oxygen

    Without a reservoir you deliver around 50 percent. With one, close to 100. It costs nothing to attach.

    Squeeze slowly over about a second, watching the chest

    Fast forceful squeezes generate high pressure that opens the oesophagus and fills the stomach. A full stomach splints the diaphragm, makes ventilation harder, and causes vomiting and aspiration.

    Deliver only enough volume for visible chest rise

    Visible rise, not maximal rise. Over-inflation raises intrathoracic pressure and reduces venous return, which drops cardiac output in a patient who cannot afford it.

    Ventilate at the correct rate and count it out loud

    Roughly 10 to 12 per minute in adults, faster in children. Under stress people ventilate far too fast, which both hyperinflates and hyperventilates. Counting aloud is the fix.

    Allow full exhalation, especially in obstructive disease

    In asthma and COPD, air is already trapped. Stacking breaths on trapped air raises pressure until the blood pressure drops or a lung ruptures. Long expiratory times, low rate.

    Common errors
    • Squeezing too fast and too hard, which inflates the stomach and drops the blood pressure
    • One-person technique when two people are available
    • Forgetting the reservoir bag
    • Hyperventilating a head-injured patient, which constricts cerebral vessels and worsens ischemia
    • Bagging an asthmatic at a normal rate, causing breath stacking
    • Failing to reposition the airway when ventilation becomes difficult
