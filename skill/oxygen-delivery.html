    Oxygen delivery and titration

    A drug with a dose, a target, and situations where more is worse.

    EMR

    Why it works

    Raising the inspired oxygen fraction raises alveolar oxygen pressure, which increases the gradient driving diffusion into blood. That is the whole mechanism. It compensates for a delivery problem; it does not fix an obstruction, a shunt, a perfusion failure, or a hemoglobin that is already occupied.

    How to do it, and why each step exists

    Every step below has a reason. If you know the reason, you can adapt when the situation does not match the textbook.

    Measure a saturation before deciding

    Oxygen is titrated to a target, not given reflexively. Routine oxygen in normoxic patients with chest pain or stroke has not been shown to help and may cause harm.

    Target 94 to 98 percent for most patients

    Enough to correct hypoxia without the harms associated with sustained hyperoxia.

    Target 88 to 92 percent in a known COPD retainer

    A small group rely more on hypoxic drive and on ventilation-perfusion matching that high oxygen disrupts. But never withhold oxygen from a hypoxic COPD patient: hypoxia kills in minutes and retention takes far longer.

    Choose the device by the concentration you need

    Nasal cannula at 1 to 6 L/min gives roughly 24 to 44 percent. Simple mask at 6 to 10 gives 35 to 60. Non-rebreather at 10 to 15 gives 60 to 90. Never run a simple mask below 6 L/min or the patient rebreathes their own carbon dioxide.

    Inflate the non-rebreather reservoir before applying it

    An empty bag delivers far less than intended, and the bag should not collapse fully on inspiration. If it does, the flow is too low.

    Ignore the oximeter entirely in carbon monoxide exposure

    A standard oximeter cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin and reads falsely high. Give high-flow oxygen regardless of the number, because here oxygen is the actual treatment.

    Reassess and adjust

    Failure to improve on high-flow oxygen is diagnostic information: it points to a shunt, a mechanical problem such as tension pneumothorax, or a perfusion problem such as pulmonary embolism, and it means you need a different intervention rather than more oxygen.

    Common errors
    • Giving high-flow oxygen to every patient regardless of saturation
    • Withholding oxygen from a hypoxic COPD patient out of fear of retention
    • Running a simple mask below 6 L/min
    • Trusting the oximeter in smoke inhalation or suspected carbon monoxide
    • Increasing the flow when the patient is not responding, rather than reconsidering the diagnosis
    • Forgetting that a hypoxic patient who needs ventilation needs a bag, not a bigger mask
