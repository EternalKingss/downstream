    Skill

    The primary survey

    A deliberately ordered search for the things that kill fastest, in the order they kill.

    EMR

    Why it works

    The sequence is not arbitrary. An obstructed airway kills in minutes, inadequate breathing in a few more, catastrophic bleeding in minutes to an hour, and a brain injury over hours. Working in that order means you address problems in the order they will end the patient's life, and it stops you being drawn to whatever is most visually dramatic.

    How to do it, and why each step exists

    Every step below has a reason. If you know the reason, you can adapt when the situation does not match the textbook.

    Scene safety and general impression from the doorway

    A dead responder helps nobody. And a first impression across the room, particularly in children, is one of the most sensitive assessments available before you touch anything.

    Catastrophic bleeding first, if present

    Modern trauma practice moves massive external hemorrhage ahead of airway, because a patient can exsanguinate faster than they can suffocate from an airway you have thirty seconds to fix. If blood is pumping out, stop it first.

    Airway with spinal consideration

    Look, listen, feel. Snoring, gurgling, or silence all mean act now. Spinal precaution is applied alongside, never instead.

    Breathing: rate, effort, symmetry, saturation

    You are looking for the small number of things that kill immediately: tension pneumothorax, open chest wound, flail segment, and inadequate ventilation. Expose and look at the chest rather than only listening.

    Circulation: pulse, skin, capillary refill, obvious bleeding

    Skin signs and capillary refill detect compensated shock long before the blood pressure moves. A normal blood pressure does not exclude significant shock.

    Disability: level of consciousness, pupils, glucose

    AVPU or GCS, pupils, and a glucose reading. Glucose belongs here because hypoglycemia imitates everything neurological and reverses in minutes.

    Exposure and environment, then cover them back up

    You cannot find what you do not look at, but hypothermia impairs clotting and worsens every trauma outcome. Expose, examine, then actively warm.

    Reassess from the top after every intervention and every move

    The primary survey is a loop, not a checklist you complete once. Airways close, chests deteriorate, and patients bleed while you are working on something else.

    Common errors
    • Being drawn to a dramatic-looking injury before completing the sequence
    • Skipping glucose in an altered patient
    • Relying on blood pressure to detect shock
    • Completing the survey once and never repeating it
    • Leaving the patient exposed after examining them
    • Continuing down the sequence when a problem you found is not yet fixed
