    Anatomy

    Uterus and placenta

    A muscular organ that grows from the size of a pear to filling the abdomen, with the placenta embedded in its wall acting as the fetus's lungs, gut, and kidneys.

    What it does when it works

    • By term the uterus receives roughly 700 to 900 mL of blood every minute, close to a fifth of cardiac output.
    • The placenta exchanges gases and nutrients between two circulations that never actually mix.
    • After delivery, the uterus clamps down hard, and that muscular contraction is what physically closes the vessels at the placental site.
    • The uterus and placenta are essential to fetal survival but not to maternal survival.

    Why this structure matters

    Two facts govern every obstetric emergency. First, blood flow is enormous, so a bleeding uterus empties a patient faster than almost any other injury. Second, the mechanism that stops postpartum bleeding is muscular contraction rather than clotting, which is why a soft boggy uterus after delivery is a hemorrhage in progress and why massaging it works.

    Why it matters in the field

    • The commonest cause of postpartum hemorrhage is a uterus that has not contracted. Firm fundal massage is an EMR-level intervention that genuinely stops bleeding.
    • After about twenty weeks, lying flat compresses the inferior vena cava against the spine and drops cardiac output substantially. Tilt the patient to the left or manually displace the uterus.
    • Placental abruption bleeds behind the placenta and may be largely concealed, so a rigid painful uterus with shock and little visible blood is an emergency.
    • Placenta praevia bleeds painlessly and bright red. Never perform a vaginal examination in third-trimester bleeding.

    What can break it

    Failure mode: Bleeding faster than anywhere else in the body
