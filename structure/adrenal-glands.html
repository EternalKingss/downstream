    Anatomy

    Adrenal glands

    Two small glands capping the kidneys, each with an outer cortex and inner medulla that function as separate organs. The cortex makes steroid hormones; the medulla is effectively a modified sympathetic ganglion that dumps adrenaline directly into the blood.

    What it does when it works

    • The cortex produces cortisol, which maintains blood pressure, mobilises glucose, and modulates inflammation, and aldosterone, which retains sodium and water and excretes potassium.
    • The medulla releases adrenaline and noradrenaline into the circulation during sympathetic activation, producing the body-wide fight-or-flight response.
    • Cortisol output rises several-fold under physical stress such as infection, trauma, or surgery.

    Why this structure matters

    Cortisol is permissive: without it, blood vessels stop responding properly to catecholamines. A patient in adrenal crisis is hypotensive and does not respond to the usual compensations, because the hormone that lets those compensations work is missing.

    Why it matters in the field

    • Anyone on long-term steroids has a suppressed adrenal response and cannot mount a stress response. Illness, injury, or abruptly stopping the steroid can precipitate a crisis.
    • Adrenal crisis presents as shock with hypotension, vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, and often low sodium and high potassium.
    • Ask about steroid use in any unexplained shock. Many patients carry a steroid card or emergency injection.
