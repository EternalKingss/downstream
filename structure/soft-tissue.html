    Anatomy

    Soft tissue and wounds

    Skin, fat, fascia, and muscle considered together as the tissue that gets injured in most trauma. Different mechanisms damage it in characteristically different ways.

    What it does when it works

    • Bleeding is stopped by vessel constriction, then a platelet plug, then a fibrin clot.
    • Inflammation clears debris and organisms, then new tissue is laid down and remodelled over weeks.
    • Healing requires blood supply, so devitalised or heavily contaminated tissue does not heal.

    Why this structure matters

    The mechanism predicts the problem. Clean incisions bleed but heal well. Crush injuries devitalise tissue and release its contents into the circulation. Degloving separates skin from its blood supply so it dies even though it looks intact. Puncture wounds seal over and trap contamination underneath.

    Why it matters in the field

    • An impaled object should be stabilised in place, not removed. It may be the only thing preventing catastrophic bleeding.
    • Amputated parts go in a dry sealed bag, then on ice. Never directly on ice and never in water, both of which destroy the tissue.
    • Wounds over joints, on the hands, or on the face have functional consequences out of proportion to their size.
    • Anything that bit or punctured deeply carries an infection and tetanus question that changes the destination.

    What can break it

    Failure mode: Tissue is torn, crushed, or devitalised
