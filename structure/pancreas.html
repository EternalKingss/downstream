    Anatomy

    Pancreas

    A gland lying behind the stomach with two entirely separate jobs. The bulk of it makes digestive enzymes that drain into the duodenum. Scattered through it are the islets of Langerhans, clusters of hormone-producing cells that release insulin and glucagon straight into the bloodstream.

    What it does when it works

    • Beta cells release insulin when blood glucose rises. Insulin unlocks glucose entry into muscle and fat, and tells the liver to store rather than release.
    • Alpha cells release glucagon when glucose falls, telling the liver to break down glycogen and manufacture new glucose.
    • The two hormones oppose each other continuously, holding blood glucose in a remarkably narrow band around 4 to 7 mmol/L.
    • The digestive portion produces enzymes powerful enough to digest the pancreas itself, which is why they are secreted in an inactive form.

    Why this structure matters

    Insulin is the only hormone that lowers blood glucose, while several raise it. That asymmetry means the body is far better defended against low sugar than high, and it is why type 1 diabetes was uniformly fatal before insulin existed. Understanding that insulin also switches off fat breakdown explains ketoacidosis entirely.

    Why it matters in the field

    • Without insulin, cells cannot take up glucose even when the blood is full of it. The patient is starving in the middle of plenty, which is why they break down fat and produce ketones.
    • Those digestive enzymes activating inside the gland is acute pancreatitis: the organ digests itself, causing severe pain and massive fluid shifts.
    • Islet cells are a tiny fraction of the gland's mass but their failure is what kills.
