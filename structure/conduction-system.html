    Anatomy

    Conduction system

    Specialised cardiac cells that generate and conduct the electrical impulse: the sinoatrial node in the right atrium, the atrioventricular node at the junction, the bundle of His, the left and right bundle branches, and the Purkinje fibres spreading through the ventricular walls.

    What it does when it works

    • The SA node fires at 60 to 100 per minute and sets the rate because it is the fastest pacemaker present.
    • The AV node deliberately delays the impulse by about a tenth of a second, which lets the atria finish emptying into the ventricles before the ventricles contract. That delay is where the atrial kick comes from.
    • Backup pacemakers exist further down. The AV junction fires at 40 to 60, the ventricles at 20 to 40. Slower, and progressively less effective.
    • Electrical activity precedes mechanical activity. A rhythm on the monitor is a statement about electricity, not about output.

    Why this structure matters

    Rate and rhythm both matter, and for different reasons. Too slow and cardiac output falls because there are not enough beats. Too fast and output also falls, because diastole shortens so much the ventricles never fill properly. And if the atria stop contributing their kick, as in atrial fibrillation, output drops by roughly a fifth in a patient who may not have a fifth to spare.

    Why it matters in the field

    • Pulseless electrical activity is this system working perfectly while the pump produces nothing. The electricity is not the problem, so shocking it will not help. Look for the mechanical cause.
    • Ventricular fibrillation is this system in complete disorder, with no coordinated contraction at all. It is electrical, and defibrillation is the answer.
    • Potassium, calcium, magnesium, hypoxia, acidosis, and hypothermia all disturb conduction. A rhythm problem is often a chemistry problem wearing a costume.
