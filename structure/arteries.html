    Anatomy

    Arteries and arterioles

    Thick-walled, muscular, elastic vessels carrying blood away from the heart. Large arteries act as elastic reservoirs that smooth pulsatile flow; arterioles are the small muscular vessels that actually control where blood goes.

    What it does when it works

    • Arterioles are the resistance vessels. Their smooth muscle tone determines systemic vascular resistance, and therefore blood pressure, and therefore which tissues get flow.
    • Blood pressure is cardiac output multiplied by systemic vascular resistance. Only two variables, which is why every form of shock is a failure of one, the other, or both.
    • Sympathetic stimulation constricts arterioles in skin, gut, and muscle to preserve flow to brain and heart. That redistribution is what makes a shocked patient pale, cool, and clammy.
    • Elastic recoil in the aorta during diastole maintains diastolic pressure, which is what actually perfuses the coronary arteries.

    Why this structure matters

    Blood pressure is not the same as perfusion, and this is the single most misunderstood point in shock. A patient can maintain a completely normal blood pressure by clamping down their arterioles while their tissues are already starving. Compensated shock is defined by a normal pressure hiding an abnormal state, and the blood pressure only falls once compensation has been exhausted.

    Why it matters in the field

    • Narrowing pulse pressure is an early sign of hypovolemia: the diastolic rises from vasoconstriction while systolic starts to fall from lost volume.
    • In a healthy adult, blood pressure often does not fall until roughly thirty percent of blood volume is gone. By the time it drops, the patient is in serious trouble.
    • Children compensate even harder and hold their pressure even longer, then crash without much warning. Never be reassured by a normal blood pressure in an injured child.
