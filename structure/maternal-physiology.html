    Maternal physiology

    Pregnancy alters almost every system. Understanding those changes is what stops you being falsely reassured by a pregnant patient's vital signs.

    What it does when it works

    • Blood volume rises by 30 to 50 percent, with plasma increasing more than red cells, so the hemoglobin concentration falls even though total oxygen carrying capacity rises.
    • Cardiac output rises by around 40 percent, mostly through increased stroke volume, and resting heart rate rises by 10 to 20 beats.
    • Blood pressure falls slightly in the second trimester because systemic vascular resistance drops.
    • Tidal volume rises substantially, the diaphragm is pushed up, functional residual capacity falls, and oxygen consumption increases by around 20 percent.
    • Blood becomes more prone to clotting, which protects against delivery hemorrhage and raises thromboembolism risk.

    Why this structure matters

    The extra blood volume means a pregnant patient can lose 1,200 to 1,500 mL before showing the usual signs of shock, and the fetus is compromised long before the mother's numbers move. Meanwhile reduced functional residual capacity plus higher oxygen consumption means she desaturates far faster than a non-pregnant patient when breathing is interrupted.

    Why it matters in the field

    • Normal-looking vital signs in a bleeding pregnant patient are not reassuring. The uterus is sacrificed first to protect the mother, so fetal distress precedes maternal signs.
    • Treat the mother and you treat the fetus. Oxygen, position, and volume for her are the best fetal resuscitation available.
    • Left lateral tilt after twenty weeks, always.
    • The airway is more oedematous and more prone to bleeding, and desaturation is fast. Preoxygenate well before anything that interrupts breathing.
