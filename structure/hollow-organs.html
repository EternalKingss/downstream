    Anatomy

    Hollow abdominal organs

    The stomach, small intestine, and large intestine, plus the gallbladder and bladder. All are tubes or sacs with a lumen, a muscular wall, and contents that belong inside them.

    What it does when it works

    • Peristalsis moves contents along in one direction using coordinated waves of smooth muscle contraction.
    • The stomach secretes acid strong enough to require an actively maintained mucus barrier for protection.
    • The small intestine absorbs nutrients; the large intestine reclaims water and houses the bacterial population.
    • Sensation from these organs travels on autonomic fibres, so pain is poorly localised and typically felt in the midline.

    Why this structure matters

    Hollow organs fail in two ways: they perforate, spilling contents that are chemically and bacterially hostile into the peritoneum, or they obstruct, causing distension, vomiting, and massive fluid sequestration into the bowel. Blunt trauma tends to burst them at fixed points; penetrating trauma opens them anywhere.

    Why it matters in the field

    • Visceral pain from a hollow organ is dull, crampy, and central. When inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum the pain becomes sharp and localises, which is why appendicitis migrates from the umbilicus to the right lower quadrant.
    • Obstruction sequesters litres of fluid into the bowel lumen. These patients are genuinely hypovolemic even though nothing has visibly left the body.
    • A perforated organ causes rigid, board-like guarding and a patient who lies completely still, because any movement hurts.

    What can break it

    Failure mode: Contents spill, or the tube blocks
