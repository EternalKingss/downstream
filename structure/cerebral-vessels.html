    Anatomy

    Cerebral blood vessels

    Four arteries supply the brain: two carotids in front and two vertebral arteries behind. They join at the circle of Willis at the base of the brain, which then distributes flow through the anterior, middle, and posterior cerebral arteries.

    What it does when it works

    • The brain receives about fifteen percent of cardiac output, roughly 750 millilitres a minute, continuously and without interruption.
    • Autoregulation keeps cerebral flow constant across a wide range of blood pressures, roughly a mean arterial pressure of 60 to 150.
    • Cerebral perfusion pressure is mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. Both terms matter.
    • The circle of Willis provides some collateral supply, though it is complete and functional in fewer people than textbooks imply.

    Why this structure matters

    Neurons begin dying within minutes of losing flow, and roughly two million die every minute during a large vessel stroke. There is usually a surrounding zone of tissue that is starved but not yet dead, kept marginally alive by collateral flow. That zone is what treatment saves, and it shrinks with every minute.

    Why it matters in the field

    • Middle cerebral artery strokes are the most common and produce the classic picture of face, arm, and speech involvement.
    • Posterior circulation strokes present with dizziness, double vision, ataxia, and vomiting rather than one-sided weakness, and they are missed far more often.
    • Because autoregulation fails below a mean arterial pressure of about 60, hypotension is devastating to an already injured brain. A single hypotensive episode measurably worsens outcomes in traumatic brain injury.
