    Anatomy

    Pleural space

    Not really a space. It is a sealed potential space between two membranes: the visceral pleura stuck to the lung surface and the parietal pleura lining the inside of the chest wall. Between them sits a few millilitres of lubricating fluid and nothing else.

    What it does when it works

    • The pressure inside is negative, roughly minus five centimetres of water at rest, dropping further on inspiration.
    • That negative pressure is the only thing holding the lung against the chest wall. The lung has no attachment of its own and is elastic, so left to itself it would recoil into a small lump near the hilum.
    • The thin fluid layer lets the two surfaces slide over each other with almost no friction through every breath.

    Why this structure matters

    The entire respiratory system runs on a vacuum seal. Break the seal and the mechanics fail immediately, no matter how healthy the lung tissue is. This is why every condition in this group looks similar: the lung is fine, it simply cannot inflate. What differs between them is only what got into the space, and how fast.

    Why it matters in the field

    • Air, blood, and fluid all do the same mechanical thing here. What separates pneumothorax, hemothorax, and effusion is the rate of onset and whether the patient is also losing circulating volume.
    • A one-way valve leak turns a survivable pneumothorax into a tension pneumothorax, because now pressure keeps climbing with every breath and starts squeezing the heart and great vessels too.
